## Name

Ἀρήτη is formed from ἀρητός, the Ionic form of ἀρατός, a verbal adjective of ἀράομαι (“pray”). The adjective means both “prayed against, accursed” (ἀρητὸς γόος, Iliad 17.37) and “prayed for”, and LSJ glosses the proper names Ἄρητος and Ἀρήτη as “the Prayed-for” (LSJ s.v. ἀρατός). Athena calls the name ἐπώνυμον, “given for a reason” (7.54), and a scholion explains that she was so called “because she was born in answer to prayer” (schol. P.V. on 7.54, Dindorf p. 325; Merry and Riddell on 7.54, p. 286). The name has a long vowel in its second syllable, and is a different word from the noun ἀρετή, “excellence” (see [[arete]]). Merry and Riddell list Arete among the few Phaeacian names that do not come from the sea (note on 8.111, p. 319; see [[phaeacians]]).

## Family

Athena tells her story in Book 7. Arete is the only child of Rhexenor, the elder son of [[nausithous|Nausithous]], whom Apollo shot down in his own hall while he was still a bridegroom, with no son. Alcinous, his younger brother, married her and honored her “as no other woman on earth is honored” (7.63–68). Her children and her husband honor her, and the people look on her as on a goddess and greet her when she walks through the town; she has a good mind of her own, and settles quarrels for those she favors, even among men (7.69–74).

So Alcinous is her uncle as well as her husband. Athena first says that she comes “from the same parents who bore King Alcinous” (7.54–55), and the scholia saw the difficulty: some took τοκῆες here to mean “ancestors”, so that she is his niece and not his sister, and they report that Hesiod made her the sister of Alcinous (schol. B.P.Q.T. on 7.54, Dindorf p. 325). Her children are [[nausicaa|Nausicaa]] and five sons (6.62–63; see [[sons-of-alcinous]]).

## Descriptions

- λευκώλενος, “white-armed”, in the verse “and white-armed Arete began to speak among them” (7.233, 11.335; see [[white-armed]]).
- περίφρων, “very wise”: the old Echeneus calls her βασίλεια περίφρων, “the wise queen” (11.345).
- βασίλεια, “queen”: so Odysseus addresses her (7.241, 13.59).
- At the hearth: Book 6 shows her twice “at the hearth … turning the sea-purple wool on her distaff” (6.52–53, 6.305–306; see [[sea-purple]], [[distaff]] and [[hearth]]).

## Elsewhere in the Odyssey

**The suppliant at her knees.** Odysseus follows the advice of Nausicaa and Athena. Still hidden in Athena's mist, he walks through the hall, throws his arms around Arete's knees, and the mist falls away. He addresses her by her father's name, “Arete, daughter of godlike Rhexenor”, and asks for an escort home; then he sits down in the ashes of the hearth (7.139–154; see [[supplication]]). The scholia note that Athena's account of her family is what lets him address her in this way (schol. P.Q. on 7.54, Dindorf p. 325).

**Her question.** She is the first to see what the others miss. She recognizes the cloak and tunic, which she made herself with her women, and asks: “Who are you, and from where? Who gave you these clothes?” (7.233–239). Odysseus answers the second question and not the first (7.240–297). J. Arft makes this question central to his reading of the Phaeacian episode (*Arete and the Odyssey's Poetics of Interrogation*, Oxford 2022, as summarized in BMCR 2024.03.15).

**Her guest.** After the tale of the dead she speaks to the Phaeacians: “He is my guest” (ξεῖνος δʼ αὖτʼ ἐμός ἐστιν), and they should not send him off in haste or stint his gifts (11.335–341). Echeneus agrees that “the wise queen” speaks to the point, but says the deed and the word depend on Alcinous (11.342–346). She packs his gifts in a chest and tells him to tie the knot himself (8.438–445). At his departure Odysseus puts a cup into her hand and wishes her joy “until old age and death come” (13.56–62), and she sends three slave women to the ship with a cloak and tunic, a chest, and food and wine (13.66–69).

## Outside Homer

**Apollonius.** In the *Argonautica* Medea supplicates Arete when the Colchians arrive to claim her (4.1011–1029). At night Arete persuades Alcinous to protect her. He tells her that he will give Medea back to her father if she is a virgin, but will not part her from a husband; Arete sends word to Jason, and the marriage is made that night (4.1068–1109). When the Argonauts leave, Arete gives Medea twelve Phaeacian handmaids (4.1221–1222; see [[apollonius-drepane]] and [[apollonius]]).
