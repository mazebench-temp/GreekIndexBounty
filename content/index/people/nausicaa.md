## Etymology

Ναυσικάα begins with ναυσι-, the combining form of ναῦς (“ship”). Her grandfather is Ναυσίθοος, “swift with ships” (6.7; see [[nausithous]]), and the father of her friend is ναυσικλειτός, “famous for ships” (6.22; see [[dymas-the-phaeacian]]). Merry and Riddell note that “almost all Phaeacian names” come from seafaring, and they list only Alcinous, Arete, Laodamas, Polybus and Rhexenor as exceptions (note on 8.111, p. 319; see [[phaeacians]]). The second part of her name has no agreed explanation in the sources consulted here.

## Family

{{quote:Od. 6.195-197 | Nausicaa names her people and her father}}

She is the daughter of [[alcinous|Alcinous]], who rules the Phaeacians (6.12, 6.17), and of his wife, whom Book 6 calls only “my mother” and Book 7 names [[queen-arete|Arete]] (7.54). She has five brothers, two married and three still unmarried (6.62–63; see [[sons-of-alcinous]]); three of them are named in Book 8, Laodamas, Halius and Clytoneus (8.118–119). Through her grandfather [[nausithous|Nausithous]], son of Poseidon and Periboea, she descends from [[poseidon|Poseidon]] (7.56–63). Her mother is also her father's niece: Arete is the only child of Alcinous' brother Rhexenor (7.63–66). Her old nurse is Eurymedusa, a slave brought by ship from Apeire, who lights the fire in her chamber (7.7–13).

## Descriptions

- λευκώλενος, “white-armed” = λευκός “white” + ὠλένη “forearm” (6.101, 6.186, 6.251; 7.12). It is the epithet of Hera, Helen and Andromache in the Iliad (see [[white-armed]]).
- ἐύπεπλος, “well-robed” = εὖ “well” + πέπλος “robe” (6.49; see [[well-robed]]).
- ἐυῶπις, “fair-faced” = εὖ “well” + ὤψ “face, eye” (6.113, 6.142; see [[fair-faced]]).
- παρθένος ἀδμής, “unwed maiden”, literally “untamed girl” (6.109, 6.228). ἀδμής is “unbroken”, the word for an animal not yet yoked (see [[unbroken]] and [[parthenos]]).
- βασίλεια, “princess” (6.115), the feminine of βασιλεύς (see [[basileus]]). Odysseus calls her ἄνασσα, “queen, lady” (6.149, 6.175), a form of address that Homer otherwise gives only to the goddesses Demeter (Il. 14.326) and Athena (Od. 3.380), as Merry and Riddell note (on 6.149, p. 265; see [[anax]]).
- θυγάτηρ μεγαλήτορος Ἀλκινόοιο, “the daughter of great-hearted Alcinous”, a whole-verse formula with her name (6.17, 6.213, 8.464; see [[great-hearted]]).
- She is “like the immortal goddesses in build and in looks” (6.16), and in Book 8 she has “beauty from the gods” (θεῶν ἄπο κάλλος ἔχουσα, 8.457; see [[like-the-goddesses]]).

## Elsewhere in the Odyssey

**At home.** She drives back to the palace at sunset. Her brothers, “like the immortals”, unyoke the mules and carry in the clothes, and Eurymedusa makes up her fire and her supper (7.1–13). Odysseus follows later, hidden in Athena's mist.

**In Odysseus' account.** Before the court he tells Arete that he found the handmaids of her daughter playing on the beach, and she was among them “like the goddesses”. She “did not miss good judgement, as you would not expect a younger person to” (7.290–294). When Alcinous faults her for not bringing the stranger home with her handmaids, Odysseus protects her: she told him to follow with the handmaids, he says, but he refused “from fear and shame”, in case the king should be angry at the sight (7.298–307). That is not what Book 6 told. She sent him to wait in the grove of Athena so that the townsmen would not talk (6.273–299). Alcinous answers with a wish that the stranger, being such a man, would stay, marry his daughter and be called his son-in-law (7.311–316). The wish is hers in another form (6.244–245).

**The farewell.** On the evening of his departure she stands by the doorpost of the hall, “having beauty from the gods”, and looks at him with wonder (8.457–460). She speaks two lines: “Farewell, stranger, so that even in your own native land you may remember me, since to me first you owe the price of your life” (8.461–462). ζωάγρια is the reward for a life saved, and πρώτῃ, “to me first”, recalls his words at the river, “it is to you … that I have come first” (6.175–176). Odysseus answers that if Zeus brings him home he will pray to her there “as to a god”, “for you gave me my life, girl” (σὺ γάρ μʼ ἐβιώσαο, κούρη, 8.464–468). She does not appear again. When Odysseus tells Penelope his wanderings, the summary says that he came to the Phaeacians, who honored him like a god and sent him home with gifts, and it does not mention her (23.338–341).

## Outside Homer

**Ball play and the stage.** Athenaeus says that Homer shows Nausicaa as the only heroine who plays ball, and that the grammarian Agallis of Corcyra gave her the invention of the ball game, as a favor to her fellow citizen (*Deipnosophists* 1.14d; see [[athenaeus-ball-games]]). He also says that Sophocles “played ball superbly” when he produced his *Nausicaa* (1.20e–f). Eustathius tells the same anecdote with another title: Sophocles, “they say”, played the part of Nausicaa playing ball when he produced the *Plyntriai* (“Washerwomen”), and won great acclaim (on Od. 6.115, p. 241 Stallbaum). Whether the two titles name one play is not settled by the sources consulted here (see [[sophocles-nausicaa]] and [[sophocles]]).

**Ancient judgement of her wish.** Aristarchus athetized her wish for a husband “such as this man” (6.244–245) and hesitated over the first line, because Alcman had used it (schol. on 6.244–245, Dindorf p. 314). Another scholion calls the words unfitting for a maiden and reports that Ephorus praised them as coming from a soul inclined to virtue. Plutarch's judgement is conditional: if she speaks from desire for a stranger, her boldness deserves blame; if she has seen his character in his words and prefers such a husband to a sailor or a dancer of her own people, she deserves admiration (*How the Young Man Should Study Poetry* 27a–b). See [[nausicaas-wish-244-245]] and [[plutarch-on-nausicaas-wish]].

**In art.** Pausanias saw a painting by Polygnotus in the building to the left of the Athenian Propylaea: Odysseus coming upon the girls who wash clothes with Nausicaa at the river, “just as Homer told it” (1.22.6). On the chest of Cypselus at Olympia he saw two girls on a mule cart, one holding the reins and one veiled, whom people took to be Nausicaa and her servant driving to the washing pools (5.19.9). See [[nausicaa-in-greek-art]].

**Latin poetry.** Virgil gives Odysseus' first words to Aeneas meeting Venus, disguised as a huntress: “what shall I call you, maiden? for your face is not mortal ... surely a goddess, the sister of Phoebus?” (*Aeneid* 1.327–329). He gives the Artemis simile of 6.102–109 to Dido among her young men, “as Diana leads her dances on the banks of the Eurotas” (1.498–504; see [[artemis-among-her-nymphs]] and [[virgil]]). Merry and Riddell (on 6.149, p. 265) compare Ovid's Salmacis, who greets Hermaphroditus with the same sequence: god or mortal, then blessed parents, brother and sister, and most blessed of all the bride he takes (*Metamorphoses* 4.320–326; compare 6.149–159; see [[ovid]]).

**Modern readers.** Samuel Butler argued that the Odyssey was written by a young woman of Trapani in Sicily who drew herself as Nausicaa (*The Authoress of the Odyssey*, 1897; see [[butler-authoress-of-the-odyssey]]). James Joyce named the thirteenth episode of *Ulysses* (1922) after her: Gerty MacDowell on Sandymount strand (see [[joyce-nausicaa]]).
