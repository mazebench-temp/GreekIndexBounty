## Etymology

Ἀλκίνοος reads as a compound of ἀλκή (“strength, defense”) and νόος (“mind”): “strong of mind”, or “mind for defense”. Merry and Riddell count Alcinous among the few Phaeacian names that do not come from seafaring (note on 8.111, p. 319; see [[phaeacians]]). The sources consulted here do not discuss the name further. Plato puns on it: Socrates will tell “not a tale of Alcinous” (Ἀλκίνου ἀπόλογον) but the tale of a brave man (ἀλκίμου ἀνδρός), Er the Pamphylian (*Republic* 10.614b).

## Family

He is a son of [[nausithous|Nausithous]], who founded the Phaeacian city, and the grandson of [[poseidon|Poseidon]] and Periboea, daughter of Eurymedon, king of the Giants (7.56–63). His brother Rhexenor was killed by Apollo's arrows soon after his marriage, leaving one daughter, [[queen-arete|Arete]]; Alcinous married her and honors her as no other woman on earth is honored (7.63–68). His children are [[nausicaa|Nausicaa]] and five sons, two married and three unmarried (6.62–63; see [[sons-of-alcinous]]); Laodamas, Halius and Clytoneus are named in the games (8.118–119), and Laodamas is the son he loves most (7.170–171).

## Descriptions

- θεῶν ἄπο μήδεα εἰδώς, “who knew counsels from the gods” (6.12). His wisdom comes from the gods (see [[metis]]).
- μεγαλήτωρ, “great-hearted”, in the formula “daughter of great-hearted Alcinous” (6.17, 6.196, 6.213, 6.299; see [[great-hearted]]).
- δαΐφρων, “wise-hearted” (6.256; see [[wise-hearted]]).
- ἥρως, “hero” (6.303; Odysseus addresses him so at 7.303; see [[heros]]).
- ἱερὸν μένος Ἀλκινόοιο, “the holy might of Alcinous”, a periphrasis for the king himself (7.167). The people listen to him “as to a god” (7.11).

## Elsewhere in the Odyssey

**The suppliant at the hearth.** When Odysseus, still in Athena's mist, clasps Arete's knees and sits down in the ashes, the hall falls silent; Alcinous does nothing until the old Echeneus tells him that it is not fitting to leave a stranger on the ground (7.153–166). Then he raises Odysseus by the hand and seats him on the chair of his son Laodamas (7.167–171). He promises the escort for the next day, without toil or pain, even if the stranger's home is farther than Euboea (7.186–196, 7.317–328). He wonders whether the stranger is a god, and says that the gods come openly to the Phaeacians (7.199–206).

**The son-in-law.** When he hears how his daughter received the stranger, he first blames her for not bringing him home with her handmaids (7.299–301). Then he wishes that such a man, “thinking as I think”, would marry his daughter, be called his son-in-law, and stay, with a house and possessions from him; but no Phaeacian will keep him against his will (7.311–316). In Book 6 Nausicaa had wished the same (6.244–245).

**The host.** His palace shines “like the gleam of the sun or of the moon”: bronze walls, golden doors, silver doorposts, gold and silver dogs made by Hephaestus to guard it forever, golden youths holding torches for the feasters, fifty slave women at the mill and the loom (7.84–103). Outside lies an orchard where pears, pomegranates, apples, figs and olives never fail, winter or summer (7.112–121). In Book 8 he calls the assembly, orders the games and the feast, and asks the twelve kings to give the stranger a cloak, a tunic and a talent of gold each (8.387–393). When the stranger weeps at the songs of Demodocus, Alcinous alone notices, and at last asks his name, his parents and his city (8.533–586). After the tale of the dead he praises him: he has told his story “as a singer does, with skill”, and he has the shape of words and a good mind, unlike the liars the earth breeds (11.363–369).

**The cost.** He sends Odysseus home with the gifts (13.1–124). When Poseidon turns the returning ship to stone, Alcinous recognizes his father's old prophecy, stops the escorts, and has twelve chosen bulls sacrificed to Poseidon (13.171–187; see [[the-wrath-of-poseidon]]).

## Outside Homer

**The tale of Alcinous.** Odysseus' narrative in Books 9–12 was known as Ἀλκίνου ἀπόλογος, “the tale told to Alcinous”. Aristotle uses the phrase twice: in the *Poetics* for the recognition in which Odysseus weeps at the singer's song (16, 1455a2), and in the *Rhetoric* for the sixty verses in which Odysseus retells it to Penelope (3.16.7, 1417a). Plato's pun is quoted above (*Republic* 10.614b).

**Corcyra.** Thucydides mentions a precinct (τέμενος) of Alcinous at Corcyra, beside the precinct of Zeus (3.70.4), and the Corcyraeans claimed the Phaeacians as the island's earlier settlers (1.25.4; see [[corcyra-and-the-phaeacians]]).

**Apollonius.** In the *Argonautica* Alcinous and Arete receive the Argonauts, and Alcinous judges between them and the Colchians who claim Medea: he will give her back to her father if she is a virgin, but he will not part her from a husband. Arete sends word to Jason in the night, and the marriage is made in the cave of Macris (4.1068–1131; see [[apollonius-drepane]] and [[apollonius]]).

**Latin poetry.** The orchard became proverbial. Virgil lists “the fruit-trees and the woods of Alcinous” among the many kinds of trees (*Georgics* 2.87). Horace calls the idle young men of his own day the youth “of Alcinous”, too busy with the care of their skin (*Epistles* 1.2.28–29).
