Nausicaa tells Odysseus how to reach the city. He is to follow the wagon with the handmaids while they cross the fields. Near the walls and the harbor he must let her go on alone (6.255–272). Then she gives her reason: “It is their unkind talk that I shun, so that no one afterward may blame me; and there are very overbearing men among the people” (6.273–274; see [[phemis]]). She acts out the talk she fears. A baser man who meets them might say: “And who is this following Nausicaa, a handsome and tall stranger? … Surely he will be a husband for her” (6.276–277). He guesses that she picked up a castaway from some far land, “since there are none nearby”, or that a god came down at her prayer to have her all her days (6.278–281). “It is better so, if she herself has gone about and found a husband from elsewhere; for truly she dishonors these men here … who are courting her, many and good” (6.282–284). She ends with her own judgment: these things would be reproaches to her, and she too blames a girl who goes with men against the will of her father and mother, “before she has come to a public marriage” (6.285–288). The imagined speech is a [[tis-speech]], a speech put in the mouth of an unnamed “someone”.

## The athetesis

A scholion in mss. H and Q (Dindorf p. 317) reports:

> ἀθετοῦνται στίχοι ιδʹ ἕως “ἀνδράσι μίσγηται” ὡς ἀνοίκειοι τῷ ὑποκειμένῳ προσώπῳ. εἴρηται οὖν τοῦτο διὰ τῶν πρὸ αὐτῶν βʹ στίχων, τῶν “ἀλεείνω φῆμιν ἀδευκέα”.
>
> “Fourteen lines are athetized, as far as ‘mingles with men’, as unsuited to the character who speaks. This has in fact been said by the two lines before them, ‘I shun the unkind talk’.”

The count fits 6.275–288 exactly. The scholion gives two grounds. The first is propriety: the lines do not suit the person, a young unmarried princess. The second is redundancy: 6.273–274 already say that she fears what people will say, so the spelled-out gossip adds nothing. The verb is passive, and the note names no critic. It should not be credited to [[aristarchus]] on the strength of his athetesis of 6.244–245 earlier in the same episode (see [[nausicaas-wish-244-245]]), though the ground of objection is the same.

Monro and Allen print the lines. Their apparatus quotes the scholion and adds that the lines are enclosed with a single bracket in the witness marked p (*uncino singulo circumscr.*). Murray prints the lines. Merry and Riddell quote the scholion in their critical note and keep the passage (p. 277).

## Other ancient notes on the passage

The scholia that comment on the lines read them as Nausicaa's mimicry of spiteful talk. Ms. B says that “since there are none nearby” (6.279) is said ironically. A note in mss. B, E, Q and T gives two readings of 6.282–284. In the first, a Phaeacian husband would shame her other suitors, so a foreigner is better. In the second, the words are ironic: “better that she found herself a husband, since she scorns the Phaeacians who court her” (Dindorf p. 317). Merry and Riddell take the whole speech to be “bitterly sarcastical”: nothing in Phaeacia was good enough for the fastidious princess (note on 6.282, p. 277). At 6.287 a scholion in ms. Q records a reading of Aristarchus about the particle at the start of the line, which Merry and Riddell interpret as ἠδʼ ἀέκητι, “and against the will” (p. 278).

## In the poem

The lines do work that 6.273–274 alone do not do. The speech that she puts into a stranger's mouth says aloud what the narrator had made her hide from her father: “she felt shame to name her blooming marriage” (6.66; see [[aidos]]). The gossip repeats the dream's news that the best of the Phaeacians are courting her (6.34–35) and her own wish for a husband like the stranger (6.244–245). It also gives the reason for the plan by which she sends Odysseus into the city alone and tells him to go to her mother (6.289–315). The closing lines (6.286–288) turn the gossip into a rule that she applies to herself. Whether a girl may say such things was the question that the ancient critics asked. The poem gives the words to her and lets her show that she knows the rule.
