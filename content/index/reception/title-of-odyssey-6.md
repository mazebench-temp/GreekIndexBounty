The traditional title of Book 6 is Ὀδυσσέως ἄφιξις εἰς Φαίακας, “the arrival of Odysseus among the Phaeacians”. Eustathius opens his commentary on the book with it: “Book Zeta is entitled ‘the arrival of Odysseus among the Phaeacians’” (Ὅτι Ὀδυσσέως μὲν ἄφιξις εἰς Φαίακας, ἡ ζῆτα ῥαψῳδία ἐπιγέγραπται, p. 234). The same words stand against Ζ in the list of the titles of the Odyssey's books that Dindorf prints before the scholia (Ἐπιγραφαὶ τῶν τῆς Ὀδυσσείας ῥαψῳδιῶν, vol. 1 p. 1).

## Where the titles come from

Dindorf's note on the list reprints a note signed by Buttmann. Buttmann says that the titles do not appear in the Ambrosian and Palatine manuscripts, and that he gives them mainly on the authority of the edition of Barnes. Dindorf adds that one title, that of Book 13, is recorded from ms. H, and that Aelian shows that older grammarians made such titles (Dindorf vol. 1 p. 1, note 1).

Aelian says that the ancients once sang Homer's poems in separate parts, and he lists the names of the parts. For the Odyssey he gives “the events at Pylos, the events at Lacedaemon, the cave of Calypso, the story of the raft, the tales to Alcinous, the Cyclops story, the Nekyia, the story of Circe, the foot-washing, the killing of the suitors, the events in the country, the events at Laertes' house” (*Historical Miscellany* 13.14). His list goes from the raft of Book 5 to the tales that Odysseus tells Alcinous in Books 9–12. The meeting with Nausicaa has no name in it. Aelian's names are names of episodes, not of books. The book title “the arrival among the Phaeacians” belongs to the later division into twenty-four books.

## The verse title

The scholia also preserve a set of verse titles, one hexameter for each book, each beginning with the name of the book's letter. For Book 6 the line is:

> Ζῆτα δὲ Ναυσικάα κόμισʼ ἐν Σχερίῃ Ὀδυσῆα.
>
> “And Zeta: Nausicaa took Odysseus into her care in Scheria.”

Dindorf says that these acrostic verses are read in the old editions and seem to exist in many manuscripts. He judges their wording and meter somewhat worse than the verse titles for the Iliad by the grammarian Stephanus, which the Palatine Anthology preserves (9.385) (Dindorf vol. 1 p. 2, note 21).

## What the titles name

The prose title names the traveller, and it uses the pattern of the titles of Books 3 and 4, “the arrival of Telemachus at Pylos” and “at Sparta”. Father and son both arrive among strangers who receive them. Book 7 continues the arrival under a second title, “the entrance of Odysseus to Alcinous”. The noun ἄφιξις is not Homeric. In Book 6 the poem expresses the arrival with verbs. Odysseus wakes and asks “to the land of what mortals have I come now?” (ἐς γαῖαν ἱκάνω, 6.119; see [[to-the-land-of-what-mortals]]). He ends the book with a prayer to Athena, “grant that I may come to the Phaeacians as one dear to them and pitied” (ἐς Φαίηκας … ἐλθεῖν, 6.327; see [[dear-and-pitied]]). The arrival is still a wish at the end of the book. He reaches the house of Alcinous only in Book 7.

The verse title names Nausicaa. Its verb κομίζω, “take in, take care of”, is the verb of the gossip that Nausicaa imagines: “perhaps she has taken in (κομίσσατο) someone who was driven off course” (6.278; see [[the-gossip-lines-275-288]]). Merry and Riddell note that κομίσσατο there includes both “rescuing” and “taking care of” (note on 6.278, p. 277). The comparison of the verse title with 6.278 is our observation. Eustathius gives a similar account of the book after its title: the poet makes the king's daughter Nausicaa a great help to Odysseus, “not without Athena”. Odysseus can be called a lover of women in a special sense, Eustathius adds, because women love and help him: Circe and Calypso with love, Athena with care, Helen at Troy, and now Nausicaa (p. 234).

The scholia also give two prose summaries (ὑποθέσεις) of the book. Both tell the dream, the washing, the ball game, the waking of Odysseus and his journey to the city behind the girl (Dindorf p. 293). Eustathius prints the first of them before his commentary (p. 234).
