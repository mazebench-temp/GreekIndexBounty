In his first speech to Nausicaa, Odysseus compares her to something he once saw “on Delos, beside the altar of Apollo, … a young sapling of a palm tree springing up” (6.162–163). The simile is treated in [[the-palm-shoot-on-delos]]. This entry follows the tree itself: the palm in the cult and story of Delos, and the later writers who say that it could still be seen.

## The palm of Leto

The *Homeric Hymn to Apollo* tells the birth of the god on Delos. When Eileithyia, the goddess of childbirth, came to the island, labor seized Leto: “she threw her arms around the palm, and pressed her knees into the soft meadow, and the earth beneath smiled; and he leapt out into the light, and all the goddesses cried aloud” (ἀμφὶ δὲ φοίνικι βάλε πήχεε, *Hymn to Apollo* 117–119). In Euripides' *Hecuba* the captive Trojan women wonder whether they will be taken to the island “where the first-born palm and the laurel raised their sacred shoots for dear Leto, the glory of her birth-pangs for the child of Zeus”. There they would sing with the Delian maidens of Artemis' golden headband and bow (*Hecuba* 455–465).

Homer names only a palm by Apollo's altar. He does not name Leto or the birth.

## The ancient dispute

The scholia on 6.163 disagree about the tree (Dindorf p. 307):

- Ms. E explains ἔρνος as an evergreen plant. A note in mss. E and V says: “he means the palm that sprang up for Leto, which she held when she gave birth.”
- Mss. B, P and Q take νέον, “young, new”, with ἀνερχόμενον, “newly springing up”. They compare Thetis' words about Achilles, “he shot up like a sapling” (Iliad 18.56). The participle, they say, suggests both the bloom already present and the hope of growth to come. Their conclusion: “he does not mean the palm that sprang up for Leto.” A further note in mss. B and P says that the poet chose a tree that is straight by nature.

The argument of B, P and Q is drawn from the text. A palm that is “young” and “springing up” cannot be the tree that stood at the god's birth. Merry and Riddell suggest that a palm was always kept growing in the precinct, like the sacred olives of the Academy at Athens (note on 6.162, p. 266). On that view the young tree that Odysseus saw would be a successor of Leto's palm.

## The palm in later writers

Later writers knew of a palm on Delos that was shown to visitors:

- **Cicero.** In the dialogue *On the Laws*, Quintus says that the oak of Marius will stand in that place as long as Latin literature lasts, “unless perhaps your Athens has been able to keep its everlasting olive on the citadel, or they show today the same tall and slender palm that Homer's Ulysses said he saw on Delos”. Many things, he goes on, last longer in memory than they could stand in nature (*On the Laws* 1.2). The words *nisi forte*, “unless perhaps”, make the remark ironic: the palm lives in Homer's verse, whatever stands on Delos. Merry and Riddell cite the passage as a statement that the palm was still to be seen in Cicero's day (p. 266).
- **Pliny.** In a list of very old trees, Pliny names the plane tree on which Marsyas was hanged, and then: “and the palm of Delos is seen, from the time of that same god” (*nec non palma Deli ab eiusdem dei aetate conspicitur*). He goes on to the wild olive at Olympia from which Hercules was first crowned, and the olive at Athens (*Natural History* 16.240).
- **Pausanias.** Writing on the crowns of the games, Pausanias says that most games give a crown of palm, and that a palm is put in the victor's right hand everywhere. The custom began, they say, when Theseus, returning from Crete, held games for Apollo on Delos and crowned the victors with palm. “And Homer too made mention of the palm on Delos, in Odysseus' supplication to the daughter of Alcinous” (8.48.2–3).

These witnesses show the palm as a sight of Delos and as a piece of Homeric learning. Cicero and Pliny name it beside the sacred olive of Athens, a tree with a myth of origin that was shown on a sacred site. Pausanias joins it to a custom of the games. None of them says what kind of tree the visitor actually saw.
