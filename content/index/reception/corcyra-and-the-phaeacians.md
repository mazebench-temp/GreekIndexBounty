Homer gives Scheria no place on a map. Nausithous led the Phaeacians there from Hypereia and settled them “far from enterprising men” (6.4–8; see [[hypereia-of-the-phaeacians]] and [[alphestes]]). Nausicaa says that her people live “far apart in the much-surging sea, the farthest away, and no other mortal has dealings with us” (6.204–205). In Greek history the island of Corcyra, off the coast of Epirus, claimed the Phaeacians as its own. This entry collects the evidence for that claim and for the ancient objections to it. The general account of Scheria is in [[scheria]].

## The Corcyraean claim

**Thucydides.** Before the Peloponnesian War, Corcyra quarrelled with Corinth, its mother city. Thucydides explains the Corinthians' anger. The Corcyraeans did not give Corinth the customary honors at festivals. They were as rich as the richest Greeks and very strong in their fleet. They were “sometimes elated … in accordance with the earlier settlement of Corcyra by the Phaeacians, who had fame in matters of ships” (κατὰ τὴν Φαιάκων προενοίκησιν τῆς Κερκύρας κλέος ἐχόντων τὰ περὶ τὰς ναῦς, 1.25.4). For this reason, he adds, they built up their navy all the more, and they had 120 triremes when the war began. Thucydides reports what the Corcyraeans thought of themselves. He does not identify Scheria with Corcyra in his own voice.

**The precinct of Alcinous.** The cult was real. In the civil strife at Corcyra in 427 BCE, the democratic leader Peithias prosecuted the five richest men of the opposing party. He charged that they had cut vine-props from “the precinct of Zeus and that of Alcinous”, and the fine was a stater for each prop (3.70.4). The men were convicted and sat as suppliants at the temples, asking to pay by installments. When Peithias, a member of the council, insisted on the full penalty, they burst into the council with daggers and killed him (3.70.4–6). The civil war followed. The Phaeacian king had a sacred enclosure in the city beside Zeus.

**Scholars and poets.** The claim passed into learned writing:

- Agallis of Corcyra, a woman grammarian, said that Nausicaa invented the ball game. Athenaeus says that she did so “as a favor to her fellow citizen” (1.14d; see [[athenaeus-ball-games]]).
- Callimachus called Corcyra Scheria, as Strabo reports (7.3.6). Pliny says that Corcyra was “called Scheria and Phaeacia by Homer, and Drepane by Callimachus too” (*Natural History* 4.52).
- Apollonius of Rhodes brings the Argonauts to the island of Drepane in the Ionian strait, “holy nurse of the Phaeacians”, where they find Alcinous and Arete (*Argonautica* 4.982–992; see [[apollonius-drepane]]).
- Strabo, telling the foundation of Syracuse, says that Archias left Chersicrates behind to settle “the island now called Corcyra, formerly Scheria” (6.2.4).
- A scholion in ms. E on 6.3 explains the land of the Phaeacians as “the island now called Corcyra” (Dindorf p. 294).

## The objections

**Aristarchus.** A scholion on 6.8, in the form used for Aristarchus' observations, notes “that the land of the Phaeacians was named Scheria and not Corcyra, and that it is outside the inhabited world as we know it” (ὅτι Σχερία ὠνομάσθη ἡ τῶν Φαιάκων γῆ καὶ οὐ Κέρκυρα, mss. E.P.Q., Dindorf p. 294). See [[aristarchus]].

**The scholia on 6.204.** The scholia argue the question at Nausicaa's words “the farthest away”. One note asks how the Phaeacians can live in Corcyra if Nausicaa says this. It answers that ἔσχατοι, “the farthest”, is said relative to Greece: the poet writes everything with Greece as the point of reference (mss. E.T., on 6.195, Dindorf p. 311). A second note gives the same answer with parallels. When Homer speaks of the greatest mountain, he means the greatest Greek mountain, not the Caucasus or the Alps. Aeschylus calls Canobus “a city at the edge of the land”, meaning the edge of Egypt. And the old servant from Apeire who nursed Nausicaa (7.8–9) came, the note says, from Epirus opposite (mss. E.P.Q.T., Dindorf p. 311). A third note takes the opposite side: here the poet clearly places the land of the Phaeacians far away, not at Corcyra (mss. P.T., Dindorf p. 311).

**Apollodorus and Eratosthenes.** Strabo reports that Apollodorus, drawing mostly on Eratosthenes, criticized those who placed Odysseus' wanderings in real places. He forgave others, but not Callimachus, who claimed to be a scholar and still called Gaudos the island of Calypso and Corcyra Scheria (Strabo 7.3.6; see [[strabo]]).

**A modern view.** Merry and Riddell take Scheria, “the Coast”, as a descriptive name. They suggest that Homer's hints of a site near the Thesprotian coast may have given rise to the Corcyra tradition. Once the identification is tested, they write, “the story melts into romance” (note on 5.34, pp. 212–213).

## The two kinds of evidence

The Corcyraean claim belongs to the history of the island. Its fleet, its cult of Alcinous and its scholars used the Phaeacians as an ancestry. The objections come from readers of the text, who noticed that Homer makes Scheria remote: Nausicaa calls her people the farthest away, and Odysseus reaches the land only by shipwreck. Neither side can be settled from Book 6, where the only fixed points are the Cyclopes near the old home and the sea around the new one.
