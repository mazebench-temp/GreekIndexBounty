{{quote:Od. 6.239-245 | Nausicaa to her handmaids}}

Athena has made Odysseus taller and poured grace on his head and shoulders. He sits apart on the shore, and Nausicaa gazes at him (6.229–237; see [[gold-poured-on-silver]]). Then she speaks to her handmaids. The stranger has not come to the Phaeacians against the will of all the gods. Before, he seemed unsightly to her; now he is like the gods (6.240–243; see [[deato-or-doato]]). She ends with a wish: “If only such a man might be called my husband, living here, and if only it pleased him to stay here” (6.244–245). Then she orders food and drink for him (6.246). The wish is spoken to the handmaids, not to Odysseus.

## The athetesis

A scholion in mss. H and Q (Dindorf p. 314) reports the verdict of [[aristarchus]]:

> ἄμφω μὲν ἀθετεῖ Ἀρίσταρχος, διστάζει δὲ περὶ τοῦ πρώτου, ἐπεὶ καὶ Ἀλκμὰν αὐτὸν μετέβαλε παρθένους λεγούσας εἰσάγων “Ζεῦ πάτερ, αἲ γὰρ ἐμὸς πόσις εἴη.”
>
> “Aristarchus athetizes both lines, but he hesitates about the first, since Alcman also adapted it, bringing on girls who say ‘Father Zeus, if only he were my husband.’”

To athetize a line was to mark it as spurious with a sign in the margin. The line stayed in the text. The scholion says why Aristarchus hesitated, but it does not say why he objected. The printed verb μετέβαλε means “changed, recast”. Dindorf's footnote calls μετέλαβε, “took over for his own use”, the more probable reading, a conjecture of Lehrs (Dindorf p. 314, note on line 24).

The argument from [[alcman]] is an argument from age. If a poet of the seventh century BCE already knew the line and turned it into a song for a girls' chorus, the line was in the poem long before the Alexandrian editors. Alcman's version keeps the wish particle αἲ γάρ and the word πόσις, “husband”, and addresses the wish to Zeus. The scholion gives no evidence of the same kind for 6.245, and Aristarchus' doubt was firmer there.

## Why the lines offended

Other scholia on the same page give the objection that the H.Q. note leaves out. Mss. E and T say: “the words seem unfitting for a maiden and licentious” (ἀπρεπεῖς παρθένῳ εἶναι καὶ ἀκόλαστοι). The same note gives the answer that some critics found in the speaker: the Phaeacians are presented as luxurious and soft-living (τρυφῶντες … ἁβροδίαιτοι), so the words fit her people. Ephorus, it adds, praised the speech as coming “from a soul well endowed for virtue”. Mss. Q and T repeat the report of Ephorus, and the scholiast adds that he himself refers the words to the luxurious life of the Phaeacians (Dindorf p. 314). The judgments of Ephorus and of Plutarch are told in [[plutarch-on-nausicaas-wish]]. The scholion does not say that Aristarchus shared the objection of propriety. That link is a probable inference, since the Alexandrian critics often judged a line by what suited its speaker (compare [[andromaches-advice]]).

Two further notes soften the wish. Ms. B paraphrases the lines and explains that Nausicaa speaks so because the Phaeacians did not seek wives from another land. A note in mss. H, Q and T, introduced with “otherwise”, takes καί in 6.245 as “or”: “would that some Phaeacian man like Odysseus were called my husband, or that it pleased Odysseus himself to stay here” (Dindorf p. 314). The note cites Iliad 15.634 for καί in the sense ἤ.

## The text

The manuscripts keep both lines. Murray prints them, and so do Monro and Allen (Oxford 1908). Their apparatus quotes the scholion and records small variants: Plutarch quotes 6.244 with ἐμεῦ for ἐμοί, and one manuscript has ἐνθάδε for αὐτόθι in 6.245. Eustathius paraphrases the lines and keeps them. He glosses ἅδοι, “might please”, as ἀρέσκοι (p. 251). Merry and Riddell report the athetesis and the hesitation (note on 6.244–245, p. 274).

## In the poem

The wish has echoes in the same book and the next one. In 6.276–284 Nausicaa imagines a baser Phaeacian who sees her with the stranger and says, “Surely he will be a husband for her” (6.277). That speech was athetized too, on the same grounds of propriety (see [[the-gossip-lines-275-288]]). In Book 7 Alcinous speaks his own wish to Odysseus with the same words: “if only, Father Zeus and Athena and Apollo, … you might have my daughter and be called my son-in-law, staying here” (αἲ γάρ, Ζεῦ τε πάτερ …, καλέεσθαι / αὖθι μένων, 7.311–314). The daughter's κεκλημένος … μίμνειν and the father's καλέεσθαι … μένων share the verbs “be called” and “stay”. This is our observation. It suggests that the poem treats the wish as fitting for the family, whatever later readers thought of it on the girl's lips.
