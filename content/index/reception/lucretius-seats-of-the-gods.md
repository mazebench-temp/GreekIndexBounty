When Athena has sent Nausicaa her dream, she goes back to Olympus, “where, they say, the seat of the gods is, firm forever” (6.42; see [[olympus]] and [[seat-firm-forever]]). The narrator describes it in three negatives and two positives. It is not shaken by winds, not wet with rain, and snow does not come near it. A cloudless clear sky is spread out, and a white gleam runs over it. There the blessed gods take their delight all their days (6.43–46).

## Lucretius

At the start of Book 3 of *De rerum natura*, Lucretius praises Epicurus. When the master's reasoning proclaims the nature of things, the terrors of the mind flee, the walls of the world open, and the poet sees things happening through the whole void (3.14–17). Then:

> apparet divum numen sedesque quietae,
> quas neque concutiunt venti nec nubila nimbis
> aspergunt neque nix acri concreta pruina
> cana cadens violat semperque innubilus aether
> integit et large diffuso lumine ridet.
>
> “The divinity of the gods appears, and their quiet dwellings, which no winds shake, no clouds sprinkle with rain, no snow congealed by sharp frost violates with its white fall, but an always cloudless sky covers them and laughs with light spread wide.” (3.18–22)

The sequence follows Homer point by point:

| Homer, Odyssey 6 | Lucretius 3 |
| --- | --- |
| θεῶν ἕδος, “seat of the gods” (42) | *divum … sedes*, “dwellings of the gods” (18) |
| οὔτʼ ἀνέμοισι τινάσσεται, “not shaken by winds” (43) | *neque concutiunt venti*, “no winds shake” (19) |
| οὔτε ποτʼ ὄμβρῳ δεύεται, “nor wet with rain” (43–44) | *nec nubila nimbis aspergunt*, “no clouds sprinkle with rain” (19–20) |
| οὔτε χιὼν ἐπιπίλναται, “nor does snow come near” (44) | *neque nix … violat*, “no snow violates” (20–21) |
| αἴθρη πέπταται ἀνέφελος, “a cloudless clear sky is spread” (44–45) | *semperque innubilus aether integit*, “an always cloudless sky covers” (21–22) |
| λευκὴ δʼ ἐπιδέδρομεν αἴγλη, “a white gleam runs over it” (45) | *large diffuso lumine ridet*, “laughs with light spread wide” (22) |

Lucretius adds what Homer's last line implies: “nature supplies everything, and nothing at any time takes away from their peace of mind” (3.23–24). Homer's gods “take their delight”. Lucretius' gods have *animi pax*, the untroubled calm that Epicurus made the life of the gods. Lucretius also changes the place. Homer puts the seat on the mountain Olympus, with the hedge “where, they say”. Epicurus put his gods in the spaces between the worlds. Cicero's Epicurean speaker in *On the Nature of the Gods* is said to come down “from the intermundia of Epicurus” (1.18). In Lucretius the lines describe a vision granted by philosophy, and the next lines say that nowhere do the regions of Acheron appear (3.25).

Merry and Riddell quote the Lucretius passage in their note on 6.42–46 (p. 256). They also cite Lucan 2.271, on Olympus above the clouds, and Seneca, *On Anger* 3.6, on the upper part of the world that is never driven into storm.

## Tennyson

Merry and Riddell write that “Tennyson imitates the passage in his ‘Morte d'Arthur’, describing the ‘island valley of Avilion’” (p. 256). In the poem, which Tennyson later took into “The Passing of Arthur” in *Idylls of the King*, the dying Arthur says that he goes to Avilion, “Where falls not hail, or rain, or any snow, / Nor ever wind blows loudly”. Homer's three negatives return, applied to the island of the blessed dead.

In his dramatic monologue “Lucretius” (1868), Tennyson gives the Roman poet a description of the gods “who haunt / The lucid interspace of world and world, / Where never creeps a cloud, or moves a wind”. Snow, thunder and the sound of human sorrow never mar “their sacred everlasting calm”. Here the line runs from Homer through Lucretius. The “interspace of world and world” is the Epicurean place between the worlds, and the list of things that do not reach the gods is Homer's.

## The ancient question

The ancient commentators asked whether Homer meant the mountain or the sky. A scholion on 6.42 says that if “where they say” refers to heaven, the words are badly put, and if to the mountain, well put. By “they say” the poet shows that he follows a tradition (Dindorf p. 297). Another explains that Olympus is snowless in its upper parts and “snowy” below the clouds, which reconciles this passage with the Iliad's ἀγάννιφος, “very snowy” (Dindorf p. 297). Lucretius' use of the passage settles the question his own way: the place is no mountain, but the calm of the gods anywhere outside the turmoil of the worlds.
