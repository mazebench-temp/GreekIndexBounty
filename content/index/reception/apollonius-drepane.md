Apollonius of Rhodes (third century BCE) wrote his *Argonautica* for readers who knew the Odyssey well. On their way home from Colchis, Jason, Medea and the Argonauts reach the island of the Phaeacians. In this earlier age Alcinous and Arete already rule there. Apollonius places the island, gives its history, and builds several of its scenes from Odyssey 6 and 7.

## The island and its past

**Drepane.** “There is an island before the Ionian strait, broad and rich, in the Ceraunian sea” (4.982–983). Beneath it, the story goes, lies the sickle, δρέπανον, with which Cronus cut his father's genitals. The poet asks the Muses' pardon for repeating the old tale. Others say that it is the harvest sickle of Demeter, who once lived there and taught the Titans to reap, for love of Macris. “Hence it is called Drepane, holy nurse of the Phaeacians; and so the Phaeacians themselves are of the blood of Uranus” (4.984–992). Apollonius does not name Corcyra. Pliny reports that Callimachus called Corcyra Drepane (*Natural History* 4.52; see [[corcyra-and-the-phaeacians]]).

**Nausithous and Macris.** Homer says only that Nausithous led the Phaeacians from Hypereia to Scheria (6.7–10; see [[nausithous]]). Apollonius gives his house a story. Heracles came “to the house of Nausithous and to Macris, the nurse of Dionysus”, to be purified of the murder of his children. There he loved the Naiad Melite, and their son Hyllus later left the island, which Nausithous ruled, with a band of Phaeacians (4.537–551). The harbor of the Phaeacian city is the Hyllean harbor (4.1125). Macris, daughter of Aristaeus, had received the infant Dionysus in Euboea. Hera drove her out, and she settled “in a holy cave of the Phaeacians, far away” and gave the people boundless prosperity (4.1131–1140).

## Scenes from Odyssey 6

**The girls with the ball.** Before the Argonauts reach the island, Thetis and the Nereids carry the Argo past the Wandering Rocks. Apollonius compares them to “girls near a sandy beach, their dresses tucked up to the waist, who play with a round ball. One catches it from another and sends it high into the air, and it never touches the ground”. So the Nereids pass the ship from hand to hand above the waves (4.948–955). The simile recalls Nausicaa and her handmaids playing ball on the shore of Scheria (6.99–101; see [[ball-play]]). Here the ball never falls, while Nausicaa's ball falls into the eddy (6.115–116). The parallel is our observation.

**The supplication of Arete.** The Colchians pursue the Argonauts and demand Medea from Alcinous. Medea, in fear, clasps the knees of Arete: “I beseech you, queen” (γουνοῦμαι, βασίλεια, 4.1011–1029). In Odyssey 6 Nausicaa tells Odysseus to pass by her father and clasp her mother's knees, because her favor brings a stranger home (6.310–315; see [[queen-arete]]). Apollonius' Arete does what Homer's Arete is said to be able to do. At night, in bed, she pleads with Alcinous for Medea. He decides that Medea goes back to her father if she is still a virgin, but stays with Jason if she is his wife (4.1068–1109). Arete sends a herald at once to tell Jason to marry her (4.1110–1123).

**A wedding on the island.** Jason and Medea are married that night in the cave where Macris once lived, on a bed spread with the Golden Fleece, while nymphs bring flowers and sing the wedding song. The cave, Apollonius says, is still called the sacred cave of Medea (4.1128–1160). In Odyssey 6 a marriage on the island is a wish: Nausicaa wishes that such a man might be her husband and stay there (6.244–245; see [[nausicaas-wish-244-245]]), and her imagined gossip speaks of a foreign husband found for her (6.276–284; see [[the-gossip-lines-275-288]]). Apollonius gives the island the wedding of a foreign couple. This comparison is our observation.

**Gifts and escort.** When the Argonauts leave, Alcinous gives them guest-gifts, and Arete gives Medea twelve Phaeacian maidservants from the palace. They leave Drepane on the seventh day (4.1220–1223). Homer's Phaeacians give Odysseus gifts and an escort home (see [[pompe]]).
