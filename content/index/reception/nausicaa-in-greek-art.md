{{quote:Od. 6.135-138 | The girls scatter}}

Two scenes of Book 6 reached the visual arts: the drive to the river in the mule cart (6.71–84) and the moment when the naked Odysseus comes out of the thicket and the girls scatter, all except Nausicaa (6.127–141). The ancient evidence for both is thin. Two works are known from the descriptions of Pausanias, and a few from surviving vases.

## Polygnotus at Athens

On the left of the Propylaea, the gateway of the Athenian Acropolis, Pausanias saw a building with paintings. He describes Diomedes and Odysseus, Orestes killing Aegisthus and the sacrifice of Polyxena. Then he turns to Polygnotus: Achilles living among the maidens on Scyros, “which Polygnotus also painted. He also painted, by the river, Odysseus coming upon the girls who were washing with Nausicaa, in the same way as Homer composed it” (ἔγραψε δὲ καὶ πρὸς τῷ ποταμῷ ταῖς ὁμοῦ Ναυσικᾷ πλυνούσαις ἐφιστάμενον Ὀδυσσέα κατὰ τὰ αὐτὰ καθὰ δὴ καὶ Ὅμηρος ἐποίησε, 1.22.6).

Pausanias' phrase “in the same way as Homer composed it” says that the painting followed the poem. He gives no further detail. ἐφιστάμενον, “coming upon, approaching”, fits 6.135–137, where Odysseus goes up to the girls (see [[polygnotus]]).

## The chest of Cypselus at Olympia

In the temple of Hera at Olympia, Pausanias describes a cedar chest decorated with figures of ivory and gold and carved in the wood. The Corinthians said that the mother of Cypselus, later tyrant of Corinth, had hidden her baby in it (5.17.5). Among its scenes Pausanias lists “girls on mules, one holding the reins, the other with a veil on her head. They think that they are Nausicaa, the daughter of Alcinous, and her maidservant, driving to the washing places” (Ναυσικᾶν τε νομίζουσιν εἶναι τὴν Ἀλκίνου καὶ τὴν θεράπαιναν, 5.19.9).

The identification is reported with νομίζουσιν, “they think”: Pausanias gives it as the opinion of others. In the poem Nausicaa drives the mules herself, and the handmaids follow on foot (6.81–84; see [[chariot-journey]]).

## The vases

Laura Carrara, in her study of Sophocles' *Nausicaa*, counts only three ancient illustrations of the meeting of the Phaeacian girls with Odysseus, all of the middle of the fifth century BCE (Carrara 2022, p. 19, note 61, with earlier literature: Hauser 1905; Séchan 1967; Touchefeu-Meynier 1992 in the *Lexicon Iconographicum Mythologiae Classicae*). One is the lid of a red-figure pyxis, a round lidded box, by the Athenian vase painter Aison, now in the Museum of Fine Arts, Boston. Carrara notes that the standing female figure inscribed with the name Nausicaa wears a short decorated overgarment (p. 19, note 62).

Carrara discusses these vases together with Sophocles' play on the same scene, the *Nausicaa* or *Plyntriai* (see [[sophocles-nausicaa]]). Carrara reports that the vase evidence, with the play's second title, suggests a chorus of women, not satyrs (p. 13). That connection is a modern hypothesis. The vases themselves were not examined for this entry.
