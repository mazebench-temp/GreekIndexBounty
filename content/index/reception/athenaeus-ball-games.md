## Athenaeus

The first book of Athenaeus' *Deipnosophistae* (“Dinner-sophists”) discusses, among much else, the feasts, singers and dances of Homer. After the singers, it turns to the dances. “The dances in Homer are some of the tumblers, others with the ball” (*Deipnosophistae* 1.14d). The tumblers are the two acrobats who whirl among the dancers on the Shield of Achilles and at the wedding feast in Menelaus' house (κυβιστητῆρε, Iliad 18.605; Odyssey 4.18). The ball dances are those of the Phaeacians: Nausicaa's game at the river (6.99–101) and the dance of Alcinous' sons with the purple ball made by Polybus (8.370–380; see [[ball-play]]).

Then Athenaeus reports who invented the ball:

- **Agallis of Corcyra**, a woman grammarian (γραμματική), gave the invention to Nausicaa, “as a favor to her fellow citizen”. The Corcyraeans held that their island was Homer's Scheria, so for Agallis the princess was a Corcyraean (see [[corcyra-and-the-phaeacians]]).
- **Dicaearchus** gave it to the Sicyonians.
- **Hippasus** gave the ball, and the gymnasia too, to the Lacedaemonians as the first users.

Athenaeus adds his own observation: “Homer brings on her alone of the heroines playing ball” (ταύτην δὲ μόνην τῶν ἡρωίδων Ὅμηρος παράγει σφαιρίζουσαν, 1.14d). The claim holds for the poems. The only other ball in Homer is the one of Alcinous' sons in Book 8, and the Iliad has only the adverb σφαιρηδόν, “like a ball”, of a severed head (Iliad 13.204).

The passage then leaves Homer. Athenaeus names men famous for ball play, Demoteles, brother of the sophist Theocritus of Chios, and one Chaerephanes. He says that a small ball called *follicle* was invented by Atticus of Naples, a trainer, for the exercise of Pompey the Great. The game with a ball called *harpaston* was formerly called *phaininda*, “which I like best of all”. He quotes the comic poet Antiphanes on the calls of the players: “out, long, past him, over him, down, up, short, give it back in the scrum” (1.14e–f). LSJ cites this passage for both words (s.v. φαινίνδα; s.v. φούλλικλον, Ath. 1.14f).

A few pages later Athenaeus says that Sophocles “played ball superbly” when he produced his *Nausicaa* (1.20f; see [[sophocles-nausicaa]]).

## Eustathius

Eustathius, on 6.100–118, treats the ball game at length. He calls the scene a model of brevity: the ball falls into a deep eddy, not on the bank, so the girls are alarmed and shout, and the long shout wakes Odysseus (p. 241). Then he turns to the history of the game. Later, “they say”, ball play was cultivated most by the Lacedaemonians among cities, by Alexander the Great among kings, and by Sophocles among private men (p. 241). He gives two ancient derivations of σφαῖρα (p. 241).

On 6.115 he reports that Dionysodorus wrote πάλλαν, “ball”, for σφαῖραν, as if from πάλλω, “toss”, and he notes that the Romans call a ball *pila* (p. 242). The Oxford text of Monro and Allen records the same variant from Eustathius in its apparatus to 6.115. Eustathius then asks whether Nausicaa's game is the one that the ancients called ἐφετίνδα, in which a player shows the ball to one person and throws it to another, as if to deceive. He says that it was also called by a second name, which the scan of his text renders unclearly (p. 242). LSJ glosses ἐφετίνδα παίζειν as “play at catch-ball” and cites the comic poet Cratinus (s.v. ἐφετίνδα). The feint suits the missed throw of 6.115–116. The poem itself says only that the princess “missed the handmaid”.

## What the ancient readers took from the scene

For Athenaeus the scene is evidence for Homeric dance and for the antiquity of a game that he enjoyed himself. For the Corcyraean Agallis it gave her island a claim to an invention. For the stage it gave Sophocles a role. The poem uses the game for the plot. The ball is the means by which Athena wakes Odysseus, so that he may see the girl who will lead him to the city (6.112–117; see [[nausicaa]] and [[athena]]).
