Johann Wolfgang von Goethe reached Sicily on his Italian journey in 1787. His *Italian Journey* (*Italienische Reise*) records how the island turned him to the Odyssey and to Nausicaa.

## Palermo and Taormina

**Palermo, 7 April 1787.** After a visit to the public garden of Palermo, the dark waves on the northern horizon and the smell of the sea “recalled to my senses and to my memory the island of the blessed Phaeacians”. Goethe hurried to buy a Homer, read “that canto” with great delight, and improvised a translation for his travelling companion, the painter Kniep.

**Palermo, 16 April 1787.** Before leaving the city he hoped to read his daily portion of the Odyssey in the garden and, on a walk, to think out further “the plan of the *Nausikaa*” and to see whether a dramatic side could be won from the subject. He wrote down the plan and drafted some passages.

**Below Taormina, 8 May 1787.** Sitting on the branches of an orange tree in a neglected garden, he went on thinking about the plan, “a dramatic concentration of the *Odyssey*”. He did not think it impossible, “only one would have to keep in view the basic difference between drama and epic”.

## The plan

Under the heading “From memory” (*Aus der Erinnerung*), Goethe describes the plan as he remembered it later. He had become convinced that there could be no better commentary on the Odyssey than the living landscape around him. The main idea was to present in Nausicaa “an excellent maiden, courted by many”, who, unaware of any inclination, has so far rejected all suitors. Moved by a strange foreigner, she steps out of her state and “compromises herself by a premature expression of her inclination, which makes the situation completely tragic”. The plan ran in five acts:

1. The first act began with the ball game. The unexpected acquaintance is made, and her scruple about leading the stranger into the city herself is already a sign of her love.
2. The second act showed the house of Alcinous and the characters of the suitors, and ended with the entrance of Ulysses.
3. The third was given to the adventurer's importance. Goethe hoped to make something artful of the told adventures, received very differently by the different listeners. During the narration the passions rise, and Nausicaa's lively interest in the stranger finally breaks out.
4. In the fourth act Ulysses proves his courage off stage, while the women remain behind with their hopes. With the stranger's great success, Nausicaa holds herself back even less and compromises herself beyond recall with her people. Ulysses, “half guilty, half innocent”, must at last declare that he is leaving.
5. In the fifth act nothing remains for the good girl but to seek death.

Goethe adds that there was nothing in the plan that he could not have painted from his own experience. He too was a traveller far from home who told adventures to entertain company, was taken by the young for a demigod and by older people for a boaster, and was in danger of arousing affections. He says that he wrote little or nothing down, worked out most of it in his head, and that later distractions pushed it aside.

## The plan and Book 6

The first act follows Book 6 closely. The ball game wakes the stranger (6.115–117). Nausicaa's “scruple” about bringing him into the city is her plan to send him in alone, for fear of what the Phaeacians would say (6.255–288; see [[the-gossip-lines-275-288]]). The suitors are Homer's: the best of the Phaeacians court her, “many and good” (6.34–35, 6.284). The tragic turn grows from the lines that ancient critics found improper, her wish that such a man might be her husband and stay (6.244–245; see [[nausicaas-wish-244-245]] and [[plutarch-on-nausicaas-wish]]). Goethe makes that “premature expression of her inclination” the cause of her ruin. Homer's Nausicaa says farewell to Odysseus in Book 8 and asks only to be remembered (8.461–462). These comparisons are our observations.

## The fragment

The play was not finished. Ibolya Tar's study of the fragment describes it as 165 lines written during the Sicilian journey of 1787. Tar also argues that the figure of Nausicaa returns as Ottilie in Goethe's novel *Elective Affinities* (*Die Wahlverwandtschaften*) (Tar 2009, English abstract). The fragment itself was not consulted for this entry.
