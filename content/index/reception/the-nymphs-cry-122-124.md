The ball falls into the eddy, the girls shout, and Odysseus wakes in the thicket (6.115–117; see [[ball-play]]). He asks the question that he asks again on Ithaca: to the land of what mortals has he come, and are they savage or kind to strangers (6.119–121; see [[to-the-land-of-what-mortals]]). Then he interprets the sound he heard: “A female cry has come around me, as of girls, / of nymphs, who hold the steep peaks of the mountains / and the springs of rivers and the grassy meadows. / Or am I perhaps somewhere near human beings who have speech?” (6.122–125). He decides to go and see (6.126).

## The words

- **ὥς τε.** Merry and Riddell join ὥς τε with κουράων, “as it were the voice of girls”, and compare Odyssey 4.45, where the gleam of Menelaus' house is “as it were of the sun or the moon” (note on 6.122, p. 263). The translation follows this reading. The translation notes also record a second way of taking the words, as an exclamation, “how a cry has come round me”.
- **θῆλυς ἀυτή.** The adjective θῆλυς, “female”, has the form of a masculine with the feminine noun. A scholion in ms. P observes that the poet did not say θήλεια (Dindorf p. 304). Ms. P glosses ἀυτή, “shout”, as φωνή, “voice”, here.
- **The reading.** Some manuscripts, and the lexicon of Apollonius the Sophist under ἀμφήλυθε, have ἀυτμή, “breath”, for ἀυτή. One manuscript has ἀοιδή, “song” (Monro and Allen, apparatus to 6.122). The scholia in P and V know ἀυτμή, and they too gloss it as “voice” in this line.
- **κουράων, νυμφάων.** The second genitive defines the first: the girls are nymphs. Merry and Riddell compare Odyssey 4.63, “of men, Zeus-nurtured kings” (p. 263).

## The ancient explanation

A scholion in mss. E, H and P explains the guess: “since he is in a lonely place, he came to this suspicion, that they really are nymphs. Then he passes on: ‘Or am I perhaps near men …?’, if they are not nymphs” (Dindorf p. 304). Mss. P and Q take the ἦ of 6.125 as a particle of doubt, not of assertion. A note in mss. B, E, P and Q explains αὐδηέντων, “who have speech”, as men who use articulate speech, or men who are kind and easy to approach. It adds that bad men and barbarians do not mix with others (Dindorf p. 304). The scholia raise no doubt about 6.123–124.

## The modern suspicion

Merry and Riddell report that 6.123–124, “though accepted without objection by the Scholl., are suspected or rejected by many modern editors” (p. 263). They give two arguments:

1. Nitzsch noted that 6.124 is identical with Iliad 20.9 and Hymn to Aphrodite 99. He also argued that a cry of nymphs would tell Odysseus very little about whether the place had mortal inhabitants.
2. Bothe objected to the pairing κουράων νυμφάων and proposed to read ἢ νυμφέων, “or of nymphs”.

Monro and Allen print both lines and record no ancient doubt about them (apparatus to 6.122–132). Merry and Riddell keep them. They suggest that the voice of nymphs might itself point to mortals, since sacrifices and altars to the nymphs are mentioned elsewhere in the Odyssey (13.350; 17.210).

## The repeated line

The line καὶ πηγὰς ποταμῶν καὶ πίσεα ποιήεντα, “and the springs of rivers and the grassy meadows”, closes a description of nymphs in all three places:

- **Iliad 20.8–9.** At Zeus' summons to the assembly of the gods, no river was absent except Ocean, “nor any of the nymphs who dwell in the fair groves / and the springs of rivers and the grassy meadows”.
- **Hymn to Aphrodite 97–99.** Anchises sees Aphrodite and asks if she is one of the goddesses, “or one of the nymphs who dwell in the fair groves, / or of the nymphs who live on this fair mountain / and the springs of rivers and the grassy meadows”.
- **Odyssey 6.123–124.** Odysseus hears the girls and takes them for nymphs who hold the mountain peaks.

The repetition is the reason for the modern suspicion. A repeated line was a common pattern of epic diction, and it is not by itself evidence of interpolation. The parallel with the Hymn has a further point. In both scenes a man alone in the countryside meets a female figure and asks whether she is divine. Odysseus will put the same question to Nausicaa when he addresses her as goddess or mortal (6.149–152; see [[god-or-mortal]]). This comparison is our observation. The sources consulted do not make it.
