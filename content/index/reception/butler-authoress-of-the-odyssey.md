Samuel Butler (died 1902), the author of *Erewhon*, published *The Authoress of the Odyssey* in 1897. The preface is dated 27 September 1897. It gathers arguments that he had first printed in 1892 in the *Athenaeum* and elsewhere, and in Italian journals published at Trapani and Acireale (Preface). Henry Festing Jones, in his introduction to the second edition, quotes Butler's own note: he “hit upon the female authorship” of the Odyssey at Chiavenna in 1891, and found out that it was written at Trapani in 1892.

## The thesis

Butler made two claims. First, the poem was written by a woman. He takes his starting point from Richard Bentley's remark that the Iliad was written for men and the Odyssey for women. Bentley meant, Butler says, that the Odyssey looks at things from a woman's point of view, and that establishes a first case for a woman author (chapter I). Second, the poem is Sicilian. Butler argues that Scheria and Ithaca “are both of them drawn from Trapani and its immediate neighbourhood”, the town at the western tip of Sicily (title of chapter VIII). He dates the poet roughly between 1050 and 1000 BCE (chapter I).

## Nausicaa as the poet

In chapter XI, “Who was the writer?”, Butler argues from Book 6. The writer must have been a young woman intimate with the household of Alcinous: a dependant, a friend such as the daughter of Dymas, or Nausicaa herself. He rejects the friend. If a friend had written the washing-day episode, he says, she would have come to the washing too, “especially after having said she would in Nausicaa's dream” (see [[daughter-of-dymas]] and [[dream-scene]]). He rejects a dependant as well. In the washing scene “we find Nausicaa, all Nausicaa, and nothing but Nausicaa, among the female *dramatis personae*”, so Nausicaa was giving herself “the little niche in her work which a girl who was writing such a work was sure to give herself”.

Butler finds more evidence in what Nausicaa says and does not do. She would not have let anyone else put into her mouth “the ill-natured talk about her which she alleges to pass current among the Phaeacians” (6.275–288; see [[the-gossip-lines-275-288]]). After Book 6 she nearly disappears: she takes her supper in her own room, is absent from the banquets and the games, and says goodbye to Odysseus briefly in Book 8 (8.457–468). Butler takes this as the writer's own wish to live apart from her father's guests. He admits that the arguments are “not absolutely demonstrative”, but concludes that Nausicaa herself is more likely than another young woman of Trapani to have been the writer (chapter XI). The frontispiece of the book is a picture that he titles “Nausicaa”, a painting on slate in the museum at Cortona known as *La Musa Polinnia* (Preface).

## Drepane, Drepanum and Corcyra

The ancient name of Trapani was Drepanum. Butler uses the Corcyra tradition for his own case. He notes that Thucydides makes the Corcyraeans descendants of the Phaeacians (1.25), and that the older name of Corfu was Drepane. When the Carthaginians had settled at the Sicilian Drepanum, he suggests, the people of Corcyra's Drepane could easily claim Phaeacian descent and call their island Scheria (chapter XIII). Thucydides in fact reports only that the Corcyraeans prided themselves on the earlier settlement of the island by the Phaeacians (see [[corcyra-and-the-phaeacians]] and [[apollonius-drepane]]).

## Reception

Butler says in the preface that no scholar answered his letters to the *Athenaeum* or his Italian pamphlets. No reply from scholars to the book was consulted for this entry. The thesis that a woman composed the Odyssey and that Nausicaa is her self-portrait is Butler's. Homer's text gives no author's name, and the identification of Scheria with any real place is disputed (see [[scheria]]).
