Nausicaa's wish, spoken to her handmaids once Odysseus has bathed and Athena has made him beautiful, troubled ancient readers. The textual side of the problem, Aristarchus' athetesis and the line of Alcman, is in [[nausicaas-wish-244-245]]. This entry gives the moral judgments.

## Plutarch

Plutarch's essay *How the Young Man Should Study Poetry* teaches how to read the poets without taking harm. In section 8 he shows that Homer often marks within one character what is done well and what is done badly. Achilles is wrong to swear that he will protect Calchas even against Agamemnon, and right to push his sword back into the sheath at Athena's word. Phoenix is right to tell how a god stopped him from killing his father (see [[phoenix-patricide-lines]]). “Where the poet's judgment is unclear,” Plutarch goes on, “we must make a distinction and alert the young man in some such way” (27A):

> If Nausicaa, seeing a stranger, Odysseus, and feeling towards him what Calypso felt, because she is luxurious and of an age for marriage, talks such nonsense to her handmaids … then we must blame her boldness and licence (ψεκτέον τὸ θράσος αὐτῆς καὶ τὴν ἀκολασίαν). But if she saw the man's character in his words and admired his conversation, full of good sense, and prays to live with such a man rather than with some sailor or dancer among her fellow citizens, she deserves admiration (ἄξιον ἄγασθαι). (27A–B)

Plutarch quotes 6.244–245, with ἐμεῦ for ἐμοί. His judgment is conditional. He does not condemn the girl. He gives two readings of the same words and leaves the choice to the reader. His next examples follow the same method: Odysseus' pleasure when Penelope draws gifts from the suitors, his counting of the Phaeacians' gifts on the shore of Ithaca, and his sleep when the Phaeacians set him ashore (Odyssey 18.281–283; 13.215–216; 13.116–119).

The second reading has support in the text. Nausicaa speaks after Odysseus' long speech of supplication, in which he praised her, blessed her future husband and wished her “a husband and a home” and “like-mindedness” (6.149–185; see [[homophrosyne]]). The words that would make her admire his “good sense” have just been spoken. Plutarch's “sailor or dancer” fits the Phaeacians as Alcinous will describe them: “best with ships”, lovers of the feast, the lyre and dances (8.246–253). These connections are our observations.

## Ephorus in the scholia

The scholia on 6.244 report the same division of opinion before Plutarch. In mss. E and T the note reads: “The words seem unfitting for a maiden and licentious. They solve the problem from the character: the Phaeacians are presented as luxurious and wholly soft-living. Ephorus, however, on the contrary praises the speech as coming from a soul well endowed for virtue (ὡς ἐξ εὐφυοῦς πρὸς ἀρετὴν ψυχῆς).” In mss. Q and T the scholiast repeats the report about Ephorus and adds his own view: “but I refer this to the soft living of the Phaeacians” (Dindorf p. 314).

Ephorus of Cyme was a historian of the fourth century BCE. The scholion does not say in which work he discussed the lines, and his own text was not consulted here. Ephorus' praise matches Plutarch's second reading, and the scholiast's answer matches the first, which also blames Phaeacian luxury (τρυφῶσα, “luxurious”, in Plutarch; τρυφῶντες in the scholion).

## Later reception

Merry and Riddell report Plutarch as “offended” by the lines and quote only his words of blame. They write that “modern feeling will doubtless side with Ephorus” (note on 6.244–245, p. 274). Plutarch's text, quoted above, gives both judgments.
