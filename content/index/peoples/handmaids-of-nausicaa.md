## Who they are

They are the [[handmaids]] (ἀμφίπολοι) of a princess: girls (κοῦραι, 6.122, 6.135, 6.222) of her household who share her work and her play. The poem calls the same girls δμῳαί, “slave women”, once, in the verse where they finish their meal at the river with Nausicaa, “the slave women and she herself” (6.99; see [[doulios]]). Their number is not given. Two sleep at the doors of her chamber (6.18–19), and “other handmaids” go with her wagon (6.84). Homer gives them no names and no single speaking part. Their only voices are the shout when the ball falls into the river (6.117), the calls with which they urge one another back (6.211), and a report to Nausicaa (6.223).

The narrator describes them with words for noble women. The two at the doors “had beauty from the Graces” (Χαρίτων ἄπο κάλλος ἔχουσαι, 6.18; see [[charis]]). A scholion saw that the phrase prepares the Artemis simile, where the nymphs are “all beautiful” (6.108), and remarks that such beauty is not what one expects of slave bodies (schol. P on 6.18, Dindorf p. 295). Nausicaa calls them ἐυπλόκαμοι, “with lovely tresses” (6.198, 6.238; see [[with-lovely-tresses]]), and λευκώλενοι, “white-armed”, the epithet of Hera and of Nausicaa herself (6.239; see [[white-armed]]).

They are distinct from the old chambermaid Eurymedusa, a woman from Apeire who was given to Alcinous as a prize, nursed Nausicaa, and lights the fire in her room when she comes home (7.7–13).

## Elsewhere in the Odyssey

In Book 7 the handmaids become part of the story Odysseus tells. He says that he saw “your daughter's handmaids playing on the shore” and that Nausicaa was among them, like the goddesses (7.290–291). Alcinous then finds fault with his daughter: she did not bring the stranger home “with the handmaid women” (7.299–301). Odysseus defends her. She told him to follow with the handmaids, he says, but he would not, from fear and shame (7.303–307). In Book 6 she had told him to follow the wagon with the handmaids only through the fields, and to wait outside the city (6.259–296; see [[nausicaa]]).

## Outside Homer

**In art.** Pausanias describes a painting by Polygnotus in the building to the left of the Propylaea at Athens: Odysseus coming upon the women washing clothes with Nausicaa at the river, “just as Homer describes it” (1.22.6). On the chest of Cypselus at Olympia he saw two girls on a mule cart, one holding the reins and one wearing a veil. They were thought to be Nausicaa and her handmaid driving to the washing places (5.19.9; see [[nausicaa-in-greek-art]] and [[polygnotus]]).

**The ball game.** Athenaeus says that Nausicaa is the only heroine whom Homer shows playing ball, and he cites Agallis of Corcyra, who credited her with the invention of the game. He also reports that Sophocles himself played ball with great skill when he produced his *Nausicaa* (*Deipnosophistae* 1.14d, 1.20e–f; see [[athenaeus-ball-games]], [[ball-play]] and [[sophocles-nausicaa]]).

**Apollonius.** When the Argonauts leave the Phaeacians, Queen Arete gives Medea twelve “Phaeacian slave women from the hall” (δμωὰς Φαιηκίδας, *Argonautica* 4.1221–1222; see [[queen-arete]] and [[apollonius-drepane]]).
