## Name

Φαίηκες is the Ionic and epic form; later Greek has Φαίακες, singular Φαίαξ (LSJ s.v. Φαίαξ). The sources consulted here give no agreed etymology. Their personal names are another matter. In Book 8 the young men who stand up for the games are called Acroneus, Ocyalus, Elatreus, Nauteus, Prymneus, Anchialus, Eretmeus, Ponteus, Proreus, Thoon, Anabesineus and Amphialus (8.111–114). The names mean, roughly, “Top-of-the-ship”, “Swift-at-sea”, “Rower”, “Shipman”, “Stern”, “Near-the-sea”, “Oarsman”, “Sea”, “Bow”, “Quick”, “Embarker”, “Sea-on-both-sides”. Merry and Riddell note that almost all Phaeacian names come from seafaring life, except Alcinous, Arete, Laodamas, Polybus and Rhexenor (note on 8.111, p. 319). [[nausicaa|Nausicaa]] and her grandfather [[nausithous|Nausithous]] carry ναυσι-, “ship”, in their names.

## Descriptions

- ἀγχίθεοι, “near to the gods” = ἀγχι- “near” + θεός “god” (5.35). Zeus says it of them when he foretells that they will honor Odysseus like a god and send him home with more gifts than he won at Troy (5.34–40).
- δολιχήρετμοι, “with long oars”, and ναυσίκλυτοι, “famous for ships”, in the verse Φαίηκες δολιχήρετμοι, ναυσίκλυτοι ἄνδρες (8.191, 8.369, 13.166).
- φιλήρετμοι, “oar-loving” (5.386).
- ἀγαυοί, “noble” (6.55), and ἀντίθεοι, “godlike” (6.241; see [[antitheos]]).
- ἔσχατοι, “the farthest away” (6.205), their own word for where they live.

## Family and history

The Phaeacians are kin of Poseidon. Their founder [[nausithous|Nausithous]] was the son of Poseidon and Periboea, youngest daughter of Eurymedon, who ruled the Giants and destroyed his reckless people (7.56–62). Nausithous had two sons, Rhexenor and [[alcinous|Alcinous]]; Rhexenor died young, and Alcinous married his only daughter [[queen-arete|Arete]] (7.63–66). Poseidon himself calls the Phaeacians “of my own race” (13.130).

They once lived in Hypereia, beside the Cyclopes, who harmed them and were stronger. Nausithous led them away to Scheria, walled the city, built houses and temples, and divided the land (6.4–10; see [[cyclopes]], [[hypereia-of-the-phaeacians]] and [[the-phaeacians-leave-hypereia]]). Under Alcinous twelve kings rule the people, and he is the thirteenth (8.390–391; see [[kings]]).

**Close to the gods.** Alcinous says that the gods come to them openly when they sacrifice, and sit at their feasts, and do not hide from a lone traveler, “since we are near to them, as are the Cyclopes and the wild tribes of the Giants” (7.201–206). Athena gave their women skill at the loom, as the men are skilled with ships (7.108–111).

**The escort.** Their gift to strangers is πομπή, the escort home (see [[pompe]]). Alcinous promises to send Odysseus home “without toil or pain”, even if his land is farther than Euboea, which their sailors once reached and came back from in a single day (7.191–196, 7.317–326). Their ships need no helmsmen or steering oars: they know the minds of men and the cities of all peoples, and they cross the sea hidden in mist and cloud (8.555–563). They boast of their ships, their running, the feast, the lyre, the dance, changes of clothing, warm baths and beds, and not of boxing or wrestling (8.246–253).

**The prophecy.** Nausithous used to say that Poseidon resented them because they gave safe escort to all men, and that one day he would strike a Phaeacian ship on its way back from an escort and hide their city behind a great mountain (8.564–571). After they bring Odysseus to Ithaca, the prophecy is fulfilled in part. With Zeus' approval Poseidon turns the returning ship to stone in sight of the city, and Alcinous orders that the escorts stop and that twelve bulls be sacrificed to Poseidon, “in case he takes pity” (13.146–187). The poem leaves them praying at the altar and does not say whether the mountain falls (see [[the-wrath-of-poseidon]]).

## Outside Homer

**Corcyra.** The Corcyraeans claimed the Phaeacians as the island's earlier settlers. Thucydides reports that they were sometimes elated “in accordance with the earlier settlement of Corcyra by the Phaeacians, who had fame in matters of ships” (1.25.4). He also mentions a precinct of Alcinous at Corcyra (3.70.4). Strabo calls Corcyra “formerly Scheria” (6.2.4), but reports that Apollodorus faulted Callimachus for identifying the two (7.3.6). In the scholia the identification is disputed: one note calls their land “the island now called Corcyra” (schol. E on 6.3), and the Aristarchean note on 6.8 says that it was named Scheria, not Corcyra, and lay outside the inhabited world (Dindorf p. 294). See [[corcyra-and-the-phaeacians]] and [[scheria]].

**Apollonius.** In the *Argonautica* the Phaeacians live on Drepane, an island named after a sickle, and they are sprung “from the blood of Uranus” (4.982–992). Heracles came to the house of Nausithous to be purified (4.537–551), and Alcinous and Arete receive the Argonauts and Medea (4.1011–1222; see [[apollonius-drepane]] and [[apollonius]]).

**Luxury.** Later readers made the Phaeacians a byword for soft living. A scholion explains Nausicaa's wish for a husband (6.244) by the luxury of the Phaeacians, who were τρυφῶντες and ἁβροδίαιτοι, “living in luxury” (Dindorf p. 314). Horace calls the idlers of his day “the young men of Alcinous, busier than is right with the care of their skin”, who sleep till midday (*Epistles* 1.2.27–31), and he hopes to come home from a seaside visit “fat, a Phaeacian” (*Epistles* 1.15.24).

**A modern reading.** Charles Segal read the Phaeacian episode as a passage between the fabulous world of the wanderings and the human world of Ithaca, with the community of Scheria set against the isolation of Ogygia (*Singers, Heroes, and Gods in the Odyssey*, 1994, as summarized in BMCR 1995.06.14).
