## The pattern

Three verses, spoken by a man who has come to a land he does not know:

{{quote:ab47a82e-3ebb-5ad8-a570-f31de9b49b18}}

The first line is an exclamation and a question: ὤ μοι ἐγώ, “ah me”, and τέων αὖτε βροτῶν, “of what mortals, now again”. Merry and Riddell, after Ebeling's lexicon, take αὖτε in a question as a mark of impatience at something that happens yet again (note on 6.119, p. 263). The next two lines set out two kinds of people as an either/or (ἦ … ἦε, “are they … or are they”). Each side has three marks:

- **ὑβρισταί**, “arrogant”, men of [[hybris|ὕβρις]]. LSJ glosses ὑβριστής “violent, wanton, insolent man”. In the Iliad Menelaus uses the word of the Trojans (*Iliad* 13.633), and in the Odyssey Laertes uses it of the suitors who hold Ithaca: “you have come to the land you ask about, but arrogant and reckless men hold it” (24.281–282).
- **ἄγριοι**, “savage”, wild like the beasts and the untilled land. Odysseus has a foreboding that the Cyclops will be a man “savage, knowing neither judgments nor laws” (ἄγριον, οὔτε δίκας ἐὺ εἰδότα οὔτε θέμιστας, 9.215).
- **οὐδὲ δίκαιοι**, “not just”, men who do not keep δίκη, the settled custom and judgment between people. Homer uses the adjective of Chiron, “the most just of the Centaurs” (*Iliad* 11.832), and of the Abii, “the most just of men” (*Iliad* 13.6).
- **φιλόξεινοι**, “kind to strangers”, literally “friends of strangers” (see [[xenia]]). LSJ: “loving strangers, hospitable”.
- **νόος θεουδής**, “a god-fearing mind”. LSJ derives θεουδής from θεο-δϝής, “fearing the god”, from θεός and δέος (“fear”), and notes that late poets took it as if it were θεοειδής, “godlike”. The ancient explanations differ. The scholia on 6.121 give “godlike”, “pleasing to the gods” (θεοαδής, from ἁδεῖν), “having a god's voice”, “fearing god” (θεοδεής) and “god-revering” (Dindorf p. 304). Merry and Riddell follow Buttmann and Monro in deriving it from fear of the gods. They read the phrase as an explanation of φιλόξεινοι: the hospitable are those who fear the anger of Zeus, god of guests (note on 6.121, p. 263).

The six words pair off. Arrogance stands against kindness to strangers, savagery against the god-fearing mind, and the lack of justice against both. In the Odyssey the test of a people's justice is its treatment of the unprotected stranger, whom Zeus protects (6.207–208; see [[strangers-and-beggars-are-from-zeus]]).

## Occurrences

- **6.119–121**: Odysseus, naked and alone, wakes at the shout of Nausicaa's handmaids on Scheria.
- **9.175–176**: the last two verses. In his tale to the Phaeacians, Odysseus tells how he said to his companions that he would go with his ship and find out who the Cyclopes were (9.172–176).
- **13.200–202**: all three verses. The Phaeacians have left Odysseus asleep on Ithaca, and he does not know his own land.
- **8.575–576**: a variant in the third person. Alcinous asks Odysseus to tell which of the peoples he met were “harsh and savage and not just” (χαλεποί τε καὶ ἄγριοι οὐδὲ δίκαιοι) and which were kind to strangers. The question that Odysseus asks on arrival becomes the subject of the story he will tell.

Each time the answer differs. The Phaeacians receive the stranger and send him home. The Cyclops eats his guests. On Ithaca Athena, disguised as a young shepherd, tells Odysseus where he is (13.221–249).
