## The pattern

νόστιμος (“of return, belonging to the homecoming”, from νόστος, “return home”; see [[nostos]]) + ἦμαρ (“day”, the epic word beside ἡμέρα). The phrase always stands at the end of the verse. It is one of the Homeric phrases that name a condition of life as a “day”, like the “day of slavery” and the “day of freedom” that Hector imagines for Andromache (δούλιον ἦμαρ, ἐλεύθερον ἦμαρ, Iliad 6.455, 6.463; see [[doulios]]).

The verbs say what happens to the day:

- **Taken away.** In the proem the companions eat the cattle of the Sun, “and he took from them the day of return” (ἀφείλετο, 1.9). Eurycleia says the same of Odysseus: Zeus “has taken away the day of return from you alone” (19.369).
- **Lost.** Telemachus says that his father's “day of return has perished” (ὤλετο, 1.168), and he tells his mother that Odysseus was not the only one at Troy to lose the day of return (ἀπώλεσε, 1.354). Melanthius wishes that it were lost for Odysseus, far away (17.253).
- **Seen.** The whole half-verse οἴκαδέ τʼ ἐλθέμεναι καὶ νόστιμον ἦμαρ ἰδέσθαι, “to come home and see the day of return”, is a wish. Athena, as Mentor, would rather return home late than die at her hearth like Agamemnon (3.232–235); Odysseus tells Calypso that he longs every day to come home (5.219–220), and he says it again to Nausicaa when he takes leave of her (8.464–468).
- **Chosen.** Telemachus says that if mortals could have everything they wished, he would choose first his father's day of return (16.147–149).

Odysseus also uses it once of the absent husband when he speaks to Eumaeus as a beggar (17.571). The adjective appears without ἦμαρ in the phrases “he is still to return” (νόστιμός ἐστι, 4.806) and “no longer to return” (19.85, 20.333), and Menelaus calls Odysseus ἀνόστιμος, “without return” (4.182).
