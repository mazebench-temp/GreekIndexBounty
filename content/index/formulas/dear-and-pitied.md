## The pattern

δός μʼ ἐς + the people or person in the accusative + φίλον ἐλθεῖν ἠδʼ ἐλεεινόν: “grant that I may come to … dear and pitied”. δός is the aorist imperative of δίδωμι, “give, grant”, with an accusative and infinitive, “grant me to come”. φίλος is “dear, a friend, welcome”, and ἐλεεινός is “pitiable, one to be pitied”, from ἔλεος, “pity” (see [[pity]]). Both adjectives are predicates of the speaker: he asks to arrive as a man whom the others will befriend and pity. The verse names the two things a suppliant needs from those he comes to.

**Priam.** The verse is first the request of Priam's prayer to Zeus, when he sets out from Troy to ransom Hector's body from the man who killed him: “Father Zeus, ruling from Ida, most glorious, greatest, / grant that I may come to Achilles' hut dear and pitied, / and send a bird of omen” (Iliad 24.308–310). Zeus hears him and sends an eagle on the right (Iliad 24.314–321). In the hut Priam supplicates Achilles, asks him to pity him, and both weep (Iliad 24.477–512).

**Odysseus.** The Odyssey verse is the same with “the Phaeacians” in place of “Achilles”: δός μʼ ἐς Φαίηκας φίλον ἐλθεῖν ἠδʼ ἐλεεινόν (6.327). It is the request of his prayer to Athena in her grove, at sunset, before he goes as a suppliant into the palace of Alcinous (see [[prayer-type-scene]] and [[phaeacians]]).

**An ancient comment.** A scholion on 6.327 says that the poet put “grant that I be loved and pitied” in a new way (καινῶς ἡρμήνευσε, schol. T, Dindorf p. 320).

## Outside Homer

Aristotle, in his rules for the introduction (προοίμιον) of a speech, has dealt with how to make the audience well disposed, and adds: “and since it is well said, ‘grant that I come to the Phaeacians as one dear and pitied’, one must aim at these two things” (*Rhetoric* 3.14.11, 1415b25–27). He takes the two adjectives as the two feelings a speaker wants from his hearers: goodwill and pity. The Perseus edition labels the quotation “Od. 7.327”; the verse is 6.327.
