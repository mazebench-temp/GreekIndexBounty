## The pattern

τρίς (“three times”) + μάκαρες (“blessed, happy”), a predicate placed first in the sentence, before the people it praises. μάκαρ is the word for the gods, “the blessed gods” (μάκαρες θεοί, 6.46), and said of mortals it gives them a happiness like theirs. τρίς, “three times”, raises the degree.

In its full form, in Book 6, the pronouncement has three parts:

1. **The blessing.** “Thrice blessed” for those near the person praised.
2. **The reason.** Why they are blessed: they see or share in the praised person.
3. **The superlative.** One person above all others is “most blessed” (μακάρτατος).

The first use in the Odyssey is a lament, not a praise. In the storm Odysseus says, “thrice blessed are the Danaans and four times, who perished then / in broad Troy” (τρὶς μάκαρες Δαναοὶ καὶ τετράκις, 5.306–307). He wishes he had died there too, for then he would have had funeral rites and the Achaeans would have spread his fame (5.308–311). The same counting in threes and fours is a different figure, used for repeated attempts (see [[three-times-and-a-fourth]]).

Elsewhere the superlative stands alone. In the underworld Odysseus tells Achilles that “no man before was more blessed than you, nor will be hereafter” (μακάρτατος, 11.483). Mortals are also blessed with ὄλβιος, “happy, prosperous”: Priam calls Agamemnon μάκαρ … ὀλβιόδαιμον, “blessed son of Atreus, child of fortune, blest by the gods” (Iliad 3.182), and in the underworld the shade of Agamemnon calls Achilles ὄλβιε, “happy” (24.36), and then, hearing how the suitors died, calls the absent Odysseus the same (24.192).

## Outside Homer

Ovid's Salmacis addresses the young Hermaphroditus in the order of Odysseus' speech to Nausicaa: if he is a god, he could be Cupid; if a mortal, “blessed are those who bore you, and happy your brother, and fortunate indeed your sister, if you have one, and the nurse who suckled you; but far, far more blessed than all is she, if you have a bride” (*Metamorphoses* 4.320–326). Merry and Riddell cite the passage on Odyssey 6.149 (p. 265). See [[god-or-mortal]].
