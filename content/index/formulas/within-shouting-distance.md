## The pattern

τόσσον … ὅσσον τε γέγωνε βοήσας, “so far … as (a man) makes himself heard when he shouts”. τόσσον and ὅσσον are the epic forms of τόσον, “so much”, and ὅσον, “as much”, used as adverbs of distance. γέγωνε is a perfect of a verb with present sense, “make oneself heard, call out so as to be heard”; βοήσας is the aorist participle of βοάω, “shout”. The subject is not named: “one, a man”. A scholion supplies it: “τις is missing: as far as someone shouting is heard” (schol. H.Q. on 6.294, Dindorf p. 318). The measure is human: the reach of a voice.

Three uses begin ἀλλʼ ὅτε τόσσον ἀπῆν, “but when he (I, we) was as far off as …”, and mark the moment when a man at sea comes within hearing of the land, or the land of him:

- Odysseus, swimming for Scheria, is a shout away when he hears the surf crashing on the rocks (5.399–401).
- Leaving the island of the Cyclops, Odysseus is a shout away when he taunts Polyphemus (9.473–474). He does it again from twice the distance (9.491).
- Passing the island of the Sirens, the ship is a shout away when the Sirens notice it and begin their song (12.181–183).

The Iliad does not have the formula, but it uses the same verb for the same measure. Odysseus' ship stands in the middle of the Achaean line, where a man could make himself heard to both ends (γεγωνέμεν ἀμφοτέρωσε, Iliad 8.222–226, 11.5–9). Other Homeric distances are measured by the reach of the body in the same way: the cast of a spear (Iliad 15.358–359), the throw of a discus (Iliad 23.431–432), the length of a furrow ploughed by mules (Odyssey 8.124).
