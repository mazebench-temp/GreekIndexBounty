## The pattern

ἕδος (“seat, abode”, from the root of ἕζομαι, “sit”) + ἀσφαλές (“firm, unshakable”, ἀ- “not” + σφάλλω “trip, overthrow”) + αἰεί (“always”). The phrase fills the second half of the verse after the main caesura.

Olympus is the gods' ἕδος in the Iliad too, in two forms: θεῶν ἕδος αἰπὺν Ὄλυμπον, “the seat of the gods, steep Olympus” (Iliad 5.367, 5.868), and ἵνʼ ἀθανάτων ἕδος ἐστί, “where the seat of the immortals is” (Iliad 5.360, 8.456). The addition ἀσφαλὲς αἰεί is found in Homer only at Odyssey 6.42.

Hesiod has the same half-verse twice in the *Theogony*. Earth is “broad-breasted, the seat firm forever of all” (πάντων ἕδος ἀσφαλὲς αἰεί, *Theogony* 117), and Earth bore starry Sky, “so that it might be a seat firm forever for the blessed gods” (ὄφρʼ εἴη μακάρεσσι θεοῖς ἕδος ἀσφαλὲς αἰεί, *Theogony* 128). The words thus serve for the foundation of the world and for the gods' place above it. In the Odyssey they name the mountain.

**The description.** In the Odyssey the phrase leads into four verses on what the seat is like: not shaken by winds, not wet by rain, not touched by snow; a cloudless clear sky (αἴθρη … ἀνέφελος) spread over it and a white gleam (αἴγλη) running over it; and in it the blessed gods take their delight all their days (6.43–46; see [[olympus]] and [[olympians]]). The Iliad gives Olympus snow: it is ἀγάννιφος, “very snowy” (Iliad 1.420, 18.186), and Thetis leaps “down from snowy Olympus” (Iliad 18.616). A scholion reconciles the two pictures: the poet calls the upper parts snowless and the lower parts, below the clouds, snowy (schol. B.H.P.Q.T. on 6.44, Dindorf p. 297).

**“They say.”** The narrator introduces the seat with ὅθι φασί, “where, they say” (6.42), a phrase he uses in the Iliad for a place known by report, the bed of Typhoeus among the Arimi and the beds of the nymphs on Sipylus (Iliad 2.783, 24.615). A scholion argues that the words are badly put if Olympus here means the sky and well put if it means the mountain, and that by “they say” the poet shows an inherited tradition, not an invention of his own (schol. E.P.Q. on 6.42, Dindorf p. 297). Merry and Riddell report that some modern editors took the phrase as a sign that the lines were inserted later, and judge that view “needlessly strong” (note on 6.42, pp. 255–256).

**Two ancient readings.** Rhianus, the Hellenistic poet and editor, read αἰθήρ, “upper air”, for αἴθρη, “clear sky” (6.44), and τῇ ἔνι, “in it”, referring to the gleam, for τῷ ἔνι, “in it”, referring to Olympus (6.46; schol. H.P., Dindorf p. 297).

## Outside Homer

Lucretius renders the description for the gods of Epicurus, whose “quiet seats” no winds shake, no clouds sprinkle with rain, and no snow defiles, while a cloudless upper air covers them and laughs with light spread wide (*De Rerum Natura* 3.18–22; see [[lucretius-seats-of-the-gods]]). Every element of 6.43–45 appears in the same order.
