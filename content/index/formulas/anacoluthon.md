## The pattern

ἀνακόλουθος means “not following on, inconsequent” (ἀν- “not” + ἀκόλουθος “following”). Greek grammar used it for a construction that changes as it goes: LSJ cites Dionysius of Halicarnassus, *On Thucydides* 41–42, for ἀνακόλουθον σχῆμα, and a scholion on Iliad 2.469 for the adverb ἀνακολούθως.

In Homer the figure belongs to speech. A speaker opens a clause that needs a main clause (the *apodosis*, the “answer” to an “if”, “since” or “when”), and then a thought, a maxim or a description pushes in. The main clause either never comes, or it comes later in a new construction. The ancient commentators describe it without the modern term. They say that the poet “gives nothing in reply” to the opening word, or they tell the reader to take the clause with a sentence several verses further on and to treat what lies between as a parenthesis (διὰ μέσου).

An English translation must choose between supplying the missing clause and keeping the break. This library keeps the break and marks it with punctuation (see the translation notes for the Odyssey).
