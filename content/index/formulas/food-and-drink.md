## The pattern

βρῶσις (“eating, food”, from the root of βιβρώσκω, “eat”) + πόσις (“drinking, drink”, from the root of πίνω, “drink”), joined by τε … τε, “both … and”. Both are nouns of action in -σις, and the pair fills the end of the verse after the bucolic diaeresis (the break before the fifth foot). The case changes with the sentence: accusative as the object of “give” or “set beside” (1.191, 6.209, 15.490), nominative in “there is food and drink in the ship” (βρῶσίς τε πόσις τε, 10.176, 12.320).

In the Odyssey it names the keep that one person gives another. An old woman servant sets food and drink before Laertes when he is worn out from work in his vineyard (1.191–193). Odysseus tells Eumaeus that Zeus gave him a kind master, “who gives you food and drink” (15.488–491). Odysseus twice tells his men that there is food and drink in the ship, once to make them eat (10.176–177) and once to keep them from the cattle of the Sun (12.320–323). The Phaeacians stow in the ship that takes him home “all the drink and food” (πόσιν καὶ βρῶσιν ἅπασαν, 13.72), with the nouns in the other order.

The Iliad has the pair only once, in a refusal. Achilles refuses to eat before he has avenged Patroclus: “neither drink nor food (οὐ πόσις οὐδὲ βρῶσις) would go down my throat, while my comrade lies dead” (Iliad 19.209–210).

The commoner pair of the feast scenes uses other nouns, ἐδητύς and ποτής or πόσις, in the closing line “when they had put away the desire for drink and food” (see [[desire-for-drink-and-food]] and [[feast]]). In the accusative πόσιν, “drink”, has the same form and accent as πόσιν, “husband” (see [[posis]]).
