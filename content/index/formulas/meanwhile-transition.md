## The pattern

ὥς (“so, thus”) sums up what has just been told, and ὁ μέν (“he, for his part”) names the character the narrative now leaves. The verse often adds where he is (ἔνθα, “there”) and what he is doing, in the imperfect, which leaves the action going on. The answering particle, αὐτάρ or δέ, brings in the next character and the next scene. The μέν … δέ pair says that the two actions belong together, and the imperfect of the first says that it goes on while the second is told.

The Odyssey uses the verse with ὁ μέν for its hero more than once. “So he lay sleeping there, much-enduring brilliant Odysseus” opens Book 6 and comes again when he sleeps in the palace of Alcinous (6.1, 7.344). “So he prayed there, much-enduring brilliant Odysseus” opens Book 7 (7.1), with the next scene, the girl driving to the city, in the following verse (7.2). Other uses close a story inside a speech: “so he was held there” (3.284), “so he perished there” (4.511, 14.137). The verse that ends Odysseus' night in the swineherd's hut has the same shape (14.523).

The plural form, ὣς οἱ μέν, “so they …”, is common at the opening of books of the Iliad, where it leaves one army and turns to the other (Iliad 9.1, 16.1, 18.1, 20.1, 22.1, 23.1). One Iliad scene ends with a sleeper, as Odyssey 6 begins: “So the father slept quietly on the peak of Gargarus, overcome by sleep and love” (ὕπνῳ καὶ φιλότητι δαμείς, Iliad 14.352–353), and Sleep then runs to the ships of the Achaeans.
