## The pattern

ἔνθʼ αὖτʼ (“then in turn”) + ἄλλʼ (“another thing”) + ἐνόησε (“thought of, devised”) + the subject with its epithet, filling the verse: ἔνθʼ αὖτʼ ἄλλʼ ἐνόησε θεά, γλαυκῶπις Ἀθήνη, “then in turn the gray-eyed goddess Athena thought of another thing”. ἄλλʼ is ἄλλο or ἄλλα with the vowel elided, and the sense is the same either way. It is the adjective, not ἀλλά, “but”, which has a different accent. νοέω is “perceive, see with the mind”, and its aorist ἐνόησε is “thought of, hit upon”.

The verse comes at a point where the action has reached a pause or a dead end, and the next verses say what the new thought is and how it is carried out. The thought is usually explained by a clause or by the deed itself.

**Athena.** Most uses belong to Athena, who moves the plot of the Odyssey forward. In Ithaca she takes the likeness of Telemachus and gathers a crew (2.382–387), and then pours sleep on the suitors (2.393–398). She makes a phantom in the likeness of Iphthime and sends it to the sleeping Penelope (4.795–797). In the storm she binds the other winds (αὐτὰρ Ἀθηναίη κούρη Διὸς ἄλλʼ ἐνόησεν, 5.382–385). She puts Penelope to sleep and beautifies her before the suitors (18.187–196). In Book 23 she holds back Dawn for the reunited couple: “and rosy-fingered Dawn would have appeared to them as they wept, had not Athena thought of another thing” (23.241–246), and later she rouses Dawn when she judges they have had enough of love and sleep (23.344–348).

**Mortals.** Helen thinks of another thing at Sparta and puts a drug into the wine (4.219–221). Penelope decides to show herself to the suitors (16.409–411). Nausicaa is the subject once (6.251).

**The Iliad.** Only at the funeral of Patroclus: Achilles cuts his fair hair for the dead (Iliad 23.140–141), and when the pyre will not burn he prays to the winds (Iliad 23.193–194). Both verses go on with στὰς ἀπάνευθε πυρῆς, “standing apart from the pyre”.
