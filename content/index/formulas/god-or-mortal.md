## The pattern

The gods in Homer go among men in human shape, and a stranger of unusual size, beauty or strength may be one of them. The speaker who suspects it builds his words on the alternative.

1. **The question.** “Are you some god, or a mortal?” or “Who are you?”
2. **If a god.** What follows if the stranger is divine: the speaker will not fight him, or he names the god he is most like, or he asks for mercy and promises offerings.
3. **If a mortal.** What follows if the stranger is human: a challenge, or a blessing.

**In battle.** Diomedes, meeting Glaucus, asks who he is and adds: “but if you are one of the immortals come down from the sky, / I would not fight with the gods of heaven” (εἰ δέ τις ἀθανάτων γε κατʼ οὐρανοῦ εἰλήλουθας, Iliad 6.123–129). If he is a mortal, “come nearer, so that you may reach the bounds of destruction sooner” (Iliad 6.142–143).

**In the Odyssey.** Alcinous uses Diomedes' verse when he wonders whether the stranger in his hall is a god (εἰ δέ τις ἀθανάτων γε κατʼ οὐρανοῦ εἰλήλουθεν, 7.199), and Odysseus answers that he is like mortals, not gods, “neither in form nor in build” (7.208–210). Telemachus, when Athena has transformed his father, says, “surely you are some god, one of those who hold the wide sky; be gracious”, and Odysseus answers, “I am no god” (16.181–187). The suitors warn Antinous that the beggar he struck may be a god, for gods go about the cities in the likeness of strangers (17.483–487).

**In praise.** In the Hymn to Aphrodite, Anchises greets the goddess, who has come to him “like an unwed maiden in stature and looks” (παρθένῳ ἀδμήτῃ μέγεθος καὶ εἶδος ὁμοίη, 82), as “queen”, and asks which of the blessed ones she is, Artemis, Leto, Aphrodite, Themis, Athena, one of the Graces or the nymphs, and promises her an altar (*Homeric Hymn to Aphrodite* 92–102). In Odyssey 6 the form is used by a man who knows that the girl is mortal, or nearly knows it, and uses the doubt as praise (see the note).

**The accent.** In 6.149 the ancient readers disputed the little word between θεός and βροτός. Ptolemy of Ascalon wrote it with a circumflex, ἦ, as the particle of a question, “are you some god? or a mortal?”, and a scholion calls that reading “more graceful”. Others read ἢ … ἢ, “either a god or a mortal” (schol. P on 6.149, Dindorf p. 306; quoted by Merry and Riddell, p. 265). Murray prints ἦ with a question mark.

## Outside Homer

Virgil gives Aeneas the same question when Venus meets him disguised as a huntress: “O how shall I call you, maiden? For your face is not mortal, nor does your voice sound human: O goddess surely, Phoebus' sister, or one of the nymphs' blood?” (*Aeneid* 1.327–329). Ovid's Salmacis begins her address to Hermaphroditus with the two conditions, god or mortal, and goes on, as Odysseus does, to bless his parents, his brother and his bride (*Metamorphoses* 4.320–326; see [[thrice-blessed]]). Merry and Riddell cite both passages on 6.149 (p. 265).
