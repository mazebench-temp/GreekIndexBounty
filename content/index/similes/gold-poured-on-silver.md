## The simile

{{quote:fe3b2497-f81f-5580-8973-fdd324b734e3}}

**The vehicle.** ὡς δʼ ὅτε τις χρυσὸν περιχεύεται ἀργύρῳ ἀνήρ, “as when a man pours gold around silver”. περιχεύεται is an aorist subjunctive of περιχέω, “pour around”, with a short vowel; ἀργύρῳ is a locative, “on the silver”, the ground on which the gold is laid (Merry and Riddell on 6.232). The work is gilding: a film of gold over a silver object. The man is ἴδρις, “skilled, knowing”, and his knowledge has a divine source: “whom Hephaestus and Pallas Athena have taught (δέδαεν) craft of every kind” (6.233–234; see [[hephaestus]] and [[athena]]). δέδαεν is a reduplicated aorist of the root δα-, “learn, teach”. The last clause gives the result: χαρίεντα δὲ ἔργα τελείει, “and he completes graceful works”.

**The tenor.** ὣς ἄρα τῷ κατέχευε χάριν κεφαλῇ τε καὶ ὤμοις, “so she poured grace upon him, on his head and shoulders” (6.235). Two words join the halves. The verb is χέω, “pour”, in both: the man “pours around” (περι-χεύεται) and the goddess “pours down” (κατ-έχευε). And the craftsman's “graceful” works (χαρίεντα) are made of the same word as the goddess's gift, χάρις, “grace, charm” (see [[charis]]). The point of likeness is an addition that makes a fine thing finer. The silver is already silver, and the gold makes it shine.

**The ancient reading.** A scholion says the comparison is for the change (ἐξαλλαγή): Odysseus, who was handsome, is made to look handsomer, as silver, which is fine, is finer with gold poured round it. It praises the poet for not taking the image from outside the scene: since Athena does the work, he uses her own craftsmen for the picture. It ends with a distinction: “nature is the cause of being beautiful, grace the cause of seeming so” (schol. H.Q. on 6.235, Dindorf p. 313). On 6.233 another scholion glosses δέδαεν as “taught” and adds “for she is a goddess of work” (ἐργάνη, schol. Q.), and on 6.234 τέχνην παντοίην is “all the parts of the craft”, or “of the goldsmith's craft” (schol. H.Q.T.). Eustathius repeats the distinction of nature and grace and likens Odysseus to “gilded silver” (ἀργύρῳ χρυσωθέντι), in his commentary on 6.230–237 (ed. Stallbaum vol. 1, p. 251; p. 1561 of the Rome edition, which Merry and Riddell cite for his note on 6.231).

## Elsewhere in Homer

**Gilding.** The Odyssey shows the craft once in a scene of its own. At Pylos, Nestor sends for the goldsmith Laerces (χρυσοχόον, 3.425) to pour gold around the horns of a heifer for Athena. He comes with his bronze tools, “the limits of his craft”, anvil, hammer and tongs, and “he poured it around the horns of the cow, working it with skill, so that the goddess might be glad when she saw the offering” (3.432–438). The phrase χρυσὸν κέρασιν περιχεύας, “pouring gold around its horns”, is also a vow of sacrifice (3.384; Iliad 10.294).

**Craft taught by Athena.** Athena gives the Phaeacian women their skill at the loom (7.110–111), and she “taught” (δέδαε, the verb of 6.233) the daughters of Pandareus to do famous works (20.72). In the Iliad she loves the craftsman Phereclus, “who knew how to make all intricate things with his hands” (Iliad 5.59–61), and a carpenter's line is straight because he knows his skill “by the counsels of Athena” (Iliad 15.410–412).

**The repetition.** The whole passage returns when Odysseus, bathed by the housekeeper Eurynome, sits down before Penelope (23.153–163). The verses 23.157–162 repeat 6.230–235, with one change: the last verse begins ὣς μὲν τῷ περίχευε, “so she poured (it) round him”, where Book 6 has ὣς ἄρα τῷ κατέχευε. The line before is new as well: “Athena poured great beauty down over his head” (23.156). The half-verse “grace on his head and shoulders” also opens Book 8, when Athena makes Odysseus taller and sturdier for the Phaeacian games (8.18–20).

## Outside Homer

**Hephaestus and Athena as teachers.** The Homeric *Hymn to Hephaestus* (Hymn 20.2–3) sings of Hephaestus, “who with gray-eyed Athena taught (ἐδίδαξεν) splendid works to men on the earth”, men who before lived in caves like beasts. Plato's Stranger lists the gifts of the gods to the first men: fire from Prometheus, and “crafts from Hephaestus and his fellow craftswoman” (τέχναι δὲ παρʼ Ἡφαίστου καὶ τῆς συντέχνου, *Statesman* 274c). Merry and Riddell cite both passages on 6.233.

**Virgil.** When Aeneas steps out of the cloud before Dido, his mother Venus has given him beautiful hair, the bright light of youth, and gladness in his eyes: “such grace as hands add to ivory, or when silver or Parian marble is set round with yellow gold” (*Aeneid* 1.588–593). Virgil keeps the setting of a meeting with a queen, the goddess who beautifies, and the gold around silver, and he adds the ivory and the marble.
