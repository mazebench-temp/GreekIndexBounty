## The simile

{{quote:f92693a0-e62c-5405-abb5-b58f6d545e4c}}

οὔλας ἧκε κόμας, ὑακινθίνῳ ἄνθει ὁμοίας: “she let fall curling locks, like the hyacinth flower”. The subject is Athena, and the verb ἧκε (“sent, let go”), with κὰδ δὲ κάρητος, “down from his head”, at the end of the verse before, gives hair that falls thick and long. οὖλος in Homer is “thick, woolly”, an epithet of cloaks (4.50) and of the wool on a cloak (Iliad 10.134), and of hair it means thick and curling (see [[thick-and-woolly]] and [[hair]]). The comparison is three words: ὑακίνθινος, “of the hyacinth”, + ἄνθος, “flower”, + ὅμοιος, “like”, agreeing with κόμας. It names no point of likeness.

**The flower.** The only other ὑάκινθος in Homer grows with dewy lotus and crocus when the earth sends up soft grass for Zeus and Hera on Gargarus, “thick and soft” (Iliad 14.347–349). Which plant the Greeks meant is not settled. Merry and Riddell (on 6.231) list the views: a kind of gladiolus, the hyacinth as we know it, or, with Voss and Nitzsch, the blue iris.

**Color or curl.** Two readings are old. Eustathius first takes the likeness to be color: the hair is black, like the hyacinth flower. He then sees the difficulty, since Athena says she will destroy the ξανθάς, “fair, tawny”, hair on Odysseus' head when she makes him old (13.399, 13.431). He suggests that long hardship may have darkened it, or that the likeness lies in the curl instead of the color (πρὸς τὸ οὐλότριχον, on 6.231, ed. Stallbaum vol. 1, p. 251). A scholion on the verse discusses only οὖλος and its many senses (schol. E on 6.231, Dindorf p. 313). Merry and Riddell prefer the curl: the Iliad's hyacinth is “thick and soft”, and the point is the clustering flowers with curling petals. They quote Houben (1856), who argues that Homer would not leave a color in doubt with a flower of several colors, and that the words describe the density and softness of the hair. Against this, the hyacinth was dark to later poets (see below). The dispute meets another. When Athena restores Odysseus before his son, he becomes “dark-skinned” (μελαγχροιής) and his beard turns κυάνεος, “dark” (16.175–176).

## Elsewhere in Homer

The verse 6.231 returns as 23.158, in the second bath and beautification, before Penelope (23.153–163). It is followed in both places by the simile of the gilder (see [[gold-poured-on-silver]]). Other flowers in Homer are the lotus and the crocus of the same Iliad passage, and the “saffron-robed” Dawn (κροκόπεπλος, Iliad 8.1).

## Outside Homer

Theocritus' reaper defends the dark skin of the girl he loves: “the violet too is dark, and the lettered hyacinth” (καὶ τὸ ἴον μέλαν ἐστὶ καὶ ἁ γραπτὰ ὑάκινθος, *Idyll* 10.28). Merry and Riddell cite this line for the dark color of the Greek hyacinth. They also report, through Nitzsch, a late imitation: the letter-writer Aristaenetus describes hair “naturally curled, like the hyacinth flower, after Homer” (*Letters* 1.1). This passage was not checked in its own text.
