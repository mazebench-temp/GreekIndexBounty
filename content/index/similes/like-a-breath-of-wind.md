## The simile

ἡ δʼ ἀνέμου ὡς πνοιὴ ἐπέσσυτο δέμνια κούρης, “and she, like a breath of wind, rushed to the girl's bed” (6.20). πνοιή (Attic πνοή) is a breath or a blowing, from πνέω, “blow, breathe”, and ἀνέμου is “of wind”. The comparing word ὡς stands inside the phrase it governs, between the genitive and its noun. ἐπέσσυτο is an aorist of ἐπισεύομαι, “rush at, dart to”, and takes the bed as its goal. The comparison is three words long and names no point of likeness. The verses around it supply it. The doors of the chamber are shut (θύραι δʼ ἐπέκειντο φαειναί, 6.19), and the goddess is next found standing above the girl's head (6.21). The wind is what passes through a closed room without opening the door.

## Elsewhere in Homer

**The winds that carry the gods.** A god's travel is often measured by the wind. Athena and Hermes bind on golden sandals that carry them over the sea and the boundless earth “together with the breaths of the wind” (ἅμα πνοιῇς ἀνέμοιο, 1.97–98, 5.44–46; Iliad 24.340–342). The messenger Iris is ποδήνεμος, “wind-footed” (Iliad 2.786).

**The dream-visitor at the door.** The closest parallel is the other dream that Athena sends in the Odyssey. In Book 4 the phantom in the likeness of Iphthime enters Penelope's chamber “past the strap of the bolt”, stands above her head and speaks (4.802–803), and when it has finished it slips away by the bolt of the doorpost “into the breaths of the winds” (ἐς πνοιὰς ἀνέμων, 4.838–839). A scholion on 6.20 refers to that passage: one should understand that the goddess slipped in again past the strap of the bolt (schol. P.Q. on 6.20, Dindorf p. 295). See [[dream-scene]].

## Outside Homer

**Hermes through the keyhole.** In the Homeric *Hymn to Hermes* the infant god returns to his cave at dawn and slips sideways through the keyhole of the hall, “like an autumn breeze, like a mist” (αὔρῃ ὀπωρινῇ ἐναλίγκιος, ἠύτʼ ὀμίχλη, 145–147). Merry and Riddell compare the Hymn on 6.20.

**Virgil.** Merry and Riddell also compare the shade of Anchises, which Aeneas tries three times to embrace and which slips from his hands, “like the light winds and most like winged sleep” (par levibus ventis volucrique simillima somno, *Aeneid* 6.700–702). The same three verses are used for the ghost of Creusa (*Aeneid* 2.792–794).
