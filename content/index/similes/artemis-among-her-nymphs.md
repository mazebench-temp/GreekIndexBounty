## The simile

{{quote:Od. 6.102-109 | Artemis among her nymphs}}

**The vehicle.** Artemis “of the showering arrows” (ἰοχέαιρα) goes through the mountains, along Taygetus in Laconia or Erymanthus in Arcadia, “delighting in wild boars and swift deer” (6.102–104; see [[artemis]], [[taygetus]] and [[erymanthus]]). With her play the nymphs, “daughters of aegis-bearing Zeus”, ἀγρονόμοι, “who range the wild country” (6.105–106; see [[nymphs]]). Her mother Leto watches and is glad in her heart (γέγηθε δέ τε φρένα Λητώ, 6.106; see [[leto]]). Artemis holds her head and brow above them all, and she is “easily known” (ἀριγνώτη), “though all are beautiful” (6.107–108).

**The tenor.** “So she stood out among her handmaids, the unwed maiden” (ὣς ἥ γʼ ἀμφιπόλοισι μετέπρεπε παρθένος ἀδμής, 6.109; see [[nausicaa]], [[handmaids]] and [[unbroken]]). The simile runs to eight verses, from οἵη (“such as”) at 6.102 to ὣς (“so”) at 6.109.

**The points of likeness.** The likeness has several points, and a scholion calls the image “exact in every point” (κατὰ πάντα ἀπαράλλακτος ἡ εἰκών, schol. P on 6.102, Dindorf p. 302).

- One stands out among many who are also beautiful. The narrator prepares this point at 6.18: the handmaids have “beauty from the Graces”, and a scholion notes that the poet thereby prepares “though all are beautiful”, which would not suit slavish bodies (schol. P on 6.18, Dindorf p. 295).
- The nymphs play (παίζουσι, 6.106), as the girls have just played at ball (ἔπαιζον, 6.100).
- Artemis is the virgin goddess, and Nausicaa is called παρθένος ἀδμής, “the unwed maiden”, in the verse that closes the simile.
- Leto's gladness is a mother's pride in her daughter. Athena has spoken of the good report that makes Nausicaa's father and mother rejoice (6.29–30), and Odysseus will call her parents “thrice blessed” (6.154).

**The text.** Murray prints κατʼ οὔρεα, “through the mountains”. A scholion reports the reading κατʼ οὔρεος, “down from the mountain”, and adds “γρ. οὔρεα, which is better” (schol. H.P., Dindorf p. 302). Merry and Riddell print οὔρεος and picture Artemis coming down from a peak and then ranging along the ridges (note on 6.102, p. 261). Gellius' quotation of the passage also has κατʼ οὔρεος (9.9.12). For ἀγρονόμοι the scholia explain the accent: with the accent on the second-to-last syllable the word means “those who range (νέμουσαι) the fields”, not “those pastured” there, and some wrote ἀγρόνομοι. The grammarian Megaclides read a different half-verse, ἀγρόμεναι παίζουσιν ἀνὰ δρία παιπαλόεντα, “gathered, they play through the rugged thickets” (schol. H.P.Q., E.V. and H.P. on 6.106, Dindorf p. 303).

**The animals.** A scholion asks why Artemis' game is boars and deer and not lions. Its answer: these places abound in boars and deer, and lions are not born there, “as Aristotle testifies” (schol. on 6.103–104, Dindorf pp. 302–303; see [[aristotle-lions-of-europe]]).

## Elsewhere in Homer

Comparison with Artemis is the Odyssey's praise for a woman of beauty and rank. Helen comes out of her chamber “like Artemis of the golden distaff” (4.122), and Penelope comes down “like Artemis or golden Aphrodite” (17.37 = 19.54). Only Nausicaa's comparison is drawn out into a scene with mountains, game, nymphs and a mother.

## Outside Homer

**Virgil.** When Dido first appears in the *Aeneid*, she walks to the temple among a crowd of young men, “such as Diana leads her dances on the banks of the Eurotas or along the ridges of Cynthus, and a thousand Oreads follow and throng on either side; she carries her quiver on her shoulder, and as she walks she towers over all the goddesses; joy thrills Latona's silent heart” (*Aeneid* 1.498–502; then “such was Dido”, 1.503–504). The parts of the Homeric simile are all there: the mountain or river places, the following nymphs, the height, the mother's joy.

**Probus' criticism.** Aulus Gellius reports, from the pupils of the grammarian Valerius Probus, that Probus thought this Virgil's least successful translation from Homer (*Attic Nights* 9.9.12–17). In Homer a girl playing among her companions in a lonely place is rightly compared with Diana hunting among the nymphs of the mountains; Dido walking with serious step through the middle of a city among Tyrian leaders has nothing in common with Diana's games and hunts. Homer tells of the goddess' hunting, while Virgil gives her a quiver to carry like a burden. Homer's Leto is glad deep in her heart, and Virgil's *pertemptant* makes the joy weak and superficial. Above all, Virgil left out the flower of the passage, “and she is easily known, though all are beautiful”, the greatest praise of beauty, that one excels among many who are beautiful and is easily known among them all.
