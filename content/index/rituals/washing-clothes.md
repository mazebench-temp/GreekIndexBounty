## The custom

Clothes were washed in the open, at pits or troughs beside running water. The washers carried the clothes into the water, trod them with their feet in the pits, and spread them out on clean ground to dry in the sun. The fullest account in Homer is the washing of Nausicaa and her handmaids at the river of Scheria:

{{quote:640fcd02-7ddf-5ad1-b6eb-61fcd6d3d865}}

The work belongs to women. In the Iliad the washing troughs of Troy stand by the two springs of the Scamander, “broad, fine, made of stone”, where the wives and daughters of the Trojans washed their shining clothes “formerly, in peace, before the sons of the Achaeans came” (Iliad 22.153–156). In the Odyssey the queen's daughter washes with her handmaids, a Phoenician woman of the household is washing by a ship when one of the Phoenician traders seduces her (15.417–421), and Penelope, in the suitor Amphimedon's account, washes the great robe that she has woven before she shows it (24.147–148).

Clean clothes mark a household's standing. Nausicaa's father must sit in council “with clean clothes on your skin”, as she tells him (6.61), her brothers want “freshly washed” clothes for the dance (6.64), and a well-washed cloak (φᾶρος ἐϋπλυνές) is a fitting gift for a guest (8.392, 8.425, 13.67). The opposite is the beggar's dirt. ῥυπόω, “be dirty”, describes the rags of the disguised Odysseus (13.435) and his own words about them (19.72, 23.115).

## Words

- πλύνω, “wash”, is the word for washing cloth. LSJ sets it against λούω, “bathe”, which is used for the body (LSJ s.v. πλύνω; see [[bathing]]). Book 6 has the Epic future πλυνέω (6.31, 6.59) and the aorist πλῦνα (6.93). νεόπλυτος, “freshly washed”, occurs only at 6.64.
- πλυνοί are the washing places. LSJ explains them as “trough, tank, or pit, in which dirty clothes were washed by treading” (s.v. πλυνός). In Book 6 they are also called βόθροι, “pits” (6.92). A scholion glosses πλυνοί as the stones on which they wash, or as the whole place (schol. B on 6.40, Dindorf p. 297), and another glosses βόθροι as “the washing pits, the tanks” (schol. B.Q. on 6.92, Dindorf p. 301). Eustathius says that elsewhere πλυνοί are tubs for washing, but that in Homer they are pits, and that the two words in Book 6 mean the same thing (Eustathius p. 236, on 6.40).
- ἐπηετανός, “abundant, never failing”, describes the pits at 6.86. LSJ gives “troughs always full” (s.v. ἐπηετανός). Merry and Riddell think that the pits were dug at the side of the river, so that the water passed in and out of them all the time (note on 6.86, p. 260).
- στείβω, “tread”, is the action of the washing (6.92). Eustathius glosses it “they trod, they rubbed with their feet” (p. 240, on 6.92).
- ῥύπος, “dirt”, and ῥυπόω, “be dirty”, name what the washing removes (6.59, 6.87, 6.93).

## Outside Homer

Eustathius reports that Sophocles, “when he produced the *Plyntriai*” (the *Washerwomen*), played the part of Nausicaa playing ball and won great acclaim (Eustathius p. 241, Rome p. 1553–1554). Athenaeus calls the play in which Sophocles played ball the *Nausicaa* (1.20e–f). See [[sophocles-nausicaa]] and [[ball-play]].

After Homer πλυνός comes to mean a washing tub or a fuller's tub (Lucian, *Fugitivi* 12, as cited by LSJ s.v. πλυνός).
