## The game

Homer has two scenes of play with a ball, and both are set among the Phaeacians. In Book 6 Nausicaa and her handmaids play on the river bank while the washing dries, and Nausicaa leads the μολπή, the play with song and movement (6.99–101). In Book 8 Alcinous tells his sons Halius and Laodamas to dance alone, because no one can match them. They take a fine purple ball made by Polybus. One bends back and throws it toward the clouds, and the other leaps from the ground and catches it before his feet touch the earth. Then they dance on the ground, passing it quickly between them, while the other young men stand round and beat time (8.370–380).

{{quote:Od. 6.99-101 | The girls play at ball}}

In both scenes the ball belongs with dance. The word for the game in Book 6, μολπή, is the word for song and dance together (see [[dance]]), and in Book 8 the throwing turns into a dance (ὠρχείσθην, 8.378). Merry and Riddell think that the girls' game was also “accompanied with a measured chant and a dance movement, to which the throwing and catching of the ball kept time” (note on 6.101, p. 261). Athenaeus, reading Homer, counts “dances with the ball” among the Homeric dances (1.14d).

## Words

- σφαῖρα, “ball”. Eustathius gives two ancient derivations: from σπάω, “draw together”, because the ball is drawn into itself and round, or from αἴρω, “lift”, because it is thrown up high (p. 241). Both are guesses of ancient grammarians.
- παίζω, “play”, is the verb of the game (ἔπαιζον, 6.100). It is used also of the nymphs who play with Artemis in the simile that follows (παίζουσι, 6.106; see [[artemis-among-her-nymphs]]).
- σφαιρίζω, “play at ball”, is a later word. LSJ cites it from Plato and later prose (s.v. σφαιρίζω).

## Outside Homer

**The inventor.** Athenaeus reports the ancient claims about who invented the ball game (*Deipnosophistae* 1.14d). Agallis of Corcyra, a woman grammarian, gave the invention to Nausicaa, “as a favor to her fellow citizen”. The Corcyraeans identified their island with Scheria (see [[scheria]]). Dicaearchus gave it to the Sicyonians, and Hippasus gave it, with the gymnasia, to the Lacedaemonians. Athenaeus adds that Nausicaa is the only one of the heroines whom Homer shows playing ball. See [[athenaeus-ball-games]].

**Sophocles.** Athenaeus says that Sophocles was trained in dance and music, and that he “played ball superbly when he produced the *Nausicaa*” (1.20e–f). Eustathius tells the same story of the *Plyntriai* (*Washerwomen*): “they say” that Sophocles played the part of Nausicaa playing ball and won great acclaim (p. 241, Rome p. 1553–1554). Eustathius also names the Lacedaemonians, Alexander the Great and Sophocles as those who cared most for ball-play in later times (same page). See [[sophocles-nausicaa]].
