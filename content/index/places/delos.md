## Etymology

Δῆλος (Dēlos). Ancient writers explained the name from the adjective δῆλος, “clear, visible”, because of a legend that the island suddenly became visible. LSJ cites this explanation from Aristotle (fragment 488) and the *Etymologicum Magnum* and marks it as uncertain. The real origin of the name is unknown.

## Descriptions

In Homer Delos appears only in Odysseus' speech to Nausicaa. He saw there, “beside the altar of Apollo, a young sapling of a palm tree springing up” (6.162–163; see [[palm-tree]] and [[altar]]).

{{quote:Od. 6.162-167 | The palm on Delos}}

He went there “and a great army followed me, on that journey on which evil sorrows were to come upon me” (6.164–165). Homer does not say when this was. Merry and Riddell place it on the voyage from Aulis to Troy, through the Cyclades. A scholion connects it with a visit of Menelaus and Odysseus to Delos to fetch the daughters of Anius, and says that Simonides told the story (see [[odysseus-at-delos]]).

## Outside Homer

**Birthplace of Apollo.** The *Homeric Hymn to Apollo* greets Leto as the mother of Artemis, born on Ortygia, and of Apollo, born “on rocky Delos”, beside the palm and the streams of the Inopus (*Hymn to Apollo* 14–18). In labor Leto “threw her arms around the palm” and set her knees on the soft meadow, and Apollo leapt out into the light (115–119; see [[leto]]). Ionians gather on Delos with their children and wives to honor Apollo with boxing, dance and song (146–150).

**The palm.** Several later writers speak of a palm shown on Delos. Pausanias explains why most games give a crown of palm: Theseus, it is said, returning from Crete, held games on Delos for Apollo and crowned the victors with palm. He adds that Homer mentions the palm on Delos in Odysseus' supplication of the daughter of Alcinous (Pausanias 8.48.3). In Cicero's *De Legibus* Quintus says, with irony, that the people of Delos show “today” the same tall and slender palm that Homer's Ulysses saw (*De Legibus* 1.2). Pliny reports that a palm at Delos is shown from the time of the god himself (*Natural History* 16.240). Merry and Riddell cite Euripides' *Hecuba* (458) for the palm of Leto's labor.
