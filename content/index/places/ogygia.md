## Etymology

Ὠγυγίη (Ōgygiē), Attic Ὠγυγία. The origin of the name is unknown. Ancient scholars connected it with the adjective ὠγύγιος, which LSJ explains as “of Ogyges”, a mythical king of Attica, and hence “primeval, primal”. Hesiod calls the water of Styx ὠγύγιον, “primeval” (*Theogony* 806). A scholion on 1.85 (E) states the connection: people call all ancient things ὠγύγια after Ogyges, an ancient king of Athens. LSJ does not list the island under the adjective.

The edition of Antimachus wrote Ὠγυλίη at 1.85. The scholia (H.M.P.Q.) say that the two places are different. Ogygia lies toward the west, while Hesiod puts Ogylia near Crete.

## Descriptions

- νῆσος … ἀπόπροθεν εἰν ἁλί, “an island far off in the sea” (7.244). No god or mortal has dealings with Calypso there (7.246–247).
- νῆσος ἀμφιρύτη, “an island washed all around”, ὅθι τʼ ὀμφαλός ἐστι θαλάσσης, “where the navel of the sea is” (1.50). Athena describes Calypso's island in these words before she names it at 1.85. Strabo quotes the line as νήσῳ ἐν Ὠγυγίῃ, “on the island Ogygia”, where the text of Homer has νήσῳ ἐν ἀμφιρύτῃ (Strabo 1.2.18).
- νῆσος δενδρήεσσα, “a wooded island” (1.51).
- τηλόθʼ ἐοῦσαν, “being far away” (5.55): Hermes has to cross a long stretch of sea to reach it.

Homer names Ogygia six times, always with νῆσος, “island” (1.85, 6.172, 7.244, 7.254, 12.448, 23.333). Calypso lives in a great cave (5.57). Around it grow alder, black poplar and cypress, where birds of the sea roost. A vine with grapes spreads over the cave, four springs flow side by side, and soft meadows of violet and parsley bloom around them. Even a god, Homer says, would stop to look and be glad (5.63–74).

**Odysseus on Ogygia.** Odysseus reaches Ogygia alone, after Zeus has wrecked his ship and he has drifted for nine days on the keel (7.249–254, 12.447–448). Calypso keeps him for seven years and wants to make him her husband and immortal (7.255–261, 23.333–336). At the council of the gods Athena proposes to send Hermes to Ogygia with the decision of the gods that Odysseus must go home (1.84–87). In Book 5 Hermes delivers it, Odysseus builds a raft, and he sails for seventeen days before he sees the mountains of the Phaeacians' land (5.278–280).

## Outside Homer

**Calypso's island in the west.** Ancient geographers disagreed about where Ogygia was. Callimachus identified it with Gaudos, an island that Strabo places off Cape Pachynus in Sicily (Strabo 7.3.6, 1.2.37, 6.2.11). Strabo reports that Apollodorus, following Eratosthenes, blamed Callimachus for this identification, since Homer had placed the wanderings in the Ocean (7.3.6, 1.2.37). Strabo himself took Homer's words about Ogygia, the navel of the sea and the daughter of Atlas as clearly invented in the Atlantic sea (Strabo 1.2.18). In Plutarch's dialogue *On the Face in the Moon* a speaker begins his myth with Homer's line “an isle, Ogygia, lies far out at sea” and puts it five days' sail west of Britain (*De facie* 26, 941A–B).

**Other uses of the name.** Strabo reports that Boeotia was once called Ogygia (Strabo 9.2.18).
