## Etymology

Ποσιδήιον is the Ionic form of Ποσιδεῖον, a neuter noun made from the name of Poseidon, “the place of Poseidon”. LSJ gives “temple of Poseidon” and cites only this line for the Ionic form (LSJ s.v. Ποσιδήϊος). The adjective Ποσιδήϊος means “sacred to Poseidon”. The Iliad uses it of the grove at Onchestus, Ποσιδήϊον ἀγλαὸν ἄλσος, “the splendid grove of Poseidon” (Il. 2.506; see [[onchestus]]). A scholion (E.V.) explains the noun as a precinct dedicated to Poseidon. Homer does not say whether it is a building or an open sacred enclosure.

## Descriptions

{{quote:Od. 6.266-267 | The shrine and the place of assembly}}

- καλὸν Ποσιδήιον, “the fine shrine of Poseidon” (6.266).
- ἀγορὴ … ἀμφίς, “the place of assembly around it” (6.266). The scholia (B.Q.) say that the agora is around the shrine. Merry and Riddell take ἀμφίς to mean that the shrine divides the agora, which lies on both sides of it (see [[assembly]]).
- ῥυτοῖσιν λάεσσι κατωρυχέεσσʼ ἀραρυῖα, “fitted with hauled stones bedded deep in the earth” (6.267). The participle ἀραρυῖα is feminine and belongs to the agora. ῥυτός, “dragged”, describes stones too large to carry, which had to be hauled into place (scholia B.Q.T.). κατωρυχής, “dug in”, describes stones sunk into the ground. The scholia gloss it as “well founded”.

The same two words describe walls of stone elsewhere in the Odyssey. Eumaeus built the wall of his yard with “hauled stones” (14.10), and the Cyclops' yard is built with stones “bedded in the earth” (9.185). Merry and Riddell infer from these parallels that the stones of the agora form a low wall, and perhaps seats. They reject the idea of a pavement. In Book 8 the Phaeacians come to their assembly place “beside the ships” and sit down “on polished stones” (8.5–6).

In Book 6 the shrine is the only building of a god that is named in the Phaeacian city. Homer says that Nausithous, the founder, “made temples of the gods” (6.10), but he names no other.
