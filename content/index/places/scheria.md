## Etymology

Σχερίη (Scheriē) is the epic form. Later Greek writes Σχερία. The meaning is uncertain. Hesychius glosses a noun σχερός as ἀκτή, αἰγιαλός, “shore, beach” (cited by LSJ s.v. σχερός). Merry and Riddell (on 5.34) take Hypereia and Scheria as plain descriptions of the landscape, “the Highlands” and “the Coast”. This reading is a modern proposal. Merry and Riddell also report an ancient story from scholion E. The island was first called Drepane (“sickle”), because the sickle with which Cronus was mutilated was kept there. It was later called Scheria from σχεῖν (“to stop”), because Poseidon, at Demeter's request, stopped a flood that threatened it.

## Descriptions

ἐρίβωλος = ἐρι- “very” + βῶλος “clod of earth”: with rich soil. Zeus foretells that Odysseus will reach “Scheria with rich soil” on the twentieth day (5.34).

ἐρατεινή, “lovely”: Athena leaves “lovely Scheria” for Marathon and Athens (7.79–80).

Homer never calls Scheria an island (νῆσος). Odysseus first sees it from the sea as “the shadowy mountains of the land of the Phaeacians”, and it looks to him like a shield on the misty sea (5.279–281). Nausicaa says that her people live “far apart in the much-surging sea, the farthest away” (6.204–205).

## In the Odyssey

The Phaeacians once lived in Hypereia, near the [[cyclopes]], who harmed them. [[nausithous]] moved them to Scheria, “far from grain-eating men”, built a wall around the city, and built houses and temples, and divided the fields (6.4–10; see [[hypereia-of-the-phaeacians]]). In Odysseus' time [[alcinous]] rules. The city has a high wall, a harbor on each side and a narrow entrance, a place of assembly around the precinct of Poseidon, and the house of Alcinous (6.262–272, 7.43–45; see [[posideion]] and [[house-of-alcinous]]).

Odysseus reaches the land on a raft from Ogygia and comes ashore at the mouth of a river (5.441–453; see [[river-of-scheria]]). He leaves it asleep on a Phaeacian ship. On its return, Poseidon turns the ship to stone in sight of the city, with Zeus' consent, and the Phaeacians sacrifice to him so that he will not hide their city behind a great mountain (13.146–187; see [[poseidon]]).

## Outside Homer

**Corcyra.** The identification of Scheria with Corcyra (Corfu) is ancient. Thucydides reports that the Corcyraeans prided themselves on their fleet partly because of “the earlier settlement of Corcyra by the Phaeacians, who had fame in matters of ships” (Thucydides 1.25.4). He reports the Corcyraean claim and does not make it in his own voice. In 427 BC a precinct (τέμενος) of Alcinous stood at Corcyra beside the precinct of Zeus (Thucydides 3.70.4). Strabo says that Chersicrates settled “the island now called Corcyra, formerly Scheria” (Strabo 6.2.4). A scholion on 6.3 also calls the Phaeacians' land “the island now called Corcyra”. Apollonius of Rhodes calls the island Drepane, “holy nurse of the Phaeacians”, and derives the name from a sickle (*Argonautica* 4.982–992). See [[corcyra-and-the-phaeacians]].

**Against the identification.** The identification was also contested in antiquity. A scholion on 6.8, in the form used for Aristarchus' observations, says that the land was called Scheria and not Corcyra, and that it lies outside the inhabited world (see [[aristarchus]]). Two scholia on 6.204 disagree: one says that the poet places the Phaeacians far away and not at Corcyra, and another answers “and yet they live in Corcyra”. Strabo reports that Apollodorus, following Eratosthenes, blamed Callimachus for calling Corcyra Scheria (Strabo 7.3.6; see [[apollodorus]] and [[strabo]]). Merry and Riddell (on 5.34) think that a passage in Odysseus' own tales, which seems to place Scheria off the coast of the Thesprotians in Epirus, may have given rise to the Corcyraean tradition. “When we attempt to establish the identity,” they write, “the story melts into romance.”

**Modern study.** S. Psoma's history of Corcyra (2022) discusses the identification with Scheria in its chapter on the island's legendary traditions. The review by P. Merciai (*BMCR* 2024.05.19) reports this. The same review reports remains of ship-sheds at Corcyra from the early seventh century BC as evidence of the island's early sea power. They are evidence for the historical city, not for the Homeric one.
