## Name

Κύκλωψ, plural Κύκλωπες. Homer does not explain the name. Hesiod does: the Cyclopes of the *Theogony* had one round eye in the middle of the forehead, “and so their name was Cyclopes” (*Theogony* 143–145), and LSJ gives the sense “round-eyed” on this evidence (LSJ s.v. Κύκλωψ). LSJ also notes that the singular in the Odyssey always means Polyphemus (1.69).

## Descriptions

- ὑπερηνορέοντες, “overweening” (6.5). The participle means “exceeding in manhood”, but Homer always uses it in a bad sense: of the Trojans, of the Cyclopes, and most often of the suitors (LSJ s.v. ὑπερηνορέων; 2.266, 4.766). A scholion glosses it “excelling in ἠνορέη, that is in courage, and so arrogant” (schol. V on 6.5, Dindorf p. 294).
- ὑπερφίαλοι, ἀθέμιστοι, “overbearing, lawless” (9.106).
- βίηφι … φέρτεροι, “greater in strength” (6.6). The Cyclops claims the same: “we are much stronger” than the gods (πολὺ φέρτεροί εἰμεν, 9.276).

## In the Odyssey

**Their way of life.** Odysseus describes them in Book 9. Trusting in the gods, they neither plant nor plow, yet wheat, barley and vines grow for them unsown. They have no assemblies for counsel and no laws (θέμιστες); they live in hollow caves on the mountain peaks, each giving law to his own children and wives, with no care for one another (9.106–115; see [[themis]]). The one whom Odysseus meets “did not look like a grain-eating man” (9.190–191). He says that the Cyclopes do not care for Zeus or for the blessed gods (9.275–278).

**Near the gods.** Alcinous counts them, with the wild tribes of the Giants, among the peoples to whom the gods are “near”, as they are to the Phaeacians (7.205–206; see [[phaeacians]]). Both peoples are kin of Poseidon: Polyphemus is his son (1.68–73), and so is the Phaeacian founder [[nausithous|Nausithous]] (7.56–62).

**Polyphemus.** The Cyclops whom Odysseus blinds is the strongest of them, the son of Poseidon and the nymph Thoosa (1.68–73). His blinding is the cause of Poseidon's anger (see [[the-wrath-of-poseidon]]). He is a different figure from Polyphemus the Lapith of the Iliad (see [[polyphemus]]).

## Outside Homer

**Hesiod.** In the *Theogony* the Cyclopes are three sons of Earth and Sky, Brontes, Steropes and Arges, who gave Zeus the thunder and made the thunderbolt (*Theogony* 139–146; see [[hesiod]]).

**Sicily.** Thucydides reports that the Cyclopes and Laestrygonians are said to be the oldest inhabitants of a part of Sicily; he cannot say what race they were or where they came from, and leaves the matter to the poets (6.2.1).

**Builders.** Later writers made the Cyclopes the builders of the prehistoric walls of Tiryns and Mycenae (LSJ s.v. Κύκλωψ, citing Hellanicus, Pherecydes, Bacchylides and Strabo 8.6.11).

**A modern reading.** Carol Dougherty reads the Phaeacians and the Cyclopes as complementary images of what an overseas settler might find (*The Raft of Odysseus*, 2001, as summarized in E. F. Cook's review, *Classical Philology* 98, 2003).
