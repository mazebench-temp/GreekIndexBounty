## In Homer

**The words.** γυμνός is “naked”. It is used of the body without clothes and of the warrior's body without armor. The verb γυμνόομαι is “strip oneself, be stripped, be exposed” (LSJ s.v. γυμνόω). The private parts are called μήδεα (6.129, 18.67, 18.87) and also αἰδώς, “shame”, the same word as the feeling (see [[aidos]]). Odysseus threatens to strip Thersites of “your cloak and your tunic, which cover your nakedness” (τά τʼ αἰδῶ ἀμφικαλύπτει, Iliad 2.261–262).

**Exposure in war.** In battle γυμνός means unprotected. In the Iliad a man is wounded where his back is “exposed” as he turns (γυμνωθείη, Iliad 12.428), and Teucer shoots Glaucus in the arm where he sees it bare (γυμνωθέντα, Iliad 12.387–389). Hector fears that if he goes to Achilles without his armor, Achilles will kill him “naked, / just like a woman” (Iliad 22.124–125).

**Stripped and unmanned.** In the Odyssey to be stripped can mean to be made helpless. Hermes warns Odysseus that Circe may make him “base and unmanned” once he is stripped (ἀπογυμνωθέντα, 10.301), and Odysseus repeats the fear to her (γυμνωθέντα, 10.341). When he takes off his beggar's rags to begin the killing of the suitors, the poet uses the same verb: “he stripped himself of his rags” (γυμνώθη ῥακέων, 22.1).

**Odysseus' nakedness.** The Odyssey's chief scene of nakedness follows the shipwreck. On Ino's advice Odysseus strips off the clothes that Calypso gave him and swims with her veil (5.343, 5.372–373). He comes ashore with nothing, and in Book 6 he meets Nausicaa and her handmaids naked. The rest of the Phaeacian episode clothes him. He receives a cloak and a tunic from the washing (6.214), which Queen Arete recognizes in the palace as her own work (7.234–238), and the Phaeacian kings give him cloaks and tunics as gifts (8.392–393).
