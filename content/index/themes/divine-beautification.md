## The motif

In the Odyssey a god can change how a mortal looks, for a short time and for a purpose. The usual agent is Athena, and the usual subject is Odysseus. The scenes share their language:

- She makes him “taller to look upon and sturdier” (μείζονά τʼ εἰσιδέειν καὶ πάσσονα, 6.230, 23.157; μακρότερον καὶ πάσσονα, 8.20; μείζονα … καὶ πάσσονα, of Laertes, 24.369).
- She lets curling locks fall from his head, “like the hyacinth flower” (6.230–231, 23.157–158; see [[like-the-hyacinth-flower]]).
- She “pours grace” (χάριν κατέχευε) on his head and shoulders (6.235, 8.19, 23.162), with the simile of the craftsman who pours gold around silver at 6.232–235 and 23.159–162 (see [[gold-poured-on-silver]] and [[charis]]).

The occasions are meetings at which the hero must be received or recognized:

1. With Nausicaa after his bath in the river (6.227–237).
2. Before the Phaeacian games, “so that he might become dear to all the Phaeacians, / and feared and respected” (8.18–23).
3. Before Telemachus in Eumaeus' hut, when Athena touches him with her golden wand, gives him clean clothes and restores his youth and dark beard (16.172–176).
4. Before Penelope, after his bath, in the lines repeated from Book 6 (23.156–162).

The same goddess does the same for others. She pours grace on Telemachus as he goes out among the people, so that all of them gaze at him (2.12–13, 17.63–64). She makes old Laertes taller and sturdier after his bath, and his son marvels at him (24.367–371). She beautifies Penelope in her sleep before she appears to the suitors: she cleans her face with the ambrosia of Aphrodite, makes her taller and sturdier, and whiter than sawn ivory (18.190–196).

**The reverse.** Athena can also make a man ugly. On Ithaca she touches Odysseus with her wand, withers his skin, takes away his fair hair, dims his eyes, and dresses him in rags, so that no one will know him (13.429–438). Odysseus explains the two powers to Telemachus: Athena makes him as she wishes, “now like a beggar, and now again / like a young man with fine clothes about his skin”, for it is easy for the gods “both to glorify a mortal man and to degrade him” (16.207–212).

**Hair.** The color of Odysseus' hair is a known problem. It is “fair” (ξανθάς) when Athena takes it away at 13.399 and 13.431, while at 16.176 his beard becomes “dark” (κυάνεαι). Ancient readers, Eustathius among them, took the hyacinth of 6.231 to mean dark hair. Merry and Riddell think rather that the comparison is to the clustering, curling petals of the flower, and not to its color (note on 6.231, p. 272).
