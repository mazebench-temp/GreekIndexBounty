φοῖνιξ is the date palm, *Phoenix dactylifera* (LSJ). The same word is “Phoenician”, the color “purple, crimson”, and the name of Achilles' old tutor (see [[purple]] and [[phoenix]]). LSJ explains the color as named from the Phoenicians, to whom the discovery of the dye was ascribed. It does not derive the tree's name, and the sources consulted here do not settle whether the tree was named from the people, from the color, or neither. In the text the tree is told apart from the man and the people only by context and by the capital letter of the names.

Homer names the palm only once. Odysseus tells Nausicaa that he once saw on Delos, beside the altar of Apollo, φοίνικος νέον ἔρνος ἀνερχόμενον, “a young sapling of a palm tree springing up”, and stood amazed at it for a long time (Odyssey 6.162–167; see [[the-palm-shoot-on-delos]], [[delos]] and [[altar]]). ἔρνος is a young shoot, the word Homer also uses of the young Achilles and of an olive sapling (Iliad 18.56, 17.53; LSJ). The palm of Odyssey 6 is young, slender and upright, and it grows in a sacred place.

## Outside Homer

**The palm of Leto.** In the *Homeric Hymn to Apollo*, Leto in labor on Delos “threw her arms around the palm” and Apollo was born (117). In Euripides the chorus of captive women imagines Delos, “where the first-born palm and the laurel raised their sacred branches for dear Leto” at the birth of the children of Zeus (*Hecuba* 458–461). The Odyssey scholia disagree whether Odysseus' palm is that tree. Mss. E.V. say that it is, and mss. B.P.Q. say that it is not (on 6.163, Dindorf p. 307; see [[the-palm-of-delos]]).

**A palm shown on Delos.** Later writers report that a palm was shown on Delos as old as the god. Pliny lists it among ancient trees still to be seen: “the palm of Delos from the time of that same god” (*Natural History* 16.240). Cicero has his brother Quintus say, with irony, that poetry makes things last longer than nature, “unless perhaps” the Delians “show today the same tall and slender palm that Homer's Ulysses said he saw at Delos” (*On the Laws* 1.2). Merry and Riddell suppose that a palm was always kept in the precinct, like the sacred olives of the Academy at Athens (note on 6.162).

**The victor's palm.** Pausanias says that at most games the prize was a crown of palm, and that at all games a palm was put in the victor's right hand. He gives the origin of the custom: Theseus, on his way home from Crete, held games for Apollo on Delos and crowned the winners with palm. He then recalls that Homer mentions the palm of Delos in Odysseus' supplication of the daughter of Alcinous (8.48.2–3). The Athenians dedicated a bronze palm with a gilded statue of Athena at Delphi after their victories at the Eurymedon (Pausanias 10.15.4).

**The tree in Greece and beyond.** Pausanias reports palms growing before the sanctuary at Aulis whose fruit was not wholly edible like the dates of Palestine, but riper than the dates of Ionia (9.19.8). Herodotus describes the date palms that cover the plain of Babylonia, and how the people tie the fruit of the palm that the Greeks call “male” onto the date-bearing trees (1.193).
