The ἠλακάτη is the distaff, the rod on which the cleaned wool is held while the spinner draws it out into thread (LSJ). The plural ἠλάκατα, which occurs only in the plural, is the wool on the distaff (LSJ) or the yarn spun off from it (Merry and Riddell on Odyssey 6.53). A scholion makes the same distinction: “ἠλάκατα is the wool, and the ἠλακάτη the wood on which the wool is wound” (B on 6.53, Dindorf p. 298). The verb is στρωφάω, “keep turning”, so ἠλάκατα στρωφᾶν, “to turn the wool”, means “to spin” (LSJ).

**The work of women.** “The loom and the distaff” (ἱστόν τʼ ἠλακάτην τε) is a formula for a woman's work. Hector sends Andromache back to the house with it: “go to the house and see to your own work, the loom and the distaff, and tell your handmaids to set about their work; war will be the care of men” (Iliad 6.490–492). Telemachus uses the same words to send Penelope to her rooms (Odyssey 1.356–358, 21.350–352). See [[loom]] and [[weaving]].

**Spinning queens.** In the Odyssey the mistress of a great house spins in the hall among her women. Helen received from Alcandre, the wife of Polybus of Egyptian Thebes, a golden distaff and a silver wool-basket on wheels with a rim of gold. Her maid sets it beside her full of finished yarn, with the distaff across it holding violet-dark wool (4.130–135). Arete spins sea-purple wool at the hearth (6.53, 6.306), Penelope sits by a pillar of the hall spinning fine wool (17.96–97), and the Phaeacian women “weave at the looms and turn the wool” (7.105). The disguised Odysseus tells Penelope's maids to go to their mistress and spin beside her (18.315–316).

**The golden distaff.** Artemis is χρυσηλάκατος (Iliad 16.183, 20.70; Odyssey 4.122). LSJ renders the epithet “with distaff of gold”, and rejects the scholia's explanation “with arrow of gold”. Later poets give it to Leto, Amphitrite, the Nereids and the Graces (Pindar, Bacchylides; cited by LSJ).
