ἐσχάρη (later ἐσχάρα) is the hearth, “fire-place, like ἑστία”, and the word is most frequent in the Odyssey (LSJ). A scholion defines it as “the place where the fire is burned” (V on Odyssey 6.52, Dindorf p. 298). Homer also uses the old case-form ἐσχαρόφιν, “at, from the hearth” (5.59, 7.169, 19.389). The hearth of a great house is in the hall, and the fire on it gives light as well as heat (6.305; compare 19.389, 20.123).

**The place of the mistress.** The mistress of the house sits at the hearth with her women. Arete sits there spinning in Book 6 (6.52–53, 6.305–307; see [[distaff]]). Eurycleia reminds Penelope that she would not believe her husband was home “by the hearth” (23.71).

**The place of the suppliant.** A stranger who asks for protection sits down at the hearth in the ashes. Odysseus does so in the house of Alcinous after he has clasped Arete's knees: “he sat down at the hearth in the ashes beside the fire” (7.153–154). The oldest Phaeacian tells the king that it is not seemly for a stranger to sit on the ground “at the hearth in the ashes” (7.159–160), and Alcinous raises him “from the hearth” and seats him on a shining chair (7.169–170). See [[supplication]].

**Sacrifice and oath.** At Eumaeus' hut the fat pig is set by the hearth to be killed (14.419–420). LSJ notes that ἐσχάρα later names a hearth-altar dug in the ground, distinct from the built [[altar]] (βωμός). The disguised Odysseus and the seer Theoclymenus swear by the hearth of the house: “let Zeus be my witness first of the gods, and the table of hospitality, and the hearth of blameless Odysseus, to which I have come” (14.158–159, 17.155–156, 20.230–231; with a different first line, 19.303–304). The oath uses the other word for hearth, ἱστίη (Attic ἑστία). Homer has no goddess of the hearth. In Hesiod, Hestia (Ἱστίη) is the first-named of the children of Cronus and Rhea (*Theogony* 453–454).

**Fires in the open.** In the Iliad the word names the fires of the Trojan camp at night, πυρὸς ἐσχάραι, “hearths of fire” (Iliad 10.418).
