ἔλαιον is formed from ἐλαία, “olive tree, olive”. In Homer it is “mostly anointing-oil, used after the bath” (LSJ). The verbs are χρίω, “anoint”, and ἀλείφω, “oil, anoint”, and its noun ἀλοιφή is “anything with which one can smear or anoint”, used both of an ointment (Odyssey 6.220, 18.179) and of the fat of a slaughtered pig (Iliad 9.208, 9.467). The anointing follows the bath in a fixed verse: “and when she had bathed him and anointed him richly with oil” (λοῦσέν τε καὶ ἔχρισεν λίπʼ ἐλαίῳ, Odyssey 3.466, 10.364, 10.450; compare Iliad 10.577). See [[bathing]].

The oil has its epithets: ὑγρόν, “liquid” (Iliad 23.281; Odyssey 6.79, 7.107), εὐῶδες, “fragrant” (Odyssey 2.339), and ῥοδόεις, “rose-scented” (Iliad 23.186). It is stored with the gold and the bronze in the storeroom of Odysseus' house (Odyssey 2.338–339). It also serves other ends than the bath. Aphrodite anoints the dead Hector with rose-scented oil so that Achilles cannot tear the body as he drags it (Iliad 23.186–187). Hera's oil is “ambrosial” and perfumed, and its fragrance reaches earth and heaven (Iliad 14.171–174). Patroclus often poured liquid oil on the manes of Achilles' horses after washing them (Iliad 23.281–282). The dancers on the shield of Achilles wear tunics that glisten softly with oil (Iliad 18.596), and the linen that the Phaeacian women weave is so close that liquid oil runs off it (Odyssey 7.107). The river that flows on top of the Peneius “like olive oil” is the subject of the simile [[like-olive-oil]] (Iliad 2.754).

λήκυθος, “oil-flask”, occurs in Homer only in Odyssey 6 (6.79, 6.215), of a flask of gold. In later Greek the word names a flask for oil and unguents, and lekythoi were also buried or burned with the dead (LSJ, citing Aristophanes, *Ecclesiazusae* 538, 996, 1032).
