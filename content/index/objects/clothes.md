εἷμα (“garment”, mostly plural εἵματα, “clothes”) and ἐσθής (“clothing”, a collective noun) both come from the verb ἕννυμι, “clothe, put on”. The named garments are the man's [[tunic]] (χιτών), worn next to the skin, and the [[cloak]] (φᾶρος or χλαῖνα) over it; the woman's [[robe]] (πέπλος, ἑανός); belts and sashes (ζῶστρα); and the [[veil]] (κρήδεμνον). The pair “a cloak and a tunic” is a formula for a full set of clothes: χλαῖνάν τε χιτῶνά τε εἵματα (Odyssey 10.542, 14.132, 14.320 and often), φᾶρός τε χιτῶνά τε εἵματα (6.214, 7.234). ῥάκος is a rag or a tattered garment. LSJ notes that the word never occurs in the Iliad. In the Odyssey it is the beggar's dress that Athena puts on Odysseus (13.434–435).

Clothes are made in the house by the women at the [[loom]], with wool that they spin on the [[distaff]], and they are kept in chests (ἐν χηλοῖσιν, 2.339; 13.10). They count as wealth beside bronze and gold: χαλκόν τε χρυσόν τε ἅλις ἐσθῆτά τε, “bronze and gold in plenty, and clothing” (5.38, 13.136). They are washed at fixed washing places. At Troy the wives and daughters of the Trojans washed their glossy clothes in stone basins by the springs of the Scamander, in the time of peace (Iliad 22.153–156).

Clothes belong to the welcome of a guest. A host bathes the stranger, anoints him and gives him clean clothes before the meal (see [[bathing]] and [[xenia]]). The question “who gave you these clothes?” therefore asks who the stranger's host was (7.238). A wanderer has lost his clothes with his home. Eumaeus says that any vagrant will make up a story “if someone gives you a cloak and a tunic” (14.132), and he promises the disguised Odysseus that Telemachus will give him “a cloak and a tunic” (14.516).
