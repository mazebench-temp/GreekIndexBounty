Homer has two words for the wagon. ἄμαξα (Attic ἅμαξα) is the common word, and it lived on in later Greek. ἀπήνη is the epic word, which later poets used for any carriage or chariot (LSJ). In Homer the two name one vehicle. The wagon that Priam's sons bring out is an ἄμαξα (Iliad 24.263, 24.266) and an ἀπήνη (24.275, 24.324). At Odyssey 6.72–73 the slaves make ready an ἄμαξα and yoke the mules to the ἀπήνη in the next line. LSJ explains ἄμαξα in its first sense as the frame or chassis of the four-wheeled wagon, and ἀπήνη as “much the same as ἄμαξα”. Its origin is unknown. Lobeck listed many similar words for a cart in Greek and other languages, and could not say whether they share a stem (quoted by Merry and Riddell on Odyssey 6.57). For ἄμαξα the old derivations are ἅμα + ἄγω, or ἀμφί + ἄξων, “with two axles” (Merry and Riddell on 6.37).

## The vehicle

The wagon has four wheels: Priam's is τετράκυκλος, “four-wheeled” (Iliad 24.324). Merry and Riddell contrast it with the two-wheeled [[chariot]], ἅρμα (note on 6.37). Its epithets describe its build. It is “high” (ὑψηλή), “with good wheels” (ἐΰκυκλος, ἐΰτροχος), “polished” (ἐΰξεστος) and “newly built” (πρωτοπαγής, Iliad 24.267). A body could be fixed on it: Priam's sons tie a wicker basket (πείρινς) onto the wagon (24.267), and the carriage of Odyssey 6 is “fitted with an upper frame” (ὑπερτερίῃ ἀραρυῖαν, 6.70; see the note). It is drawn by mules, hence the adjective ἡμιονείη, “mule-” (6.72; Iliad 24.266), or by oxen (Iliad 24.782). The yoke and its fastenings are described in detail only for Priam's wagon (24.266–274; see [[mules]]).

The wagon carries what is heavy or many. After the truce the Trojans lift their dead “onto wagons” (Iliad 7.426). A stone is too heavy for two men of the present day to heave “onto a wagon” (12.448). The doorstone of the Cyclops is so large that “twenty-two good four-wheeled wagons” could not move it (Odyssey 9.241–242). In the land of the Laestrygonians a smooth road brings wood to the city on wagons (10.103–104). In the Iliad's last book the wagon carries Priam's ransom to Achilles and brings Hector's body back to Troy (24.275–276, 24.578–590, 24.710–718).

“The Wagon” is also the name of the Great Bear: Ἄρκτον θʼ, ἣν καὶ Ἄμαξαν ἐπίκλησιν καλέουσιν, “and the Bear, which they also call the Wagon” (Iliad 18.487 = Odyssey 5.273).

## Outside Homer

The mule wagon became a racing vehicle. Pausanias reports that a race for mule wagons was added to the Olympic games at the seventieth Olympiad and dropped at the eighty-fourth (5.9.1–2). LSJ also cites Pindar for the mule-drawn racing ἀπήνη (*Pythian* 4.94, *Olympian* 5.3).
