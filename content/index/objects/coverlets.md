ῥῆγος is a rug or blanket, in Homer mostly plural, ῥήγεα (LSJ). Its epithets are καλά, “fine”, πορφύρεα, “purple”, and σιγαλόεντα, “glossy” (see [[glossy]] and [[purple]]). LSJ infers that the ῥῆγος was of wool, because Homer names it beside linen as a different cloth (Odyssey 13.73, 13.118; compare Iliad 9.661). Eustathius derived the word from a verb ῥῆξαι, “to dye” (reported by LSJ).

Coverlets belong to the bed made for a guest. The order to the maids is given in fixed verses: “to set beds under the portico, and to throw fine purple coverlets on them, and spread rugs over them, and put woolen cloaks on top to wear” (Odyssey 4.297–299, 7.336–338; Iliad 24.644–646). For Phoenix in Achilles' hut the women spread fleeces, a coverlet and fine linen (Iliad 9.660–661), and on Circe's island a maid throws fine coverlets over the chairs (Odyssey 10.352–353). On the Phaeacian ship that takes Odysseus home the crew spread a coverlet and linen for him to sleep on, and they lift him ashore asleep, “with the linen and the glossy coverlet” (13.73, 13.118).

To have coverlets is a sign of standing. Nestor will not let Telemachus sleep on his ship, as if his house were that of a man without clothes and poor, with no cloaks and coverlets (Odyssey 3.346–351). The ghost of Odysseus' mother tells him that Laertes has “no bed, cloaks or glossy coverlets”, and sleeps in the ashes by the fire in winter (11.188–191).
