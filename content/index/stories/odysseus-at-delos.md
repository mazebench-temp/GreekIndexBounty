## The story

Homer gives the story in four verses of Odysseus' speech to Nausicaa:

{{quote:f0f2c797-b271-5366-b6d5-7f16bb64b1ea}}

That is all. Odysseus says that he came to Delos, that a great army (λαός) followed him, and that the journey was the one “on which evil sorrows were to come upon me”. He saw a young palm shoot (φοίνικος νέον ἔρνος) springing up beside the altar of Apollo, and stood amazed at it for a long time, “since never yet had such a stem come up out of the earth” (see [[delos]], [[palm-tree]] and [[apollo]]). He does not say when the visit was or why he went.

The ancient commentators filled the gap. A scholion takes the “great army” as the Greek expedition against Troy, and says that Menelaus came to Delos with Odysseus for the daughters of Anius, who were called the Oinotropoi; it adds that the story is also in Simonides' *Kateuchai* (schol. E on 6.164, Dindorf p. 308). Merry and Riddell place the visit on the voyage from Aulis to Troy, through the Cyclades and Delos (on 6.162, p. 266; see [[aulis]]). Both are inferences from the words “a great army” and “evil sorrows”; Homer names neither Troy nor the occasion.

## In the poem

**Why it is told.** The memory serves the compliment. Odysseus has just said that he has never seen such a mortal; the palm on Delos is the one sight he can compare with her, and “in just the same way” as he wondered at it, he admires the girl and is afraid to touch her knees (6.160–169; see [[the-palm-shoot-on-delos]] and [[god-or-mortal]]). Nausicaa has just been likened to Artemis (6.102–109, 6.151–152), and the palm stands by the altar of her brother Apollo.

**What it reveals.** The story is the only piece of his past that the nameless stranger gives Nausicaa before he mentions Ogygia (6.170–172). A scholion reads it as a calculated self-presentation: he shows himself as one of the notable men, so that he does not seem to be a trader or an oarsman (schol. E.P.Q.V. on 6.164, Dindorf p. 308). A leader followed by an army, on a journey of “evil sorrows”, is a hint of who he is, without his name (see [[odysseus]]).

## Outside Homer

The palm on Delos had a life of its own. The *Homeric Hymn to Apollo* tells that Leto, giving birth on Delos, threw her arms around a palm (117), and the scholia on 6.163 disagree on whether Odysseus means that palm (Dindorf p. 307). Cicero has his brother Quintus say, with irony, that the Delians show “today” the same tall and slender palm that Homer's Ulysses said he saw (*De Legibus* 1.2), and Pliny says that a palm from the time of Apollo was still to be seen on Delos (*Natural History* 16.240; see [[the-palm-of-delos]]).
