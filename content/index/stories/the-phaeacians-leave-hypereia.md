## The story

The narrator tells the story in ten verses, as Athena goes to the Phaeacian city:

{{quote:b880326a-5152-50b6-8d7d-a0dcfcc4af72}}

1. **Hypereia.** The Phaeacians “once in former days” lived in Hypereia “with its wide dancing floors” (6.4; see [[hypereia-of-the-phaeacians]] and [[with-wide-dancing-floors]]).
2. **The Cyclopes.** Their neighbors were the Cyclopes, “overweening men”, who kept doing them harm and were stronger (6.5–6; see [[cyclopes]] and [[overweening]]). The iterative verb σινέσκοντο says that the harm was repeated.
3. **The departure.** “From there godlike Nausithous made them rise up and led them away” (6.7; see [[nausithous]]). A scholion explains ἀναστήσας as “having sent out a colony” (ἀποικίαν στειλάμενος, schol. H on 6.7, Dindorf p. 294).
4. **The new city.** He settled them in Scheria, “far from grain-eating men” (6.8; see [[scheria]] and [[alphestes]]). He drove a wall around the city, built houses, made temples of the gods and divided the plowlands (6.9–10; see [[polis]] and [[temple]]).
5. **The succession.** Nausithous died and went to Hades, and his son Alcinous ruled “then” (6.11–12; see [[alcinous]]).

The acts of Nausithous are the acts of a founder: to lead the people out, to build a wall, houses and temples, and to divide the land. Homer does not say how long ago this happened. Alcinous is the founder's son, so the migration is one generation before the time of the poem.

## In the poem

**Where it is told.** The story stands at the start of the Phaeacian books, when the narrator first brings the reader to the Phaeacian city. It explains who the Phaeacians are before Odysseus meets any of them. Nausicaa's later words depend on it. The Phaeacians live “far apart in the much-surging sea, / the farthest away, and no other mortal mingles with us” (6.204–205). Nausicaa's directions describe the city that Nausithous founded: a high rampart, a harbor on either side, and the house of Alcinous (6.262–263, 6.299–303).

**The later books.** Book 7 adds the founder's descent. Athena tells Odysseus that Nausithous was the son of Poseidon and Periboea, the daughter of Eurymedon, king of the Giants. Nausithous ruled over the Phaeacians and was the father of Rhexenor and Alcinous (7.56–63). Alcinous says that the gods appear openly to the Phaeacians, “since we are near to them, as are the Cyclopes and the wild tribes of the Giants” (7.201–206). Book 8 adds the founder's prophecy: Poseidon would one day shatter a Phaeacian ship returning from an escort and cover their city with a great mountain (8.564–571). Book 13 fulfills the first half of it (13.125–187; see [[the-wrath-of-poseidon]]).

**The Cyclopes.** The people who drove the Phaeacians out are the people whom Odysseus will describe in Book 9. There the Cyclopes have no assemblies or laws, do not plant or plow, and have no ships (9.106–115, 9.125–129). The Phaeacians whom they once harmed are a people of ships and of a walled city (6.262–272). The poem does not draw the contrast in words. It is an inference from the two descriptions.

## Outside Homer

**Corcyra.** Many ancient readers identified Scheria with Corcyra. A scholion on 6.3 calls the Phaeacian land “the island now called Corcyra” (schol. E, Dindorf p. 294). Thucydides says that the Corcyraeans prided themselves on “the earlier settlement of Corcyra by the Phaeacians” (1.25.4), and Strabo calls the island “now called Corcyra, formerly Scheria” (6.2.4). Other ancient critics rejected the identification. A scholion in the Aristarchean form notes that the Phaeacian land “was named Scheria, not Corcyra”, and that it lies outside the inhabited world (schol. E.P.Q. on 6.8, Dindorf p. 294). See [[scheria]].

**Another origin.** Apollonius of Rhodes gives the Phaeacians a different origin. Their island Drepane is named after the sickle that lies under it, and the Phaeacians themselves are “of the race of the blood of Ouranos” (*Argonautica* 4.982–992). Apollonius says nothing of a migration from Hypereia in these lines.
