## The story

**The cause.** Zeus explains it at the poem's first council: Poseidon “is angry without ceasing” because of the Cyclops whom Odysseus blinded, Polyphemus, his son by the nymph Thoosa. Since then he does not kill Odysseus, but drives him wandering away from his country (1.68–75). The first statement of the anger comes even earlier: all the gods pitied Odysseus “except Poseidon, who raged furiously / against godlike Odysseus until he reached his own land” (1.19–21).

**The curse.** Odysseus tells the story in Book 9. When he shouts his name from the ship, the blinded Cyclops prays to his father: may Odysseus never come home, or, if it is his fate “to see his dear ones and to reach his well-built house and his native land”, may he come late and in misery, having lost all his companions, on a foreign ship, and find troubles in his house. Poseidon hears him (9.526–536). Teiresias confirms the anger in the underworld, because Odysseus blinded the god's son (11.100–103), and tells him how to end it: after killing the suitors he must carry an oar inland to men who do not know the sea and sacrifice there to Poseidon (11.119–131).

**The storm.** Coming back from the Ethiopians, Poseidon sees Odysseus on his raft near the land of the Phaeacians, “where it is his lot to escape the great noose of misery”, and raises a storm: “I think I can still drive him to his fill of evil” (5.282–290). He leaves him swimming and says, “Wander over the sea in this way, suffering many evils, / until you mingle with men nurtured by Zeus” (5.375–381). The sea goddess Ino asks why Poseidon has become so furiously angry with him (5.339–341).

**The Phaeacians pay.** The Phaeacians are Poseidon's own kin (7.56–62, 13.130), and their founder [[nausithous|Nausithous]] used to say that the god resented their safe escorts and would one day shatter one of their ships on its way home and cover their city with a mountain (8.564–571). After they bring Odysseus to Ithaca asleep, Poseidon complains to Zeus that mortals no longer honor him. With Zeus' consent he turns the returning ship to stone in sight of the city. Alcinous recognizes the prophecy, stops the escorts, and has twelve bulls sacrificed to Poseidon “in case he takes pity”, and the poem leaves the Phaeacians praying at the altar (13.125–187).

**The end.** Athena tells Odysseus on Ithaca why she did not help him openly at sea: she did not want to fight her father's brother Poseidon, who was angry because Odysseus blinded his son (13.339–343). Teiresias' instructions point beyond the poem. Odysseus repeats them to Penelope: the oar carried inland, the sacrifice to Poseidon, and a gentle death “from the sea” in old age (23.264–284).

## In the poem

**The limit set by the anger.** “Until he reached his own land” (πάρος ἣν γαῖαν ἱκέσθαι) gives the anger an end point. The Phaeacians will bring him to that land, and Zeus has already said that it is his fate to reach it (5.41–42). The words of the Cyclops' curse, “to see his dear ones and to reach his well-built house and his native land” (9.532–533), are nearly the words of Zeus' decree (5.41–42) and of Nausicaa's promise to Odysseus (6.314–315).

**An open end.** The poem does not say whether Poseidon buries the Phaeacian city under the mountain, nor does it narrate the journey inland with the oar. I. de Jong's narratological commentary treats such unfinished threads as “open ends”, and her reviewer counts the Phaeacians' danger among them (BMCR 2002.06.12).
