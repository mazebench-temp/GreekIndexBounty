## The story

**The dream.** While Odysseus sleeps in his bed of leaves on Scheria, Athena goes to the house of Alcinous, king of the Phaeacians, “planning a homecoming for great-hearted Odysseus” (6.1–14). She comes to his daughter's bed in the likeness of a friend, the daughter of Dymas, and scolds her for her neglected clothes: her marriage is near, the best of the Phaeacians are courting her, and she must ask her father at dawn for the mules and the wagon and go to wash them (6.15–40; see [[daughter-of-dymas]] and [[dream-scene]]).

**The wagon.** At dawn Nausicaa asks her father for the wagon. She feels shame to name her marriage, but “he understood everything” and grants it; her mother packs food, wine and a golden flask of oil, and Nausicaa drives off with her handmaids (6.48–84; see [[alcinous]], [[queen-arete]] and [[wagon]]).

**The river.** At the washing pools the girls tread the clothes, spread them on the shore, bathe, eat, and play at ball. Nausicaa leads them, and stands out among them as Artemis does among her nymphs (6.85–109; see [[washing-clothes]], [[ball-play]] and [[artemis-among-her-nymphs]]). As they are about to leave, Athena contrives the next step: the princess throws the ball, misses, the girls shout, and Odysseus wakes (6.110–117).

**The stranger.** He wonders what people live there, breaks off a leafy branch to cover himself, and comes out “like a mountain-bred lion” among the girls. Terrible with the brine, he puts the handmaids to flight; only Nausicaa stands her ground, because Athena puts daring in her heart (6.117–141; see [[the-lion-and-the-girls]]). He decides not to clasp her knees but to plead from a distance, and makes a speech of praise and request: goddess or mortal, blessed parents and blessed husband, the palm shoot on Delos, twenty days at sea, a plea for a rag and the way to town, and a wish for her marriage (6.141–185; see [[god-or-mortal]], [[the-palm-shoot-on-delos]] and [[homophrosyne]]).

**The reception.** She answers that Zeus deals out fortune to good and bad, promises clothing and all that a suppliant needs, and names her people and her father (6.186–197). She calls back her handmaids and tells them that strangers and beggars come from Zeus (6.198–210). They give him clothes and oil; he bathes alone, out of shame, and Athena makes him taller and pours grace over him. Nausicaa, gazing, tells her handmaids she wishes such a man could be her husband, and they give him food and drink (6.211–250; see [[divine-beautification]] and [[bathing]]).

**The way to the city.** Nausicaa yokes the mules and gives him his instructions. He is to walk with the handmaids behind the wagon as far as the city's edge, wait in the grove of Athena so that the townsmen will not gossip about the princess and the handsome stranger, then go to her father's house, pass the king on his throne, and clasp the knees of her mother (6.251–315; see [[tis-speech]] and [[grove-of-athena]]). They reach the grove at sunset. Odysseus prays to Athena that he may come to the Phaeacians dear and pitied; she hears him, but does not yet appear to him, out of respect for Poseidon (6.316–331; see [[dear-and-pitied]] and [[the-wrath-of-poseidon]]).

## In the poem

**The plan of the gods.** The meeting carries out what Zeus decreed in Book 5: Odysseus will reach Scheria on the twentieth day, and the Phaeacians will honor him like a god and send him home with gifts, “for so it is his fate to see his dear ones and to reach his high-roofed house and his native land” (5.31–42). Odysseus tells Nausicaa that he escaped the sea “yesterday, on the twentieth day” (6.170), and Nausicaa's last words promise him the hope “to see your dear ones and to reach your well-built house and your native land” if her mother is kind (6.313–315 ≈ 5.41–42). Athena runs the episode. She plans the homecoming (6.14), sends the dream (6.20–40), wakes Odysseus by the lost ball (6.112–117), gives Nausicaa courage (6.139–140), and beautifies him (6.229–235; see [[athena]]).

**From the wanderings to the human world.** Book 6 is the first time the poem shows Odysseus among human beings: in Book 5 he is with the nymph Calypso and then alone at sea. His question on waking, whether the people are savage or kind to strangers and god-fearing, is the question of the whole voyage (6.119–121; see [[to-the-land-of-what-mortals]]). Here it is answered by a girl, in terms of [[xenia]]: “from Zeus are all strangers and beggars” (6.207–208; see [[strangers-and-beggars-are-from-zeus]]).

**Supplication by words.** The scene is a [[supplication]] without the gesture. Odysseus weighs clasping her knees and chooses “soothing words” at a distance, “lest the girl be angry in her mind” (6.141–147; see [[supplication-type-scene]], [[decision-scene]] and [[gounata]]). The full ritual is kept for the queen in Book 7, where he clasps Arete's knees as Nausicaa told him to (7.139–154).

**Marriage in the air.** The dream speaks of Nausicaa's wedding, the request to her father avoids it, Odysseus' speech praises the man who will marry her and wishes her a husband and like-mindedness, and Nausicaa tells her handmaids that she would like a husband like the stranger (6.27–35, 6.66, 6.158–159, 6.180–185, 6.244–245; see [[marriage]] and [[posis]]). The imagined gossip of the townsmen raises the same idea and rejects it for her (6.276–288). In Book 7 Alcinous wishes that such a man would marry his daughter and stay (7.311–316). The poem leaves the possibility open and then closes it: Odysseus goes home to Penelope.

**Recalled later.** Odysseus tells the story to Arete and Alcinous, and praises the girl's good sense (7.290–297). When Alcinous blames her for not bringing him home, Odysseus says that it was his own choice (7.298–307). At the feast in Book 8 Nausicaa, standing by the doorpost of the hall, asks him to remember her in his own country, “because you owe the price of your life to me first”, and he promises to pray to her as to a goddess, “for you gave me my life, girl” (8.457–468). When Odysseus tells Penelope his story in Book 23, he names the Phaeacians and their honors but not Nausicaa (23.338–341).

## Outside Homer

**On the stage.** Sophocles wrote a play called *Nausicaa*, and Athenaeus reports that he played ball with great skill when it was produced (*Deipnosophistae* 1.20e–f; see [[sophocles-nausicaa]] and [[sophocles]]).

**In art.** Polygnotus painted Odysseus coming upon the girls washing clothes with Nausicaa, “just as Homer describes it”, in a building beside the Propylaea at Athens, and on the chest of Cypselus at Olympia a girl driving a mule cart beside a veiled companion was taken to be Nausicaa and her handmaid (Pausanias 1.22.6, 5.19.9; see [[nausicaa-in-greek-art]] and [[polygnotus]]).

**Latin poetry.** Virgil gives Odysseus' words to Aeneas, who asks his disguised mother Venus whether she is a goddess, perhaps the sister of Phoebus (*Aeneid* 1.327–329), and he gives the Artemis simile to Dido (1.498–502; see [[virgil]]). Ovid's Salmacis greets Hermaphroditus with the same sequence of god or mortal, blessed parents and blessed bride (*Metamorphoses* 4.320–326; see [[ovid]]).

**Modern readers.** Samuel Butler argued that the Odyssey was written by a young woman who drew herself as Nausicaa (*The Authoress of the Odyssey*, 1897; see [[butler-authoress-of-the-odyssey]]).
