## Meaning

παρθένος is “maiden, girl” (LSJ). LSJ gives no derivation. Homer uses it of a girl who is grown but not yet married, and pairs it with ἠΐθεος, the unmarried young man. On Achilles' shield “young men and maidens” (ἠΐθεοι καὶ παρθένοι) dance together (*Iliad* 18.593). Hector, deciding whether to face Achilles, says that this is no time to talk with him “from oak or rock, as a maiden and a young man talk together” (ὀαρίζετον, *Iliad* 22.126–128).

The word names a status more than a body. Astyoche bore Ares two sons in her father's house as a παρθένος αἰδοίη, “a modest maiden”, who went up to the upper room where the god lay with her in secret (*Iliad* 2.513–515). LSJ lists this passage under “unmarried women who are not virgins”. A child born to such a girl is παρθένιος, “son of a maiden”, like Eudorus, whom Polymele bore to Hermes (*Iliad* 16.179–181). Diomedes insults Paris as a παρθενοπίπης, a “girl-ogler” (*Iliad* 11.385).

The diminutive form παρθενική is used in the same way: the girls and young men who carry the grapes on the shield (*Iliad* 18.567), the shades of young girls in the underworld (*Odyssey* 11.39), and Athena disguised as “a young maiden carrying a pitcher” (*Odyssey* 7.20). The “maiden girdle”, παρθενίη ζώνη, is what Poseidon loosed when he lay with Tyro (*Odyssey* 11.245).

The commoner word for a girl is κούρη, which is also “daughter”. The translation of Odyssey 6 keeps “girl” and “daughter” for κούρη and “maiden” for παρθένος. παρθένος stresses the coming change: the maiden will become a wife, a γυνή. Twice it is joined with ἀδμής, “unwed”, literally “untamed”, the word for an animal not yet broken to the yoke (*Odyssey* 4.637; see [[unbroken]]). The Odyssey uses the phrase παρθένος ἀδμής only of Nausicaa.

## Outside Homer

In later Greek παρθένος is also “virgin” in the strict sense, set against γυνή (Sophocles, *Trachiniae* 148). Παρθένος, “the Maiden”, becomes a title of goddesses. It is the title of Athena at Athens (Pausanias 5.11.10, 10.34.8), and Euripides uses it of Artemis (*Hippolytus* 17). These references are given as LSJ cites them.
