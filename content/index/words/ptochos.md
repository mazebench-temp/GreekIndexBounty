## Meaning

LSJ glosses πτωχός “beggar” and cites 6.208 first. The verb πτωχεύω is “go begging”. LSJ connects the word with πτώσσω, “cower, shrink”, which in the Odyssey also has the sense “cringe like a beggar, go begging”: Melanthius says that the beggar will not work but wants to go “cringing through the people” (πτώσσων κατὰ δῆμον) to feed his belly (17.227–228).

The noun and the verb occur on 27 lines of Homer, all in the Odyssey. The first is in Book 6. All the others are in Books 14–24, where Odysseus is in disguise:

- **The pair stranger and beggar.** Nausicaa says that “strangers and beggars are all from Zeus” (ξεῖνοί τε πτωχοί τε, 6.207–208). Eumaeus repeats the verse to the disguised Odysseus (14.57–58). The suitors call Odysseus “a stranger and a beggar” in the scene of the bow (21.292).
- **The disguise.** A formula describes it: “like a wretched beggar and an old man” (πτωχῷ λευγαλέῳ ἐναλίγκιον ἠδὲ γέροντι). Odysseus uses it of his plan (16.273), the narrator uses it as he walks to the city (17.202) and as he enters his hall (17.337), and the shade of Amphimedon uses it in his story (24.157).
- **Begging in the hall.** Odysseus begs from each suitor “as if he had long been a beggar” (17.366). Antinous calls beggars “painful, the spoilers of feasts” (17.377).
- **The gods of beggars.** Struck by Antinous, Odysseus prays: “if there are gods and Furies of beggars” (17.475).
- **Irus.** A rival appears, “a public beggar, who begged through the town of Ithaca and was known for his greedy belly” (18.1–2). The two beggars fight for the right to the door (18.1–107; see [[gaster]]).
- **The beggar's defense.** To Melantho, Odysseus says that he begs because “need presses”, and that he too was once a rich man who gave to wanderers (19.71–80).

The beggar of the Odyssey is defined by his belly (see [[gaster]]) and by his wandering. Homer pairs πτωχός with words for wandering: πτωχοὶ καὶ ἀλήμονες ἄνδρες, “beggars and wandering men” (19.74), and πτωχὸς ἀνὴρ ἀλαλήμενος, “a beggar man who has wandered here” (21.327).

## Related words

- [[xenia|ξεῖνος]] (“stranger, guest”): the beggar is a stranger who has nothing to give back. The host's duty covers both (6.207–208).
- [[gaster|γαστήρ]] (“belly”): the beggar's need and his shame.
- [[supplication]]: the beggar asks, as a suppliant does, and relies on the same god.
