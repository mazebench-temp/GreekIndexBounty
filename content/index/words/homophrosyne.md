## Meaning

ὁμοφροσύνη is made of ὁμο- (“same”) and φρον- (“mind”, as in [[phrenes|φρένες]] and φρονέω “think”). LSJ glosses it “unity of mind and feeling”, and the verb ὁμοφρονέω “be of the same mind, have the same thoughts”. The adjective ὁμόφρων is “agreeing, united”. The translation keeps one English stem for the Greek stem: “like-mindedness”, “like-minded”.

The noun occurs twice in Homer and the verb twice, all in the Odyssey:

- **6.181**: Odysseus prays that the gods may give Nausicaa “a husband and a home, and … like-mindedness that is good” (see the note on Book 6).
- **6.183**: the verb, of “two who are like-minded in their thoughts” as they keep a house, “a man and a woman”.
- **9.456**: the blinded Cyclops says to his ram: “if only you were like-minded with me and could speak” (εἰ δὴ ὁμοφρονέοις ποτιφωνήεις τε γένοιο), to tell him where Nobody is hiding.
- **15.198**: in the plural, of friends. Telemachus asks Nestor's son Peisistratus to leave him at his ship. They are guest-friends from their fathers' time and of one age, and “this journey will set us even more in like-mindedness” (ὁμοφροσύνῃσιν ἐνήσει, 15.196–198).

The adjective occurs once, in the Iliad, in a negative. Achilles tells Hector that there are no oaths between lions and men, and that “wolves and lambs do not have a like-minded heart” (οὐδὲ λύκοι τε καὶ ἄρνες ὁμόφρονα θυμὸν ἔχουσιν, *Iliad* 22.262–263).

So the word covers three bonds: wife and husband, friend and friend, and, in the Cyclops' wish, a master and his ram. In each, two parties think the same thing. The poem does not use the word of Odysseus and Penelope. Their reunion turns on a thing that only the two of them know, how Odysseus built their bed (23.173–206).

## Related words

- [[phrenes|φρένες]] (“mind”) and [[noos|νόος]] (“mind, thought”): ὁμοφρονέοντε νοήμασιν, “like-minded in their thoughts” (6.183), joins both stems.
- [[philotes|φιλότης]] (“friendship, love”): the scholia gloss ὁμοφροσύνη as “genuine friendship” (6.181).
- See [[husbands-and-wives]] and [[marriage]].

## Outside Homer

LSJ cites later uses of ὁμοφροσύνη in Ionic and later prose, among them Democritus (186 in LSJ's numbering), Dionysius of Halicarnassus (*Roman Antiquities* 9.45) and Ocellus Lucanus (4.6), and in Apollonius' *Argonautica* (2.716). Merry and Riddell, on 6.182, cite a modern editor (Lowe) who compared the Nurse in Euripides' *Medea*: the greatest safety comes “when a woman does not stand apart from her husband” (*Medea* 14–15).
