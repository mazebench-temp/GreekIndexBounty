## Meaning

LSJ glosses ὄλβος “happiness, bliss, esp. worldly happiness, weal”, and ὄλβιος “happy, blest, esp. with reference to worldly goods”. In Homer ὄλβος is a gift of the gods. They “spin” it for a man (ἐπικλώθω), “deal it out” (νέμω), “give” it, and “take it away”. The origin of the word is not known. LSJ suggests, with “perhaps”, a link with Lithuanian *algà*, “wage”.

The noun occurs in Homer as follows:

- **Spun by the gods.** Telemachus at Pylos: “the gods have not spun such prosperity for me and my father; but now I must endure all the same” (3.208–209). Menelaus: a man's son is easy to know when Zeus “spins prosperity for him at his marriage and his birth” (4.207–208).
- **Dealt out by Zeus.** Nausicaa: “Zeus the Olympian himself deals out prosperity to men” (6.188; see the note on Book 6).
- **With wealth.** ὄλβῳ τε πλούτῳ τε, “in prosperity and wealth”, is a formula. Bathycles, whom Glaucus killed, stood out among the Myrmidons by it (*Iliad* 16.596). Peleus surpassed all men by it (*Iliad* 24.536). Odysseus' Cretan stranger says the same of his own father, Castor (14.206).
- **Taken away.** Penelope speaks of herself as one “whose prosperity Zeus took away” (18.273).
- **Wished for a beggar.** To Irus, Odysseus says: “you seem to me a wanderer, as I am, and the gods are going to grant prosperity” (18.18–19). The suitor Amphinomus greets the beggar Odysseus: “may prosperity come to you hereafter; but now you are held by many evils” (18.122–123). The herdsman Philoetius uses the same two verses (20.199–200).

The adjective ὄλβιος occurs once in the Iliad, of Priam in former days (*Iliad* 24.543; LSJ: “nowhere else in Il.”). Priam calls Agamemnon ὀλβιόδαιμον, “blessed by a god” (*Iliad* 3.182). In the Odyssey it is frequent. The neuter plural ὄλβια is “rich gifts” or “happily” in a wish: θεοὶ δέ τοι ὄλβια δοῖεν, “may the gods give you blessings” (8.413, 24.402; also 7.148, 13.42). Odysseus in his beggar's tales says he was once ὄλβιος (17.420 = 19.76; 18.138). The shade of Agamemnon greets the dead Achilles, and later the absent Odysseus, with ὄλβιε, “blessed one” (24.36, 24.192).
