## Meaning

φῆμις is a poetic form of φήμη, from the root of φημί (“say”). LSJ glosses it “speech, talk”, and more exactly “common opinion or judgement expressed in talk”, and so “reputation”. φάτις, from the same root, is “voice or saying among men, common talk, rumour” (LSJ). In Homer both words name the talk of a community about one of its members. The talk is usually feared.

The occurrences in Homer:

- ***Iliad* 10.207**: Nestor hopes that a spy may “hear some talk among the Trojans” (φῆμιν). Here the word is plain report.
- **6.29**: in Nausicaa's dream, a “good report” (φάτις … ἐσθλή) goes up among men from fine wedding clothes (see the note on Book 6).
- **6.273**: Nausicaa shuns the “unkind talk” (φῆμιν ἀδευκέα) of the Phaeacians.
- **14.239**: in his false tale to Eumaeus, Odysseus says that he could not refuse to lead ships to Troy: “the harsh talk of the people held me” (χαλεπὴ δʼ ἔχε δήμου φῆμις).
- **15.468**: the diners go “to the session and the talk of the people” (ἐς θῶκον … δήμοιό τε φῆμιν). LSJ takes φῆμις here as “the place of talk”.
- **16.75 = 19.527**: Penelope stays in the house, “respecting the bed of her husband and the talk of the people” (εὐνήν τʼ αἰδομένη πόσιος δήμοιό τε φῆμιν). The talk is an object of [[aidos|αἰδώς]].
- **21.323**: the suitors fear “the talk of men and women” (φάτιν ἀνδρῶν ἠδὲ γυναικῶν) if a beggar strings the bow. The next line imagines what “some baser man” would say (21.324).
- **23.362**: Odysseus warns Penelope that “the report of the suitors” he has killed will go out at sunrise (φάτις).
- **24.201**: the shade of Agamemnon says that Clytemnestra's deed will be a hateful song among men and will “give harsh repute to women” (χαλεπὴν δέ τε φῆμιν ὀπάσσει), even to a good one.

φάτιν also stands in *Iliad* 9.460, one of four lines that the text prints in brackets (9.458–461). There Phoenix says that a god put “the talk of the people” in his heart, so that he did not kill his father.

The noun φήμη is different in Homer. It is an ominous word, a chance remark that a listener takes as a sign from the gods (2.35; 20.100, 20.105).

The verb μωμεύω, “blame, find fault with”, occurs once in Homer (6.274). LSJ makes it the same as μωμάομαι, which the Iliad uses once: Helen will not go to Paris' bed, because “the Trojan women will all blame me hereafter” (μωμήσονται, *Iliad* 3.411–412). Hesiod has μωμεύω in *Works and Days* 756. The noun μῶμος, “blame”, is in Antinous' reply to Telemachus (2.86).

## Related words

- [[kleos|κλέος]] (“fame”): the report that goes out about a man, above all after his death. φῆμις is the talk of the people at home, about the living.
- [[oneidos|ὄνειδος]] (“reproach”): what the talk says. Nausicaa fears that the gossip “would become reproaches against me” (6.285).
- [[nemesis|νέμεσις]] (“indignation”) and [[aidos|αἰδώς]] (“shame”): the feeling that the talk voices, and the feeling that makes a person fear it.
- [[demos|δῆμος]] (“people”): the talk is δήμου φῆμις, “the talk of the people” (14.239; 16.75).
- [[praise-and-blame]]: the theme of good report and reproach.
