## Meaning

LSJ gives three senses of γαστήρ that occur in Homer:

- **The belly as a part of the body.** In the Iliad it is mostly the place of a wound: a spear is fixed “in the middle of the belly” (*Iliad* 13.372, 13.398, 13.506; also 4.531, 5.539). It is also the womb (*Iliad* 6.58).
- **The belly as craving food.** LSJ cites 6.133 first for this sense. It is the sense of the Odyssey.
- **A stuffed paunch**, a kind of sausage. The suitors set out goats' paunches filled with fat and blood as a prize for the beggars' fight (18.44–45, 18.118), and Odysseus tossing in his bed is compared to a man turning such a paunch over a fire (20.25–28).

The noun γάστρη, the “belly” of a tripod (8.437), is a different word.

## The belly in the Odyssey

The hungry belly is a theme of the poem. It is named as a force that “bids” and “urges” a man, and it is called “accursed” (οὐλομένη) and “hateful” (στυγερή). Most of the passages are spoken by Odysseus:

- **The lion** (6.133): the belly “bids” the hungry lion go even into the fold (see the note on Book 6).
- **At the table of Alcinous** (7.215–221): Odysseus asks to be allowed to eat, “for there is nothing more shameless than the hateful belly”, which “bids” a grieving man eat and drink and makes him forget what he has suffered.
- **To Eumaeus** (15.343–345): “for the sake of the accursed belly men have evil cares”, when wandering and pain come to them.
- **At the door of his house** (17.286–289): “there is no way to hide a belly that is eager, accursed, which gives many evils to men”. For its sake ships are fitted out against enemies.
- **The beggar's blow** (17.473–474): Antinous struck him “because of the wretched belly, the accursed one, which gives many evils to men”.
- **Before the fight with Irus** (18.53–54): “but the belly, the evil-doer, urges me on” to fight a younger man.

Others use the word to scorn the beggar. Irus “was known for his greedy belly” (γαστέρι μάργῃ, 18.2). Melanthius says that the beggar wants to feed “his insatiable belly” (γαστέρʼ ἄναλτον, 17.228), and Eurymachus says the same (18.364). Odysseus answers that Eurymachus would not reproach him with his belly if he saw him at work in the fields or fighting in the front rank (18.366–380).

In the Iliad the belly comes up once in this sense, and Odysseus speaks there too. When Achilles wants the army to fight without eating, Odysseus answers that “it is not possible for the Achaeans to mourn a dead man with the belly”, that is, by fasting (*Iliad* 19.225; LSJ).
