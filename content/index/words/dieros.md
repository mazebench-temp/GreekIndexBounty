## Meaning

διερός occurs twice in Homer, both times in the Odyssey:

- **6.201**: Nausicaa tells her handmaids that “there is no such man, no living mortal (διερὸς βροτός), nor will there be one” who would come to the land of the Phaeacians bringing war (see the note on Book 6).
- **9.43**: Odysseus tells how, after the raid on the Cicones, he urged his men “to flee with a διερός foot” (διερῷ ποδὶ φευγέμεν), and they did not obey. LSJ translates “with nimble foot”.

After Homer the common sense is “wet, liquid”: “the wet blood” in Aeschylus (*Eumenides* 263), and “the wet” opposed to “the dry” in Anaxagoras (LSJ).

The meaning in Homer is disputed. The evidence:

1. **“Living, active.”** LSJ gives “active, alive” for both Homeric places, and cites Aristarchus on 6.201. A scholion reports: “Aristarchus takes it thus, as ‘the living one’” (schol. E.H.P.Q.T. on 6.201, Dindorf p. 311). Another scholion explains why “wet” can mean “living”: life holds moisture and warmth, and death holds cold and dryness, so the dead are called ἀλίβαντες, “sapless” (schol. H on 6.201). Merry and Riddell prefer this view. They link διερός with διαίνω, “moisten”, and compare ὑγρός, which means both “moist” and “supple, active” (note on 6.201, p. 270). On this view “nimble” at 9.43 and “wet” after Homer come from one root.
2. **“Fleeing” or “to be feared.”** Merry and Riddell report that other commentators connect the word with δίεσθαι and δέος, “fear” (compare Latin *dirus*), and translate “timid” or “fleeing”. LSJ also gives this possibility for the first sense: “perh. cogn. with δίεμαι”, and “perh. for δϝι-ερός, ‘to be feared’”. A gloss in a Palatine manuscript explains διερός as βλαπτικός, πειρατικός, πειρατής, “harmful, piratical, a pirate” (Dindorf p. 311). Merry and Riddell report that Lehrs (*Aristarch.* 56, as they cite him) took this to be Aristarchus' own view: the man is “not one whom we need flee”. They reject it. The two reports of Aristarchus therefore disagree.
3. **A different word.** The ancient critic Callistratus wrote δυερός, “wretched”, from δύη, “misery” (schol. E.H.P.Q.T. on 6.201). The Oxford text of 1908 records δυερός written above the line in one manuscript (P6), and a third reading, δʼ ἱερός, in one family of manuscripts (o).

The translation gives “living” at 6.201.
