## Meaning

ἀλφηστής is a noun used as an adjective of men. In Homer it occurs only in the Odyssey, three times, always with ἀνήρ:

- **1.349**: Telemachus says that Zeus “gives to grain-eating men, as he wishes, to each one” (ἀνδράσιν ἀλφηστῇσιν).
- **6.8**: Nausithous settled the Phaeacians in Scheria, “far from grain-eating men” (ἑκὰς ἀνδρῶν ἀλφηστάων; see the note on Book 6).
- **13.261**: in his false tale to Athena, Odysseus says that the son of Idomeneus “beat grain-eating men with his swift feet” in Crete (ἀνέρας ἀλφηστὰς).

It recurs in early hexameter poetry (Hesiod, *Works and Days* 82; *Homeric Hymn to Apollo* 458, both cited by LSJ) and twice in tragic lyric (Aeschylus, *Seven against Thebes* 770; Sophocles, *Philoctetes* 709).

There are two derivations, and the sources do not settle between them:

1. **“Earners, enterprising men.”** The word comes from ἀλφάνω, “bring in, earn” (Homer has its aorist: ἵνα μοι βίοτον πολὺν ἄλφοι, “so that he may earn me a great living”, 17.250). LSJ takes this view: “lit. earners …, i.e. enterprising men”, “esp. of traders or seafarers” (13.261; *h.Ap.* 458). The same root is in ἀλφεσίβοιαι, girls “who bring in oxen” as bride-gifts (*Iliad* 18.593). Merry and Riddell favor this derivation. They say the sense of men as “hard-working, gain-getting” creatures contrasts with the gods who “live at ease”, and they cite Aeschylus, *Seven* 770, as using the word in this sense (note on 1.347–349, pp. 39–40).
2. **“Bread-eating.”** The word is made of ἄλφι, “barley meal” (as in ἄλφιτον), and ἔδω, “eat”. Merry and Riddell report this view and compare σιτοφάγος, “grain-eating”, of a man (9.191) and phrases like “eating the fruit of the plowland” (*Iliad* 6.142). They find this sense intended in Sophocles, *Philoctetes* 707–709 (note on 1.347–349, pp. 39–40). The translation follows this derivation: “grain-eating men”.

The ancient scholia give a third gloss, connected with the first: “inventive, provident” (εὑρετικῶν καὶ προνοητικῶν, schol. H on 6.8, Dindorf p. 294), and on 1.349 “inventive, ingenious, from ἀλφῶ, that is ‘find’” (schol. B.E. on 1.349, Dindorf p. 62).

On either derivation the phrase names ordinary mortals. Merry and Riddell's contrast with the gods who “live at ease” (θεοὶ ῥεῖα ζώοντες) applies to the first. The formulas for men who “eat the fruit of the plowland” set men apart from the gods in the second.
