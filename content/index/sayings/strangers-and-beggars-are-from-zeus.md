**The context.** Nausicaa has accepted the suppliant (6.191–197; see [[supplication]]). She calls back her handmaids, who ran away at the sight of him, and tells them that no enemy comes to the Phaeacians: “this is some unhappy man, a wanderer”, and “we must care for him now” (6.199–207). The maxim gives the reason, and the orders follow: food, drink and a bath in the river (6.209–210; see [[xenia]] and [[bathing]]).

**The two halves.**

1. **πρὸς Διός, “from Zeus”.** The preposition with the genitive can mean “sent by Zeus” or “under the protection of Zeus”. Merry and Riddell give both, the first as the literal sense, and compare the judges “who guard the laws from Zeus” (πρὸς Διός, Iliad 1.238–239) (note on 6.207, p. 271). The translation renders “from Zeus” and records the other sense in its notes (TRANSLATION.md, on 6.207–208). The stranger (ξεῖνος) and the beggar (πτωχός) are named together. In Book 6 Odysseus is a stranger in a beggar's need, and from Book 17 he plays the beggar in his own house. See [[ptochos]].
2. **δόσις δʼ ὀλίγη τε φίλη τε, “a small gift is a dear one”.** The phrase has no verb. A scholion explains: small to the one who gives, dear to the one who receives, for need holds even a little thing dear (schol. B, quoted by Merry and Riddell, note on 6.208, p. 271). Merry and Riddell compare Achilles' complaint that his own prize is “small but dear” (ὀλίγον τε φίλον τε, Iliad 1.167).

**Eumaeus.** The swineherd repeats the maxim to the disguised Odysseus in Book 14, with a different end. It is not right for him to dishonor a stranger, “not even if one worse than you should come; for from Zeus are all / strangers and beggars, and a gift, small and dear, / is ours”, “for that is the way of slaves” (14.56–59). In Nausicaa's mouth the small gift is a king's daughter's gift. In Eumaeus' mouth it is the gift of a slave who has little to give.

**The same belief elsewhere.** The Odyssey names Zeus as the protector of strangers and suppliants several times. Odysseus warns the Cyclops that Zeus is “the avenger of suppliants and strangers, / Zeus of strangers, who attends on reverend strangers” (9.270–271). In Ithaca Odysseus, as a beggar, curses Antinous: “if there are gods and Erinyes for beggars”, may death come to Antinous before his wedding (17.475–476). The suitors' scorn of the beggar is the breach of the rule that Nausicaa states.

## Outside Homer

The emperor Julian quotes the line with the words in the other order, πτωχοί τε ξεῖνοί τε, “beggars and strangers” (Julian 291B in Spanheim's pages, as recorded in the apparatus of the Oxford text of 1908 to 6.208; Julian's text was not checked here). Callistratus read τῶ μιν, “so him”, for τὸν νῦν at 6.207 (same apparatus).
