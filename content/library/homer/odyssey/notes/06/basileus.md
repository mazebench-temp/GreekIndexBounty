**βασίλεια.** The feminine of βασιλεύς occurs once in the book. Athena has planned that Odysseus should wake and see the girl, and “then the princess threw the ball toward a handmaid” (σφαῖραν ἔπειτʼ ἔρριψε μετʼ ἀμφίπολον βασίλεια, 6.115; see [[ball-play]]). LSJ glosses βασίλεια “queen, princess”. In the Odyssey the word is used of queens, Penelope (4.697, 4.770) and Arete (7.241, 13.59), and here of a king's daughter. The translation gives “princess” for βασίλεια and keeps “queen” for ἄνασσα, Odysseus' address to her (6.149, 6.175; see [[anax]]). The title stands at the end of the line, at the moment the princess, playing like a girl among her maids, sets the plot in motion.

**The kings of the Phaeacians.** Nausicaa meets her father at the door “as he went to join the famous kings / in council, to which the noble Phaeacians were calling him” (μετὰ κλειτοὺς βασιλῆας / ἐς βουλήν, 6.54–55; see [[boule]] and [[kings]]). The Phaeacians have more than one βασιλεύς. In Book 8 Alcinous says that twelve kings rule among the people, and he himself is the thirteenth (8.390–391). Nausicaa, in turn, calls her father the one “on whom the power and the strength of the Phaeacians depend” (6.197). Merry and Riddell compare Herodotus 6.109 for the construction τοῦ δʼ ἐκ … ἔχεται, “depend on him” (note on 6.197, p. 270). As in the Iliad, βασιλεύς names a rank that several men share, with one of them the greatest (see the article above).
