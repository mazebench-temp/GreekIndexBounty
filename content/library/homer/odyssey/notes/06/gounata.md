---
summary: Odysseus, naked before Nausicaa, debates whether to clasp her knees and decides not to; he says “I beseech you” (literally “I clasp your knees”) and that he is afraid to touch them. Nausicaa tells him to throw his hands around her mother's knees, the gesture he will make in Book 7.
grc: [γουνοῦμαι]
en: [clasp the knees, clasped her knees, beseech]
passages:
  - 6.141-149 | The knees not clasped
  - 6.310-315 | The knees of Arete
---

**The decision.** Nausicaa alone stands her ground, and Odysseus is “torn two ways in thought”. He could clasp the knees of the girl and entreat her, or stand apart and entreat her with soothing words (6.141–144). He chooses words, “lest the girl be angry in her mind if he clasped her knees” (μή οἱ γοῦνα λαβόντι χολώσαιτο φρένα κούρη, 6.145–147; see [[supplication]] and [[decision-scene]]). The usual gesture of supplication is to take the knees, often with a hand on the chin, and so to hold the person begged (see the article above). A scholion calls 6.144 superfluous, since Odysseus is not in doubt about what to ask but about how to ask it, and reports that Athenocles also suspected the line (schol. H.P. on 6.144, Dindorf p. 306).

**The word for the gesture.** His speech opens γουνοῦμαί σε, ἄνασσα, “I beseech you, queen” (6.149; see [[anax]]). γουνοῦμαι is the verb of supplication made from γόνυ, “knee”: literally “I clasp your knees”. Odysseus says the words and does not make the gesture. A scholion on the line notes exactly this: what he did not do in act he puts forward in speech, and he makes plain the reason he declined to touch her (schol. on 6.149, Dindorf p. 306). The reason comes at the end of his comparison of her to the palm on Delos: “I admire you, lady, and stand amazed, and I am terribly afraid / to touch your knees” (6.168–169; see [[thambos]]). The fear also fits his praise: he has just likened her to Artemis (6.150–152). The same formula is used by suppliants who do take the knees: the seer Leodes and the singer Phemius say γουνοῦμαί σʼ, Ὀδυσεῦ, “I beseech you, Odysseus”, as they beg for their lives (22.312, 22.344).

**The knees of the queen.** Nausicaa's directions end where his supplication will be completed. In the hall he is to pass by her father and “throw your hands around the knees / of our mother, so that you may see the day of homecoming” (μητρὸς περὶ γούνασι χεῖρας / βάλλειν, 6.310–311; see [[queen-arete]]). In the next book Athena, too, tells him to go first to the queen (7.53–77), and Odysseus does as both have said: “Odysseus threw his hands around the knees of Arete” (7.142). The gesture he would not make to the daughter at the river he makes to the mother in the hall.
