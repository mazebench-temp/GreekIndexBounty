**Companions of Artemis.** In the simile for Nausicaa among her handmaids, the nymphs go with Artemis over the mountains.

{{quote:83f4c3bb-b5cc-5021-a107-1a4f0ef9d4f7}}

They are κοῦραι Διὸς αἰγιόχοιο, “daughters of aegis-bearing Zeus” (6.105), the title the Iliad gives the nymphs of the mountains (Il. 6.420; see [[aegis-bearing]] and [[zeus]]). Artemis is also Zeus' daughter (6.151), so the goddess and her companions are sisters. In the comparison the nymphs stand for the handmaids, and Artemis, taller than all of them, stands for Nausicaa (6.107–109; see [[artemis]], [[artemis-among-her-nymphs]] and [[handmaids]]).

ἀγρονόμοι (6.106) occurs only here in Homer. LSJ gives “haunting the country” and cites this line for the nymphs (LSJ s.v. ἀγρονόμος). The word is ἀγρός, “field, wild country”, + νέμομαι, “range over”. The scholia discuss it at length:

- It is accented on the second-last syllable, ἀγρονόμοι, with an active sense, “the ones who range in the country”. Some wrote ἀγρόνομοι (H.P.Q.).
- Another explanation is “those who dispense the hunt”, from ἄγρα, “hunting” (E.V.).
- Megaclides read a different line: “gathering, they play through the rugged thickets” (H.P.).

Merry and Riddell take παίζουσι, “they play”, of hunting as a sport, since the nymphs go with the huntress. They compare Sophocles, *Oedipus Tyrannus* 1103 (Merry and Riddell on 6.106). There LSJ reads the other accent, ἀγρόνομοι πλάκες, “uplands that give open pasture”.

**Nymphs or girls?** The girls' shout wakes Odysseus, and he does not know who has made it.

{{quote:55c22fab-c15c-50bb-b70d-046e887f4b26}}

The cry is “as of girls, of nymphs”, κουράων … νυμφάων (6.122–123). Merry and Riddell take νυμφάων as a closer definition of κουράων. The places that Odysseus names are the haunts of the nymphs: mountain peaks, the springs of rivers and grassy meadows (see [[steep]] and [[grassy]]). The last line is identical with Iliad 20.9, where the nymphs come to the assembly of the gods. A scholion (E.H.P.) explains the guess. Because Odysseus is in a lonely place, he first suspects that the voices really are nymphs. Then he turns to the other possibility, that he is “near human beings who have speech” (6.125; see [[to-the-land-of-what-mortals]]).

Some modern editors suspected lines 123–124. Merry and Riddell report the objections of Nitzsch and Bothe: the line repeats Iliad 20.9, a cry of nymphs would say little about whether men live there, and κουράων νυμφάων is an awkward pair (see [[the-nymphs-cry-122-124]]). The scholia accept the lines without comment on their genuineness.

The confusion has a basis in the language. νύμφη is a goddess of the wild places and also an ordinary young woman of an age to marry (see the etymology in the general article). Homer does not call the girls νύμφαι, but they are of the age that the word describes, and the simile has just set them beside the goddesses. Odysseus has also just come from Calypso, a nymph who lived in a cave on her island (5.57; see [[ogygia]]). He keeps the same doubt when he speaks to Nausicaa, and asks whether she is a goddess or a mortal (6.149; see [[god-or-mortal]]).
