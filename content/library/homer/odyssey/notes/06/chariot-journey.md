summary: Nausicaa drives the mule wagon to the river and back in the verses of the chariot journey. The mules are yoked, she takes the whip and the glossy reins and “whipped them to a run”, but the gods' horses that “flew on” become two clattering mules, and on the way home she drives at a walker's pace so that the handmaids and Odysseus can keep up.

**The yoking.** The king's slaves make ready the “well-wheeled mule wagon”, bring up the mules and yoke them under the carriage (6.71–73; see [[wagon]] and [[mules]]). The girl brings out the clothes, her mother packs food, relishes and wine, and adds olive oil in a golden flask, and Nausicaa mounts (6.74–80).

**The drive out.** The verses are those of the warrior's or the god's drive, with one change at the end.

{{quote:de6033d5-4571-502b-88da-33832c41bd1d}}

“And she took the whip and the glossy reins” (μάστιγα καὶ ἡνία σιγαλόεντα, 6.81) is the phrase of Iliad 5.226, where Aeneas offers Pandarus the whip and reins of his chariot. “And whipped them to a run” (μάστιξεν δʼ ἐλάαν, 6.82) is the head of the verse that in the Iliad and in Odyssey 3 goes on “and the pair flew on not unwilling” (Iliad 5.366, 8.45; Odyssey 3.484, 3.494, 15.192). Here the second half is new: καναχὴ δʼ ἦν ἡμιόνοιιν, “and there was a clatter of the two mules”. The dual ἡμιόνοιιν keeps the pair of the formula, but the pair are mules, and they are heard, not seen to fly. “And they stretched out without end” (ἄμοτον τανύοντο, 6.83) is said of horses at full stretch (τανύοντο, Iliad 16.375). The load is the clothing and the girl herself, and the verse of a lady's escort follows, “not alone; other handmaids also went along with her” (6.84; cf. Iliad 3.143, Helen on her way to the wall).

**The arrival.** At the washing pools they loose the mules from under the carriage and drive them along the river “to graze on the honey-sweet field grass” (6.88–90). The arrival of the formula has the same two steps, unyoking and feeding. When Iris or Poseidon halts a team, the god “unyoked them from the chariot” and set ambrosial fodder before them (Iliad 5.368–369, 13.34–35). On Scheria the fodder is the grass by the river.

**The return.** For the journey home Nausicaa yokes “the strong-hoofed mules” herself and climbs up (6.252–253). The drive to the grove has its own verses.

{{quote:54766c66-4489-5ae7-aef9-befbabb5d589}}

She lashes the mules “with the shining whip” (ἵμασεν μάστιγι φαεινῇ, 6.316), after a speech, in the verse-opening ὣς ἄρα φωνήσασʼ ἵμασεν that in the Iliad sends off a team at speed (ὣς ἄρα φωνήσας ἵμασεν καλλίτριχας ἵππους, Iliad 11.531; Odyssey 5.380). The mules leave the river and run well (6.317–318). But the purpose of the drive is the opposite of speed. “She drove with care, so that they could follow on foot, / the handmaids and Odysseus, and she laid on the lash with judgment” (νόῳ δʼ ἐπέβαλλεν ἱμάσθλην, 6.319–320). The scholia gloss μάλα as “with skill” and νόῳ as “drove with art” (schol. Q, V on 6.319–320, Dindorf p. 320). The sun sets as they reach Athena's grove (6.321; see [[sunset-lines]] and [[grove-of-athena]]). The drive ends in the next book, when her brothers unyoke the mules at the palace door and carry in the clothes (Odyssey 7.2–6).

The type-scene of the heroic chariot is used in full for an errand of washing. Its formulas keep their shape, and the changed half-verses (the clatter of the mules, the measured whip) show the scale of the errand. See [[chariot]].
