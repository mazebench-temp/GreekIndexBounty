Odysseus comes out of the thicket holding a leafy branch before him:

{{quote:459846f4-cdcc-58be-99ca-660a3d21a3ae}}

ἀλκὶ πεποιθώς, “trusting in his courage”, is a formula for the end of the verse. The dative ἀλκί is an old form beside the usual ἀλκῇ (see the article above). The phrase occurs six times in Homer, five of them in the Iliad, always of a creature or a man in the fury of battle:

- Aeneas stands over the body of Pandarus “like a lion trusting in his courage” (*Iliad* 5.299).
- Idomeneus waits for Aeneas “like a boar in the mountains trusting in his courage” (*Iliad* 13.471).
- Menelaus is “like a mountain-bred lion trusting in his courage” (*Iliad* 17.61). This verse is the closest to 6.130.
- A wounded boar wheels among the dogs “trusting in his courage” (*Iliad* 17.725–728).
- Hector stands firm over the body of Patroclus, “trusting in his courage” (*Iliad* 18.158).

In Book 6 the courage of the battle formula belongs to the lion. The man it describes has no weapon and no clothes. He wants help from the girls, and he decides to speak to Nausicaa from a distance (6.141–147).
