**A dancing people.** The Phaeacians' old home is “Hypereia with its wide dancing floors” (εὐρυχόρῳ, 6.4; see [[with-wide-dancing-floors]] and [[hypereia-of-the-phaeacians]]). In the city of Scheria Nausicaa's three unmarried brothers “always want to wear freshly washed clothes / when they go to the dance” (ἐς χορὸν ἔρχεσθαι, 6.64–65; see [[sons-of-alcinous]] and [[washing-clothes]]). A scholion explains that the Phaeacians, being luxurious, danced every day (schol. H.P.T. on 6.65, Dindorf p. 299). Alcinous himself says so in Book 8: the feast, the lyre and dances, changes of clothes, warm baths and beds are always dear to the Phaeacians (8.248–249). He then has the young men dance for Odysseus (8.250–265), and his two sons dance with the ball (8.370–380; see [[ball-play]]).

**Nausicaa leads.** By the river the girls play at ball, “and among them white-armed Nausicaa began the song and dance” (ἤρχετο μολπῆς, 6.101). μολπή is “dance or rhythmic movement with song” (LSJ s.v. μολπή, citing this line). The scholia disagree whether here it means the game or the song (schol. on 6.101, Dindorf p. 302). Merry and Riddell take it as play with chant and dance (note on 6.101, p. 261). The simile that follows sets her among her handmaids as Artemis among the nymphs who play with her (6.102–109; see [[artemis-among-her-nymphs]]).

**The girl in the dance.** In his supplication Odysseus imagines the joy of Nausicaa's family “as they watch such a young shoot going into the dance” (χορὸν εἰσοιχνεῦσαν, 6.157; see [[thrice-blessed]]). The dance is the place where a young girl is seen by her people. The Iliad gives the same setting to Polymele, whom Hermes loved when he saw her “among the singers / in the dance of Artemis” (Iliad 16.181–183). Odysseus' praise passes at once from the dance to the man who will win her with bride-gifts (6.158–159; see [[marriage]]).
