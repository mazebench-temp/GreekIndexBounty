**A prayer for another.** Odysseus ends his supplication with a wish in the optative: “may the gods give you as much as you long for in your mind, / a husband and a home” (σοὶ δὲ θεοὶ τόσα δοῖεν, 6.180–181). He has nothing to give, so he asks the gods to give in his place (see [[nothing-better-than-like-mindedness]] and [[supplication]]). The wish names no god and has no sacrifice; it is a prayer only in its form.

**The girl's imagined prayer.** In the gossip that Nausicaa imagines, a townsman suggests that the handsome stranger may be “some much-prayed-for god” who has come down from the sky to her “at her prayer” (εὐξαμένῃ πολυάρητος θεός, 6.280–281; see [[tis-speech]] and [[marriage]]). πολυάρητος, “much prayed for”, is made from πολυ-, “much”, and ἀράομαι, “pray”. The Odyssey uses it once more, when the nurse tells Autolycus that his grandson, the infant Odysseus, is a child “much prayed for” (19.404). The joke of the townsman is that a girl's prayer for a husband has been answered by a god.

**Odysseus' prayer.** At sunset they reach Athena's grove, and Odysseus prays at once (ἠρᾶτο, 6.323). The prayer has the three parts of the form, invocation, claim and request, and its claim is a reproach: “now at least listen to me, since before you never listened / when I was being shattered” (6.325–326). The request is to come to the Phaeacians “dear and pitied” (6.327). The narrator closes with the formula of a heard prayer: “So he spoke in prayer, and Pallas Athena heard him” (6.328). She does not yet appear to him, out of respect for Poseidon (6.329–331). The parts of the prayer are treated in [[prayer-type-scene]], [[hear-me]], [[atrytone]] and [[dear-and-pitied]]; the goddess's absence in [[seeing-the-gods]].

**Aristotle's use.** Aristotle quotes the request, “grant that I come to the Phaeacians dear and pitied”, as a model for the aims of a speaker's introduction: to win the hearers' goodwill and their pity (*Rhetoric* 3.14.11, 1415b25–27).
