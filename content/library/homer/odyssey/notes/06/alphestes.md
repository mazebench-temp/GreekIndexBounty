---
summary: The book's second sentence places the Phaeacians “far from grain-eating men”, apart from ordinary mortals. What the separation means depends on how the disputed word is read.
passages:
  - 6.7-8 | Far from grain-eating men
---

The narrator tells how Nausithous led the Phaeacians away from the Cyclopes:

{{quote:11ca5a3c-83f5-5cc8-bb57-e835a04ae5c1}}

The phrase ἑκὰς ἀνδρῶν ἀλφηστάων defines Scheria by distance. The Phaeacians left one bad neighbor, the Cyclopes, who “kept doing them harm” (6.5–6), and settled where there are no neighbors at all. Nausicaa says the same thing later in her own words: “we live far apart in the much-surging sea, the farthest away, and no other mortal mingles with us” (6.204–205; see [[phaeacians]]).

The scholion on this line glosses ἀλφηστάων as “inventive and provident men” (schol. H on 6.8, Dindorf p. 294). LSJ cites the line under the sense “earners, enterprising men”, and adds “of the Phaeacians”. The translation renders “grain-eating men”, after the second derivation (see the article above). On the first reading, the Phaeacians live apart from men who must work for a living. On the second, they live apart from the mortals who eat bread. Zeus calls them “near to the gods” (ἀγχίθεοι, 5.35), and in Book 7 Alcinous says that the gods appear to them openly and feast beside them (7.201–205).

The same line holds a small textual point. Aristarchus read εἷσεν δὲ Σχερίῃ, “and settled them in Scheria”, as the text does. The scholia say that the Aristarchean copies have no ἐν, and that some read δʼ ἐν Σχερίῃ (schol. H.P. and E.Q. on 6.8, Dindorf p. 294).
