summary: Two baths in the river. Nausicaa and her handmaids bathe and anoint themselves after the washing, with oil that her mother gave her. Odysseus is offered the guest's bath, but he refuses to be bathed by the girls and washes the brine off himself.

**The oil for the girls.** As the wagon leaves, Nausicaa's mother gives her “liquid olive oil in a golden flask, / so that she, with her handmaid women, might anoint herself after bathing” (χυτλώσαιτο, 6.79–80; see [[olive-oil]] and [[queen-arete]]). χυτλόομαι is rare. A scholion says that the poet uses it of anointing after a bath, not of anointing alone, and that water mixed with oil was called χύτλον (schol. B.Q.T. on 6.80, Dindorf p. 300). Merry and Riddell derive χύτλον from χυτός, “poured”, and explain that the verb covers both bathing and anointing, the two acts described at 6.96 (note on 6.80, p. 259).

**The girls bathe.** When the clothes are washed and spread out, the girls “bathed and anointed themselves richly with olive oil” (λοεσσάμεναι καὶ χρισάμεναι λίπʼ ἐλαίῳ, 6.96), ate their meal by the river, and waited for the clothes to dry (6.96–98; see [[washing-clothes]]). The bath belongs to the day's work and comes before the meal, as the guest's bath does in a house.

**The guest's bath offered.** When Nausicaa accepts the suppliant, her orders follow the usual order of hospitality: food and drink, and a bath “in the river, where there is shelter from the wind” (6.209–210; see [[xenia]]). The handmaids set him in the shelter, lay out clothes, give him oil in the golden flask, and tell him to bathe in the stream (6.211–216). In the houses of the Odyssey a guest is bathed by the women of the house (see the article above). The handmaids here are ready to do the same.

**The bath refused.** Odysseus tells the girls to stand away, so that he can wash the brine from his shoulders himself and anoint himself, “for truly ointment has long been away from my skin” (6.218–220). Then he gives his reason:

{{quote:Od. 6.221-222 | I will not bathe in front of you}}

His shame at being naked in front of unmarried girls overrides the custom (see [[nakedness]] and [[aidos]]). He washes himself in three movements: the brine from his back and broad shoulders, the scurf of the sea from his head, and then oil all over (6.224–227). The verb ἔσμηχεν, “he scrubbed”, occurs only here in Homer. The bath ends as baths do in the Odyssey, with clean clothes (6.228). Then Athena adds what no bath can, and makes him taller and more beautiful (6.229–237; see [[divine-beautification]]).
