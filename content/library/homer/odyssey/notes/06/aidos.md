summary: Three uses of the verb, none of the noun. Nausicaa “felt shame to name the marriage” to her father; Odysseus “feels shame to strip naked” before the girls; and Athena does not appear openly to Odysseus because she “stood in awe of her father's brother” Poseidon, where one ancient copy read ἅζετο.

The noun αἰδώς does not occur in Book 6. The verb occurs three times, in two senses: the shame that holds a person back from saying or doing something before others, and the respect owed to a greater power (see the article above).

**Shame to name the marriage.** Athena has told Nausicaa in a dream that her marriage is near and that she must wash the clothes for it (6.25–40). In the morning Nausicaa asks her father for the wagon. She gives other reasons: he needs clean clothes for the council, and her brothers want freshly washed clothes for the dance (6.57–65). The narrator gives the real one (6.66–67):

{{quote:Od. 6.66-67 | She felt shame to name the marriage}}

αἴδετο γὰρ θαλερὸν γάμον ἐξονομῆναι, “she felt shame to name the marriage of her prime”. The shame is a girl's modesty before her father. Alcinous sees through the excuse: “he understood everything” (ὁ δὲ πάντα νόει, 6.67) and gives her the mules and the wagon without a question. Merry and Riddell note the contrast with her words to the handmaids later. With no man listening, she wishes aloud for such a husband (6.244–245), and they cite 6.66 for the girl who is “very different” in the presence of men (note on 6.244, p. 274; see [[posis]]). The translation renders the verb as in Hector's “I feel shame before the Trojans” (αἰδέομαι Τρῶας, *Iliad* 6.442).

**Shame to strip.** The handmaids have set out clothes and oil, and Nausicaa has told them to bathe the stranger in the river (6.210–216). Odysseus sends them away (6.218–222):

{{quote:Od. 6.218-222 | Odysseus will not be bathed}}

αἰδέομαι γὰρ / γυμνοῦσθαι κούρῃσιν ἐυπλοκάμοισι μετελθών, “for I feel shame / to strip naked when I have come among girls with lovely tresses”. He has already met them naked, with a branch held before him (6.127–129). Now, with clothes at hand, he will not be washed by them. The scholia saw a problem here. Elsewhere in the poem a young woman bathes a guest: Nestor's youngest daughter Polycaste bathes Telemachus (3.464–465). One scholion argues that “bathe him” means “provide for his bath”, and that the handmaids rightly understood the order so (schol. P.Q.T. on 6.215, Dindorf p. 312). Another says that Odysseus' refusal conflicts with being bathed by maidens and offers solutions. The words “having come among” mark him as a stranger. Perhaps maidens never bathe men at all, since the poet says “the slave women bathed him”, not girls. Whether Hebe was a maiden is disputed. Helen, at any rate, could bathe Odysseus (schol. Q.T. on 6.221, Dindorf p. 312). Dindorf refers to the scholion on 3.464 for the same question. The text itself gives Odysseus' reason as shame (see [[bathing]] and [[nakedness]]).

**Awe of a god.** At the end of the book Odysseus prays to Athena in her grove, and she hears him (6.323–328; see [[athena]]). She does not come to him openly (6.329–331):

{{quote:Od. 6.329-331 | Athena stands in awe of Poseidon}}

αἴδετο γάρ ῥα / πατροκασίγνητον, “for she stood in awe of / her father's brother”, Poseidon, the brother of Zeus (see [[kasignetos]] and [[poseidon]]). Here the verb is the respect of a younger god for an elder of her family, who is angry with Odysseus. A scholion records a variant: “it is also written ἅζετο” (γρ. ἅζετο, schol. P on 6.329, Dindorf p. 320). ἅζομαι is the verb of reverent awe, used above all of the gods (see the article above). Athena gives the reason herself when she reveals herself on Ithaca: “I did not wish to fight with Poseidon, my father's brother, who put anger in his heart against you, enraged because you blinded his dear son” (13.341–343).
