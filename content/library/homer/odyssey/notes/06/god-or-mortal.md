---
summary: Odysseus opens his supplication of Nausicaa with the question whether she is a goddess or a mortal, and builds the first part of his speech on its two answers. If a goddess, she is Artemis; if a mortal, her family is thrice blessed. Later in the book Nausicaa turns the same thought on him.
refs: [6.149-153, 6.243]
---

{{quote:Od. 6.149-153 | Goddess or mortal?}}

**The two conditions.** “If you are a goddess, one of those who hold the wide sky” (εἰ μέν τις θεός ἐσσι, 6.150), she is most like Artemis, “in looks and stature and build” (6.151–152; see [[artemis]] and [[artemis-among-her-nymphs]]). “But if you are one of the mortals who dwell on the earth” (εἰ δέ τίς ἐσσι βροτῶν, 6.153), then the blessing of her family follows (6.154–159; see [[thrice-blessed]]). The second condition opens with the words of Diomedes' challenge to Glaucus, εἰ δέ τίς ἐσσι βροτῶν (Iliad 6.142). There it leads to a threat of death; here it leads to praise.

**Why ask.** Odysseus has just decided to keep his distance and to entreat her with soothing words (6.143–148; see [[decision-scene]]). The question gives him a way to praise without seeming to flatter. A scholion explains the device: to speak to her straight out as to a goddess would have raised the suspicion of flattery; by doubting he makes his words more credible, as if he were drawn by what he sees. It adds that the order is well chosen, since he puts the thought of divinity first, as the one he leans toward (schol. H.Q. on 6.149, Dindorf p. 306). On the accent of ἦ, see the entry.

**The narrator's ground.** The doubt is not empty. The narrator described the sleeping girl as “like the immortal goddesses in build and in looks” (6.16) and likened her to Artemis among the nymphs (6.102–109). Odysseus' first comparison thus repeats what the reader has been shown.

**Turned around.** After Athena has made Odysseus taller and poured grace on him, Nausicaa says to her handmaids: “before, he truly seemed to me to be unsightly, / but now he is like the gods who hold the wide sky” (θεοῖσιν ἔοικε, τοὶ οὐρανὸν εὐρὺν ἔχουσιν, 6.242–243; see [[gold-poured-on-silver]]). The verse ends with the relative clause of Odysseus' own question (6.150). In Book 7 Alcinous asks the question about him outright, and Odysseus denies that he is like the gods (7.199–210).
