**Athena's plan.** The book's action begins with Athena's purpose. She goes to the house of Alcinous, “planning a homecoming for great-hearted Odysseus” (νόστον Ὀδυσσῆι μεγαλήτορι μητιόωσα, 6.14; see [[athena]]). Everything that follows, the dream, the washing, the ball that wakes Odysseus, serves that plan. The whole verse comes back in Book 8, when she goes through the city in the likeness of Alcinous' herald (8.8–9).

**Escort and homecoming.** Nausicaa ends her instructions with the promise: “listen quickly to my word, so that very quickly / you may gain an escort and a homecoming from my father” (πομπῆς καὶ νόστοιο, 6.289–290). The two nouns stand together: the πομπή, the Phaeacians' service of carrying a traveler home by ship, is the means, and the νόστος is the end (see [[pompe]]). Only the Phaeacians can give Odysseus both. He has no ship and no companions left.

**The day of homecoming.** The last instructions name the goal in a formula: if he clasps the knees of her mother, he will “see the day of homecoming / rejoicing, and quickly, even if you are from very far away” (νόστιμον ἦμαρ ἴδηαι, 6.311–312; see [[gounata]]). νόστιμον ἦμαρ is literally “the homecoming day”; LSJ explains it as “the day of return, i.e. the return itself”. The phrase is in the proem: the Sun “took from them”, Odysseus' companions, “the day of homecoming” (1.9). It recurs through the Odyssey (for example 1.168, 3.233, 5.220) and does not occur in the Iliad. The following lines spell out what the day means: “there is hope for you to see your dear ones and to reach / your well-built house and your native land” (6.314–315). Nearly the same three lines close Athena's advice in the next book (7.75–77).

The word νέεσθαι, “go back”, is used in the book of Nausicaa as she prepares to go home from the river (πάλιν οἶκόνδε νέεσθαι, 6.110; see the article above). Her homecoming is a short drive to her father's house; his is the subject of the poem.
