  - 6.175-179 | Queen, have pity

**The feminine of ἄναξ.** ἄνασσα is the feminine of ἄναξ, “lord” (see the article above); LSJ glosses it “queen, lady”. Odysseus uses it twice in his speech to Nausicaa. It comes at the opening, “I beseech you, queen: are you some goddess, or a mortal?” (γουνοῦμαί σε, ἄνασσα, 6.149), and at the point where he turns to his request, “But, queen, have pity” (ἀλλά, ἄνασσʼ, ἐλέαιρε, 6.175). The translation keeps “queen” for ἄνασσα and gives “princess” to βασίλεια, the narrator's word for her at 6.115 (see [[basileus]]).

**A title for goddesses.** Merry and Riddell note that Homer uses the address elsewhere only of goddesses, of Demeter (*Iliad* 14.326) and of Athena, to whom Nestor prays “but, queen, be gracious” (ἀλλὰ ἄνασσʼ ἵληθι, 3.380). They conclude that it carries “a special compliment” when given to a woman (note on 6.149, p. 265). LSJ lists 6.149 as an address to a mortal and 6.175 among addresses to goddesses. The word fits the question with which the speech opens: Odysseus leaves open whether he speaks to a goddess like Artemis or to a mortal (6.149–153; see [[god-or-mortal]]). A scholion on 6.149 explains the tactic. To speak to her outright as a goddess would raise the suspicion of flattery, but by doubting he makes his words more credible (schol. on 6.149, Dindorf p. 306).

Nausicaa is the king's daughter and not yet a queen. Her mother Arete, to whom she sends him, is the queen of the house (6.303–315). In the next book Athena tells Odysseus that Alcinous honors Arete as no other woman on earth is honored by her husband (7.66–68).
