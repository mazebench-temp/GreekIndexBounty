---
summary: Odysseus compares Nausicaa to a young palm he once saw beside Apollo's altar on Delos. It is the only mention of Delos in Homer, and the comparison gives his praise the authority of something he has seen.
refs: [6.164]
---

**A memory as a comparison.** Odysseus has said that he never saw such a mortal, man or woman, and that awe holds him as he looks at Nausicaa (6.160–161). He gives one parallel: “once on Delos, beside the altar of Apollo, I saw such a thing” (6.162). The thing is a young shoot of a palm, φοίνικος νέον ἔρνος, springing up (6.163). He stood amazed for a long time, because no such stem had ever come up out of the earth, and he admires the girl and stands amazed in the same way (6.166–168; see [[the-palm-shoot-on-delos]] and [[thambos]]). He has already called her a θάλος, “a young shoot”, going into the dance (6.157). Nausicaa is compared first to Artemis, Apollo's sister (6.151–152), and then to a plant at Apollo's altar (see [[artemis]] and [[apollo]]).

**The journey.** The aside “for I went there too, and a great army followed me” tells Nausicaa that the stranger was once a leader of men (6.164). A scholion (E.P.Q.V.) makes this point: Odysseus presents himself as a man of rank, not a merchant or a rower. The next line, “on that journey on which evil sorrows were to come upon me” (6.165), points to the Trojan war without naming it (see [[odysseus-at-delos]]).

**Which palm.** Scholia disagree whether Odysseus means the palm of Leto. One (E.V.) says that he means the palm that sprang up for Leto and that she held when she gave birth. Others (B.P.Q.) say that he does not, and join νέον with ἀνερχόμενον: the shoot was “newly” coming up. In the *Homeric Hymn to Apollo* Leto clasps a palm when she bears Apollo on Delos (*Hymn to Apollo* 117). Homer says only that the shoot was young and stood by the altar (see [[the-palm-of-delos]] and [[palm-tree]]).
