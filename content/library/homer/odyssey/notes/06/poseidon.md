---
summary: Poseidon never appears in Book 6, but he shapes it. His shrine stands in the Phaeacian place of assembly, Odysseus blames him for the wreck, and his anger is the reason that Athena hears the prayer in her grove without showing herself.
refs: [6.330-331]
---

**The shrine in the city.** Nausicaa's account of the city includes a shrine of Poseidon. The place of assembly lies “around the fine shrine of Poseidon”, καλὸν Ποσιδήιον ἀμφίς, fitted with great hauled stones (6.266–267; see [[posideion]] and [[assembly]]). The shipyard work follows at once: tackle, cables, sails and oars (6.268–269). A people of ships honors the god of the sea. Book 7 gives the reason in the family tree. Nausithous, the founder who led the Phaeacians to Scheria (6.7–10), was the son of Poseidon and Periboea (7.56–62; see [[nausithous]] and [[phaeacians]]). The god who wrecks Odysseus is the ancestor of the people who will send him home.

**The wreck, named in a prayer.** Odysseus names Poseidon only by his title. In the prayer to Athena he says that she never listened “when I was being shattered, when the famous shaker of the earth shattered me”, ῥαιομένου, ὅτε μʼ ἔρραιε κλυτὸς ἐννοσίγαιος (6.326; see [[shaker-of-the-earth]] and [[prayer-type-scene]]). Odysseus used the same title, κλυτὸς ἐννοσίγαιος, when he spoke to himself in the sea, before he reached the river: “I know how the famous shaker of the earth is angry with me” (5.423).

{{quote:e47a9a30-e22c-5c9c-bc01-522455b24adb}}

ῥαίω means “break, shatter”. LSJ cites this passage for the passive in the sense “suffer shipwreck”, and Od. 8.569 and 13.151 for the active “wreck a ship” (LSJ s.v. ῥαίω). The verb returns in the Phaeacian part of the story. Alcinous remembers his father's prophecy that Poseidon would one day “shatter” a Phaeacian ship on its way home from an escort (8.564–569). In Book 13 Poseidon uses the verb himself when he tells Zeus that he wants to wreck the ship (13.151), and Alcinous, when he sees the ship turned to stone, repeats his father's words (13.177; see [[the-wrath-of-poseidon]]).

**Athena's father's brother.** The narrator explains why Athena hears the prayer but does not appear.

{{quote:befe53f9-fc2f-5b42-a0a6-7eee67ebc3a0}}

πατροκασίγνητος is “father's brother” (LSJ, citing this line, Od. 13.342 and Il. 21.469; see [[kasignetos]]). A scholion (B) glosses it: Poseidon, the brother of her father Zeus. Athena gives the same reason herself on Ithaca. She did not want “to fight Poseidon, her father's brother”, who was angry because Odysseus blinded his son (13.341–343; see [[athena]] and [[cyclopes]]).

The last line and a half repeat the opening of the poem. In Book 1 all the gods pitied Odysseus “except Poseidon; and he raged unceasingly against godlike Odysseus until he reached his own land” (1.20–21). At 6.330–331 the narrator repeats the verb μενέαινεν and the whole of the second line, ἀντιθέῳ Ὀδυσῆι πάρος ἣν γαῖαν ἱκέσθαι. Only the adverb changes. ἀσπερχές, “unceasingly”, becomes ἐπιζαφελῶς, “furiously”. In the Iliad ἐπιζαφελῶς describes the anger of Agamemnon (Il. 9.516). The scholia report that Aristarchus accented the word with a circumflex (schol. on 6.330).

**Why Athena waits.** Merry and Riddell explain the line from the rules of the divine world. Athena does not show herself until Odysseus is on Ithaca, beyond Poseidon's power, because one god does not openly oppose another. They compare Artemis in Euripides, *Hippolytus* 1328–1330 (Merry and Riddell on 6.330). The time limit is already in the text. Poseidon's anger lasts “until he reached his own land” (6.331). Poseidon himself knows that it is Odysseus' fate to escape his misery in the land of the Phaeacians (5.288–289). In Book 13 his last act of anger falls on the Phaeacian ship that brought Odysseus home, and the Phaeacians end their part in the poem with a sacrifice to Poseidon around his altar (13.125–187).
