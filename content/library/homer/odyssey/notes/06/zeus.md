---
summary: Zeus does not act in Book 6, but Nausicaa states two rules about him. He deals out prosperity to the good and the bad as he wishes, so Odysseus must endure his lot (6.188–190), and all strangers and beggars come from him (6.207–208). He is also named as the father of Artemis, Athena and the nymphs.
relations:

**Prosperity from Zeus.** Nausicaa answers Odysseus' long supplication with a general reflection.

{{quote:47b5e8f8-2b7c-55d6-870c-b0fe68248da2}}

Ζεὺς ... Ὀλύμπιος, “Zeus the Olympian”, deals out ὄλβος, “prosperity”, to good and bad men alike, “as he wishes” (see [[olbos]], [[olympian]] and [[zeus-deals-out-prosperity]]). Merry and Riddell find the point in ὅπως ἐθέλῃσιν: the good and the evil are given by no rule but the god's will. They compare Helen's words in Book 4, that Zeus gives good and evil now to one man, now to another (4.236–237), and Achilles' two jars in the Iliad (Iliad 24.527–533). Two scholia give two readings of 6.189. In the first, Zeus mixes good and bad and gives each man his share by merit. In the second, it rests with the god to distribute good and evil, and they do not come to men according to their virtue or vice. Nausicaa draws a practical conclusion. Zeus has given Odysseus these hardships, and he must endure them, but now that he has come to the Phaeacians he will not lack clothing or anything a suppliant needs (6.190–193). Merry and Riddell treat 6.188–190 as a parenthesis inside the sentence that begins at 6.187.

**Strangers and beggars.** When the handmaids run from Odysseus, Nausicaa calls them back.

{{quote:435b9acc-47c3-55d3-875f-6b3a3b7b414d}}

πρὸς Διός, “from Zeus”: a scholion explains that strangers and beggars are under Zeus and pitied by him (see [[strangers-and-beggars-are-from-zeus]], [[xenia]] and [[ptochos]]). Merry and Riddell render the phrase “sent by Zeus”, and then “under the protection of Zeus”, and compare the old Phaeacian Echeneus in Book 7, who proposes a libation to Zeus, “who attends on suppliants, who are to be respected” (7.165). Alcinous repeats the line (7.181). Eumaeus, the swineherd, says the same words when he receives the disguised Odysseus (14.57–58). The Oxford text of 1908 records that Callistratus read τῷ μιν for τὸν νῦν at 6.207 (scholion and apparatus), and that the emperor Julian quotes 6.208 with the two nouns in reverse order (“Iulian. 291 B” in the apparatus). A scholion explains “a small gift is a dear one”: small for the giver, dear to the receiver, because need counts even a little as dear.

**Father.** The nymphs who play with Artemis are “daughters of aegis-bearing Zeus” (6.105; see [[nymphs]] and [[aegis-bearing]]). Odysseus likens Nausicaa to “Artemis, the daughter of great Zeus” (6.151; see [[artemis]]). Athena is “child of Zeus” (6.229), and in his prayer Odysseus calls her “daughter of great Zeus” and “child of aegis-bearing Zeus” (6.323–324; see [[athena]], [[daughter-of-zeus]] and [[child-of-aegis-bearing-zeus]]).
