---
summary: The simile gives the first sight of Odysseus in Book 6 as the girls will see it, a beast of prey coming out of the wood, between his decision to try his luck (6.126) and his decision to keep his distance (6.141–147). It answers the simile of Artemis the huntress twenty verses before.
---

**Its place.** Odysseus has woken, asked what people live here, and resolved to “try for myself and see” (πειρήσομαι, 6.126). He comes out of the thickets and breaks off a leafy branch “to shield the man's genitals about his body” (6.127–129). The simile follows, and its lion too is out to “make an attempt” (πειρήσοντα, 6.134): the man's trial and the beast's attack are named with the same verb.

**The girls' view.** The narrator turns at once to how he looks to them: “Terrible he appeared to them, marred by the brine, / and they fled in fear, this way and that, over the jutting beaches” (6.137–138). The lion is thus a picture of the man as the girls see him and as his need drives him, not of what he will do. A scholion on 6.137 explains their terror by inexperience: all youth is fearful, girls most of all, and girls reared in luxury are struck by what is strange to them (schol. P.Q.T., Dindorf p. 305). Only Nausicaa stays, because Athena “put daring in her mind and took the fear from her limbs” (6.139–140; see [[athena]] and [[nausicaa]]).

**After the huntress.** Nausicaa among her handmaids has just been likened to Artemis, who delights in hunting boars and “swift deer” (6.104; see [[artemis-among-her-nymphs]]). The lion goes after the same quarry, “the wild deer” (6.133). In the first simile the girls are a goddess' hunting band; in the second they are the prey. The two pictures meet when the one girl who was likened to the goddess stands her ground before the man likened to the lion (6.141).

**The turn.** The beast does not spring. He weighs his approach, decides against touching her, and speaks “a soothing and shrewd word” (6.141–148; see [[decision-scene]]). The man who came out like a lion begins his speech with “I beseech you” (6.149; see [[supplication-type-scene]]).
