**A founded city.** The book opens with the Phaeacians' history. They once lived in Hypereia, next to the Cyclopes, who harmed them; Nausithous led them away and settled them in Scheria (6.4–8; see [[scheria]]). His work as founder is told in four acts:

{{quote:Od. 6.9-10 | The founding}}

A wall around the city, houses, temples of the gods, and the plowlands divided into lots. Merry and Riddell explain that the wall comes first because it marks the site and size of the city, and because the Phaeacians had learned from their old neighbors to live in a walled town; ἐδάσσατʼ ἀρούρας means that he allotted them land to farm (notes on 6.9 and 6.10, p. 253).

**Land and city.** The Phaeacians have a δῆμος, a land and its people, and a πόλις, the city (δῆμόν τε πόλιν τε, 6.3). Twice the people are those “who hold this city and land” (τήνδε πόλιν καὶ γαῖαν ἔχουσιν, 6.177, 6.195): Odysseus says he knows none of them, and Nausicaa names them. Odysseus' plea has the city as its goal: he ponders whether to beg her to “show him the city” (6.144), and then asks, “Show me the town” (ἄστυ δέ μοι δεῖξον, 6.178). She answers with the same words, “I will show you the town” (ἄστυ δέ τοι δείξω, 6.194).

**πόλις and ἄστυ.** The two words stand in successive lines (6.177–178). Merry and Riddell see no intended distinction between them in this passage (note on 6.177–178, p. 267). They add that the etymology points to a difference: they connect πόλις with the root πλε- or πελ- and take it as the settlement of a multitude, and they take ἄστυ (ϝάστυ) as a “dwelling”, from the root of Sanskrit *vāsta*, “house”. LSJ gives the old initial ϝ from inscriptions, but doubts the link with Sanskrit *vasati*, “dwell”. LSJ also cites *Iliad* 17.144, πόλιν καὶ ἄστυ, as the one place where ἄστυ is the town as a place and πόλις the community. The translation keeps the two words apart, “city” for πόλις and πτόλις, and “town” for ἄστυ (6.178, 6.194, 6.296; see the translation notes).

**The city described.** The washing pools are “a long way from the city” (6.40), and so Nausicaa drives the wagon. On the way back she describes the city that Odysseus will enter (6.262–269):

{{quote:Od. 6.262-269 | The city of the Phaeacians}}

The rampart (πύργος) is high; there is a fine harbor on either side, and a narrow entrance between them. Merry and Riddell read this as a town on a peninsula joined to the land by a narrow neck, and compare the Phoenician city of Tyre with its two harbors (note on 6.263, p. 275). Along the road the ships are drawn up, each man with his own ship-shed (see [[epistion]]). There is the place of assembly around the shrine of Poseidon, built of great stones bedded in the earth (see [[posideion]] and [[assembly]]). It is a city of seafarers. The sentence that begins with this description has no proper main clause. Nausicaa's directions resume only at 6.295 (Merry and Riddell, note on 6.262, p. 275).

**At a distance from the city.** Nausicaa does not want to enter the city with a strange man (6.273–288; see [[phemis]]). She tells Odysseus to wait in Athena's grove, “as far from the city (πτόλιος) as a man can make himself heard when he shouts” (6.294). When he thinks she has reached the house, he is to “go into the city of the Phaeacians” and ask the way (6.296–299). The city is where reputation is made. The gossip she fears would come from “the people” (κατὰ δῆμον, 6.274, 6.283).
