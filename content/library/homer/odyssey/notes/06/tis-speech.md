**The setting.** Nausicaa has just told Odysseus to follow the wagon through the fields with her handmaids. At the edge of the city he must stop, because “it is their unkind talk that I shun, so that no one afterward / may blame me; and there are very overbearing men among the people” (6.273–274; see [[phemis]] and [[praise-and-blame]]). Then she gives the talk a voice:

{{quote:Od. 6.275-285 | Who is this following Nausicaa?}}

**The form.** The speech is introduced like the imagined speeches of the Iliad. Hector twice pictures what “someone will say” in time to come, with the same subjunctive εἴπῃσι (καί ποτέ τις εἴπῃσι, Iliad 6.459, 7.87). Here the speaker has a character as well: κακώτερος, “a baser man”, met on the road (ἀντιβολήσας, 6.275). The speech ends with the singular become plural, ὣς ἐρέουσιν, “so they will say” (6.285): one man stands for the town. Hector's imagined speakers give a hero his fame, or a widow her grief. Nausicaa's speaker gives her a bad name, and the last word of the frame is ὀνείδεα, “reproaches” (6.285).

**The gossip.** The baser man asks two questions and offers three explanations. Who is this handsome, tall stranger, and where did she find him? He will be her husband. Perhaps she took in a man from a far land, blown off course from his ship, “since there are none nearby”; or a god has come down from the sky at her prayers, and she will have him all her days; or, better still, she has gone looking and found a husband elsewhere, “for truly she dishonors these men here among the people, / the Phaeacians, who are courting her, many and good” (6.276–284; see [[much-prayed-for]], [[marriage]] and [[phaeacians]]). The scholia hear irony in “since there are none nearby” and in “better so” (schol. B on 6.279, schol. B.E.Q.T. on 6.282, Dindorf p. 317). The guesses repeat, in a sneering voice, what has really happened on the beach: a stranger driven from his ship, who looks like a god after Athena's work (6.242–243), and whom Nausicaa has just wished for as a husband (6.244–245; see [[nausicaa]] and [[odysseus]]).

**Her own judgment.** She closes with a statement in her own voice. She too would blame (νεμεσῶ) a girl who, against the will of her father and mother, keeps company with men before a public marriage (6.286–288; see [[nemesis]]). The imagined blame is therefore one she shares.

**Doubts and a parallel.** An ancient critic athetized the fourteen verses from 6.275 to 6.288 as unfitting to the character of the speaker, and as already said in the two verses before them (schol. H.Q. on 6.275, Dindorf p. 317; see [[the-gossip-lines-275-288]]). The Odyssey has one close parallel. In Book 21 the suitor Eurymachus tells Penelope that the suitors fear “the talk of men and women, / lest some other baser man of the Achaeans say” that worse men court the wife of a blameless man and cannot string his bow, while a wandering beggar strung it easily: “so they will say, and these things would be reproaches to us” (21.323–329). The frame is the same: φάτις, “talk”, τις εἴπῃσι κακώτερος, and ὣς ἐρέουσ(ιν) with “these things would become reproaches”. In both places the talk turns on a stranger who outshines the local suitors.
