---
summary: Nausicaa's description of the Phaeacian city is a description of a harbor town. The ships are drawn up along the road, each in its own berth, the men tend their tackle and shape their oars, and the Phaeacians care for ships, not for the bow.
heading: The ships of the Phaeacians
passages:
  - 6.262-272 | The harbor and the ships
---

**The harbor town.** In Book 6 the ships are seen through Nausicaa's directions to Odysseus (6.262–272). The city has a high rampart, “a fine harbor on either side of the city, and a narrow entrance” (6.263–264). The curving ships are drawn up along the road, “for all have a ship-shed, each man his own” (6.264–265; see [[curving]] and [[epistion]]). Merry and Riddell discuss the verb εἰρύαται. The scholia hesitate between “have been hauled up” and “guard the road”, and Merry and Riddell prefer the first, “are drawn up along the road”, as of beached ships in the Iliad (note on 6.264). The place of assembly lies around the [[posideion]], the shrine of the sea-god, and the ships' tackle is tended there (6.266–268).

{{quote:Od. 6.262-272 | The ships of the Phaeacians}}

**The work on the ships.** “There they tend the tackle of the black ships, cables and sails, and they taper the oars” (6.268–269; see [[black-ships]] and [[oars]]). The tackle is listed as πείσματα, “cables”, and σπεῖρα, “cloths”, here the sails (see the translation notes). Merry and Riddell report a variant σπείρας in Eustathius, glossed in a scholion (T) as the warping cables by which ships are hauled (critical note on 6.269). The verb ἀποξύνουσιν means “sharpen” or “taper”, of the blades of the oars. Merry and Riddell compare the epithet προήκης, “tapering”, given to oars at Odyssey 12.205 (note on 6.269).

**Ships, not bows.** The reason for this activity is given in a contrast: “For the Phaeacians do not care for the bow or the quiver, but for masts and the oars of ships and balanced ships, glorying in which they cross the gray sea” (6.270–272; see [[bow]], [[quiver]], [[mast]], [[balanced-ships]] and [[gray-sea]]). Nausicaa has said that no enemy comes to the Phaeacians, who live far off in the sea (6.201–205), and their craft is seafaring. Merry and Riddell note that ἀγαλλόμενοι, “glorying”, fits the bond between the Phaeacians and their ships, which in Book 8 “themselves know the thoughts and minds of men” (note on 6.272; Odyssey 8.559). The names of the people point the same way: Nausithous, Nausicaa, and Dymas “famous for ships” (6.7, 6.17, 6.22; see [[phaeacians]] and [[famous-for-ships]]).

**A ship in the gossip.** In the imagined talk of a townsman, the stranger behind Nausicaa may be a man “driven off course, from his own ship” (πλαγχθέντα … ἧς ἀπὸ νηός, 6.278), since no men from abroad live near. The suggestion fits a people that sails everywhere and receives few visitors (6.204–205; see [[the-gossip-lines-275-288]]).

**What the ships will do.** Book 6 shows the ships at rest. In the following books a Phaeacian ship takes Odysseus home to Ithaca while he sleeps, and on its return Poseidon turns it to stone as it comes near the city (13.70–125, 13.159–164; see [[poseidon]] and [[pompe]]).
