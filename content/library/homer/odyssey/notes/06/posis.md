summary: Nausicaa wishes that “such a man might be called my husband”, two lines that Aristarchus athetized; in her imagined gossip the townsmen say the stranger “will be a husband for her” and mock her for finding a husband from elsewhere. The same form πόσιν in βρῶσίν τε πόσιν τε (6.209, 6.246, 6.248) is a different word, “drink”.
passages:
  - 6.239-245 | Nausicaa's wish
  - 6.276-284 | The gossip about a husband

**Two words πόσις.** Book 6 has πόσις “husband” three times (6.244, 6.277, 6.282) and πόσις “drink” three times, in the half-line βρῶσίν τε πόσιν τε, “food and drink” (6.209, 6.246, 6.248; see [[xenia]]). The accusatives are written alike, πόσιν. The husband is the Indo-European word for “lord” (see the article above); the drink is a noun from πίνω, “drink” (LSJ s.v. πόσις B). The entry's patterns now exclude πόσιν followed by τε, the shape of the drink formula.

**The wish.** After Athena has made Odysseus taller and poured grace on him, Nausicaa speaks to her handmaids, with Odysseus sitting apart on the shore (6.236–245; see [[charis]]):

{{quote:Od. 6.242-245 | Nausicaa's wish}}

τοιόσδε πόσις κεκλημένος εἴη, “such a man might be called my husband”: Merry and Riddell compare Odysseus' oath “may I no longer be called the father of Telemachus” (*Iliad* 2.260) and Hera's “I am called your wife” (σὴ παράκοιτις κέκλημαι, *Iliad* 4.60), and note that οἱ in the next line turns the general wish toward Odysseus himself (note on 6.244, p. 274). She wishes for a husband “of this kind”, not yet for this man, and for one who would stay in Scheria. The poem has already told why marriage is on her mind. Athena, in a dream, told her that her marriage is near and that the best of the Phaeacians are courting her (6.27–35; see [[marriage]]). With her father she “felt shame to name the marriage” (6.66; see [[aidos]]). With her handmaids, and out of Odysseus' hearing, she names it.

**The athetesis.** The scholia report that Aristarchus athetized both lines, 6.244 and 6.245, and that he hesitated over the first, because the lyric poet Alcman had used it: Alcman brings on maidens who say “Father Zeus, if only he might be my husband” (schol. H.Q. on 6.244–245, Dindorf p. 314; see [[aristarchus]]). The scholion gives no reason for the athetesis. Another scholion says the words seem unfitting for a maiden and licentious, and that some critics explained them by the luxurious life of the Phaeacians; it reports that Ephorus praised the speech as coming from a soul well disposed to virtue (schol. E.T. and Q.T. on 6.244, Dindorf p. 314). Plutarch makes the judgment conditional. If Nausicaa says this because she has fallen for a stranger, as Calypso did, she is to be blamed for boldness and lack of restraint. If she has seen the man's character in his words and prays for such a husband rather than a sailor or a dancer of her own people, she deserves admiration (*How the Young Man Should Listen to Poems* 27A–B). The base text, like the Oxford edition of 1908 and Eustathius, keeps both lines.

**Husband in the gossip.** On the way back to the city Nausicaa imagines what a base Phaeacian would say if he saw her with Odysseus: “And who is this following Nausicaa, a handsome and tall / stranger? And where did she find him? Surely he will be a husband for her” (πόσις νύ οἱ ἔσσεται αὐτῇ, 6.276–277). The speaker goes on to mock: “It is better so, if she herself has gone about and found a husband / from elsewhere; for truly she dishonors these men here among the people, / the Phaeacians, who are courting her, many and good” (6.282–284; see [[phemis]]). Merry and Riddell call the speech “bitterly sarcastical” (note on 6.282, p. 277). The townsman says aloud what Nausicaa wished in private. In the gossip, a husband found by the girl herself and from outside is a dishonor to the local suitors. Nausicaa herself blames a girl who “mingles with men before she has come to a public marriage” (ἀμφάδιον γάμον, 6.288; see [[nemesis]]). The scholia record that some critics athetized the whole passage 6.275–288 as unfitting for her character (schol. H.Q. on 6.275, Dindorf p. 317).

In Book 7 her father makes the same wish in the same terms. Alcinous prays that a man such as Odysseus might “have my daughter and be called my son-in-law, staying here” (καλέεσθαι / αὖθι μένων, 7.311–314), as Nausicaa wished that such a man might “be called” her husband and stay (κεκλημένος, αὐτόθι μίμνειν, 6.244–245). Neither wish is fulfilled: Odysseus goes home to his own wife.
