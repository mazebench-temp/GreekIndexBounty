summary: Odysseus asks for pity twice in Book 6, once from Nausicaa (“queen, have pity”) and once from Athena, whom he asks to let him reach the Phaeacians “dear and pitied”. Nausicaa answers the first request with acts, without the word.
grc: [ἐλεεινόν]
en: [have pity, pitied]
passages:
  - 6.175-179 | “But, queen, have pity”
  - 6.327 | Dear and pitied
---

**The request to Nausicaa.** After the praise and the account of his twenty days at sea, Odysseus turns to the request with ἀλλά, ἄνασσʼ, ἐλέαιρε, “But, queen, have pity” (6.175). The reason follows: she is the first person he has come to after many evils, and he knows no one else in the land (6.175–177). The pity he asks for is practical. He wants a guide to the town and a rag to wear (6.178–179; see [[supplication]]). ἐλεαίρω is the verb with which the weaker ask the stronger, as in the Iliad (see the article above).

**The answer without the word.** Nausicaa does not say that she pities him. She calls him “some unhappy man (δύστηνος), a wanderer”, says that “we must care for him now”, and gives the orders for food, drink and a bath (6.206–210; see [[strangers-and-beggars-are-from-zeus]]). Her reason is not her feeling but the rule that strangers and beggars are from Zeus.

**Dear and pitied.** At sunset in Athena's grove Odysseus asks the goddess for the same thing from the whole people: “Grant that I may come to the Phaeacians as one dear to them and pitied” (φίλον … ἠδʼ ἐλεεινόν, 6.327). φίλος, “dear”, and ἐλεεινός, “pitied”, name the two things a suppliant needs from those he comes to: friendship and pity. The verse and its use by Aristotle are treated in [[dear-and-pitied]] and [[prayer]]. Nausicaa, and Athena in Book 7, tell him that his homecoming depends on whether the queen is “minded in friendship” toward him (6.313–315, 7.75–77).
