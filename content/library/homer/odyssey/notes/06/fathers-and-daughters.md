The Iliad's fathers and daughters are mostly captives, priests and gods. Book 6 of the Odyssey gives the first full scene in Homer between a king and his unmarried daughter at home.

**The parents.** Nausicaa wakes from the dream and goes to tell her parents (6.50–51). The two are placed in their spheres. Her mother sits at the hearth with her women and spins sea-purple wool. Her father is going out to the council of the kings (6.52–55; see [[queen-arete]] and [[alcinous]]). Nausicaa goes to her father.

{{quote:Od. 6.56-70 | Papa dear}}

**“Papa dear”.** She stands very close and begins πάππα φίλʼ, “Papa dear” (6.57). LSJ calls πάππας “papa, child's word for father; mostly in voc.” and cites this line first (s.v. πάππας). The word occurs nowhere else in Homer. A scholion calls it a lisping sound, an affectionate address of a younger person to an older. Another scholion lists the words of this kind, which are used in speaking to a person and never in speaking about him: τέττα to a friend, ἄττα to a foster-father, θεῖε to an uncle, and πάππα to a father (Dindorf pp. 298–299, on 6.57).

**The reasons.** Her request is phrased as a question: “would you not make ready for me a carriage?” (6.57). She names the reasons that concern the men of the family. He should sit in council in clean clothes, and her five brothers want freshly washed clothes for the dance, “and all these things are a care to my mind” (6.60–65; see [[washing-clothes]] and [[sons-of-alcinous]]). A scholion notes the change from the dream: Athena spoke openly of the marriage, but Nausicaa shifts the need to the clothes of her father and brothers. The scholion adds that her words still carry a hint of the truth, because she is of an age to marry (schol. P.Q.T. on 6.60, Dindorf p. 299).

**What she does not say.** The narrator gives the reason for her silence: “for she felt shame to name the marriage of her prime / to her dear father” (αἴδετο γὰρ θαλερὸν γάμον ἐξονομῆναι, 6.66; see [[aidos]] and [[marriage]]). Then: “But he understood everything” (ὁ δὲ πάντα νόει, 6.67). The father's answer grants more than the wagon: “I do not grudge you the mules, child, nor anything else” (6.68). He does not name the marriage either. He gives the order to the slaves, and the wagon is made ready (6.69–73).

**The rule of the parents.** At the end of the book Nausicaa states the rule by which she judges other girls. She would blame a girl who, “against the will of her dear father and mother, while they are living”, mingles with men before a public marriage (6.286–288). Merry and Riddell take the point to be that such a girl has parents to guide her and so has no excuse (note on 6.287, p. 278). The rule makes the father's consent, and the mother's, the condition of a marriage. It explains her fear of the town's gossip if she enters the city with the stranger (6.273–285; see [[praise-and-blame]]).

**Her father's house.** In her directions to Odysseus the father is the king on his throne, who “sits and drinks his wine like an immortal” (6.308–309). Odysseus is to pass him by and supplicate the mother (6.310–315; see [[supplication]]). In Book 7 Alcinous shows the other side of the father. He blames his daughter for not bringing the stranger home herself, and Odysseus takes the blame on himself (7.299–307). Then he wishes that such a man would marry her and stay (7.311–315).
