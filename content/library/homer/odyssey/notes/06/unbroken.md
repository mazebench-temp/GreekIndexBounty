summary: The narrator twice calls Nausicaa παρθένος ἀδμής, “the unwed maiden” (6.109, 6.228). The word is the one used for a heifer or a mule that has never been yoked.
grc: [ἀδμής]
en: [unwed]
---

ἀδμής means “untamed, unbroken”, from δάμνημι, “to tame”. In the Odyssey it describes a yearling heifer “which no man has yet led under the yoke” (3.382–383) and twelve mares with their unbroken mules (4.636–637). In Book 6 the narrator gives it twice to Nausicaa, in the phrase παρθένος ἀδμής, “the unwed maiden”, at the end of the verse.

- **6.109.** It closes the Artemis simile: “so she stood out among her handmaids, the unwed maiden” ([[artemis-among-her-nymphs]]). Artemis is the virgin goddess, and the word marks the girl as unmarried like her.
- **6.228.** Odysseus puts on “the clothes that the unwed maiden had given him” (6.228). The clothes are hers to give, and her marriage has been the subject of the book since the dream (6.27–35).

The same book is full of the language of the yoke. The mules are yoked and unyoked at each stage of the journey (6.73, 6.88, 6.111, 6.253). The word describes a girl who is near the end of her maidenhood: Athena told her that she would “not be a maiden much longer” (6.33). See [[parthenos]], [[nausicaa]] and [[marriage]].
