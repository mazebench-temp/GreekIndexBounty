summary: The book opens and closes its account of Nausicaa with public talk. In the dream, fine clothes win her a “good report”; at the edge of the city, she shuns the “unkind talk” that a stranger at her side would start.

**A good report.** Athena comes to Nausicaa in a dream in the likeness of a friend and urges her to wash the clothes. Her marriage is near, and she must have fine clothes for herself and for the bridal party. Then she gives the reason:

{{quote:bafa2490-0abf-5062-99cb-bfd5fddb926f}}

The talk is good here, and it is about the bride's household and its goods. The joy of father and mother follows it. Merry and Riddell explain the verb: the report “spreads” among men, rising step by step from those in the procession to “the public”. They note that ἀναβαίνει with this sense has no exact parallel (note on 6.29, p. 254). The scholia record a variant. Callistratus read χάρις (“delight”) for φάτις, in the sense of χαρά, “joy”, and said that Aristophanes of Byzantium had altered the word to φάτις (schol. H.P. on 6.29, Dindorf p. 296).

**Unkind talk.** At the edge of the city Nausicaa tells Odysseus to drop behind, and she gives her reason in a long speech (6.273–288). She shuns their “unkind talk” (φῆμιν ἀδευκέα, 6.273), “so that no one afterward may blame me” (μή τις ὀπίσσω μωμεύῃ, 6.273–274). Merry and Riddell take ὀπίσσω as “hereafter”, not “behind my back”, and cite 2.179 (note on 6.273, p. 277). The adjective ἀδευκής is rare. Homer has it three times, all in the Odyssey: here, of a death (ὀλέθρῳ ἀδευκέι, 4.489), and of a doom (ἀδευκέα πότμον, 10.245). The ancient explanations differ. The scholia give “not sweet”, so “bitter”, from γλεῦκος (“sweet new wine”); or “unexpected”, from a verb δεύκω “see” (schol. on 6.273, Dindorf pp. 316–317). LSJ translates the phrase as “bitter gossip”.

Then she acts the gossip out (see [[tis-speech]]). “Some baser man” (τις … κακώτερος, 6.275) meets them and speaks:

{{quote:8a7d3247-859b-52fb-b66c-460106685a7d}}

The imagined speaker asks who the handsome stranger is and where she found him, and he guesses that the man will be her husband (see [[posis]]). He suggests that he is a castaway, or a god come down in answer to her prayers, and that she scorns the many good Phaeacians who court her (see [[xenia]] and [[god-or-mortal]]). Nausicaa closes: “So they will say, and these things would become reproaches against me” (6.285; see [[oneidos]]). She adds that she herself would blame another girl who did such things (6.286–288; see [[nemesis]]). Her fear of talk agrees with her own judgment of others.

Some ancient critics found the speech unfitting. A scholion reports that fourteen lines, from 6.275 to 6.288, were marked as spurious, because they do not suit the character of the speaker and because 6.273–274 have already said the same thing (schol. H.Q. on 6.275, Dindorf p. 317).

**The pattern.** The talk of the people constrains the women of the poem. Penelope stays in the house “respecting the bed of her husband and the talk of the people” (16.75 = 19.527). Helen in the Iliad will not go to Paris, because the Trojan women would blame her (*Iliad* 3.411–412). The suitors, too, fear what “some baser man” would say (21.323–324). That half-verse, μή ποτέ τις εἴπῃσι κακώτερος, is close to Nausicaa's καί νύ τις ὧδʼ εἴπῃσι κακώτερος (6.275).
