---
summary: Nausicaa answers the castaway's plea with a maxim. Zeus deals out prosperity to good and bad as he wishes, so Odysseus' misery says nothing against his worth, and he must endure it.
passages:
  - 6.187-190 | Zeus deals out prosperity
---

Odysseus has prayed that the gods may give Nausicaa a husband, a home and like-mindedness (6.180–185). She begins her answer with a judgment of him and a maxim:

{{quote:47b5e8f8-2b7c-55d6-870c-b0fe68248da2}}

The verse names [[zeus]] as the one who “deals out” (νέμει) ὄλβος (see also [[zeus-deals-out-prosperity]]), and it adds two qualifications: “to the good and the bad” and “as he wishes, to each one”. Merry and Riddell put the point in the words ὅπως ἐθέλῃσιν, “the arbitrary dispensation of good and evil”. They compare Helen's words that Zeus gives good and evil “now to one, now to another” (4.236–237), and the two jars of Zeus in the Iliad (*Iliad* 24.527–533; note on 6.188–189, p. 269). Telemachus says nearly the same thing of the singer's sad song: Zeus “gives to grain-eating men, as he wishes, to each one” (1.348–349; see [[alphestes]]).

The ancient commentators read the line in two ways. One scholion takes it to mean that Zeus mixes evils with goods and deals them out to each “according to merit” (πρὸς ἀξίαν). The other takes it to mean that the distribution rests with the god and that good and evil do not come to each man according to his virtue or his wickedness (schol. P.V. and E.Q. on 6.189, Dindorf p. 310).

The maxim serves Nausicaa's argument. Merry and Riddell explain the sentence from Ζεύς to ἔμπης as a parenthesis: he looks neither base nor senseless, only wretched, and wretchedness, like prosperity, is dealt out by the will of the gods (note on 6.187, p. 269). The conclusion follows in 6.191–193: since he has come to their land, he will lack neither clothing nor anything else that a suppliant should have (see [[xenia]]).

The last half-line, σὲ δὲ χρὴ τετλάμεν ἔμπης, “you must endure them all the same”, recurs nearly unchanged when Telemachus speaks of himself at Pylos, in a sentence with the same noun: “the gods have not spun such prosperity for me … but now I must endure all the same” (3.208–209). The verb τετλάμεν, “endure”, has the root of Odysseus' epithet πολύτλας, “much-enduring” (6.1, 6.249).
