---
summary: In the lion simile the hungry lion goes “after the wild deer” (6.133).
grc: [ἀγροτέρας]
---

Odysseus comes out of the thicket “like a mountain-bred lion”, which “goes among the cattle or the sheep / or after the wild deer; and his belly bids him / make an attempt on the sheep, and go even into the close-built fold” (6.130–134). The three kinds of prey run from the farm to the wild. See [[the-lion-and-the-girls]], [[deer]] and [[gaster]].

A scholion explains ἀγροτέρας ἐλάφους as deer “that graze in the fields”, or as ἀγηροτέρας, “longer-lived”, “for the animal lives long” (schol. P, Q, T on 6.133, Dindorf p. 305). The second explanation is an ancient folk etymology. The same book has deer in the Artemis simile: Artemis goes “delighting in wild boars and swift deer” (6.104; [[artemis-among-her-nymphs]]).
