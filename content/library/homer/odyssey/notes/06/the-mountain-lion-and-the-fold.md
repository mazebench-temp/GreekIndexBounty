---
summary: Two verses of Sarpedon's lion return in Odyssey 6, where Odysseus comes out of the thicket toward Nausicaa and her handmaids; the lion's opening (6.130 ~ Iliad 12.299) and the verse of the attempt on the close-built fold (6.134 = Iliad 12.301). In the Odyssey the lion is driven by its belly, not by its proud spirit.
refs: [6.130, 6.134]
---

βῆ δʼ ἴμεν ὥς τε λέων ὀρεσίτροφος, “And he went on like a mountain-bred lion” (6.130), begins as the Iliad's simile begins, with δʼ for ῥʼ (Iliad 12.299). The verse ends differently, ἀλκὶ πεποιθώς, “trusting in his courage”, the ending of another Iliadic lion (Iliad 17.61). The third verse of Sarpedon's simile then returns whole: μήλων πειρήσοντα καὶ ἐς πυκινὸν δόμον ἐλθεῖν, “make an attempt on the sheep, and go even into the close-built fold” (6.134 = Iliad 12.301). The translation keeps the Iliad's English for it.

{{quote:f184502e-9a0f-5afd-9689-ad3b3721b3e2}}

The subject of “bids” is changed. In the Iliad it is θυμὸς ἀγήνωρ, “his proud spirit” (Iliad 12.300); in the Odyssey it is γαστήρ, “his belly” (6.133; see [[gaster]]). The herdsmen with dogs and spears, and the choice between seizing a sheep and being struck among the foremost (Iliad 12.302–306), have no place in the Odyssey's version, which ends with the man's need: “for need had come upon him” (6.136). The Odyssey's simile has its own entry, [[the-lion-and-the-girls]].
