---
summary: Four times in Book 6 the girls on the shore are “with lovely tresses”, always in the dative plural ἐυπλοκάμοισι(ν), twice of the girls whom the naked Odysseus must approach and twice of the handmaids whom Nausicaa addresses.
grc: [ἐϋπλοκάμοισι, ἐϋπλοκάμοισιν]
---

The epithet has one form in Book 6, the dative plural, and it always closes a phrase with a noun for the girls. Two uses go with κούρῃσιν, “girls”, and both concern Odysseus' nakedness. At the end of the lion simile the narrator says that “Odysseus was about to go among the girls with lovely tresses / and mingle with them, naked though he was” (6.135–136; [[the-lion-and-the-girls]]). Later Odysseus himself uses the same words when he sends the handmaids away: he is ashamed “to strip naked when I have come among girls with lovely tresses” (6.221–222; [[aidos]]). The narrator's phrase passes into the hero's own speech, and the nakedness that the simile left unspoken becomes his reason for modesty.

{{quote:Od. 6.135-136 | Naked among the girls}}

The other two uses go with ἀμφιπόλοισιν, “handmaids”, in the narrator's lines that introduce Nausicaa's orders: she “ordered her handmaids with lovely tresses” (6.198) and “spoke among her handmaids with lovely tresses” (6.238). Both orders concern the stranger's care: to stand still and give him food, drink and a bath (6.199–210), and to give him food and drink again (6.239–246). See [[handmaids-of-nausicaa]] and [[nausicaa]].

The girls' hair is visible in this book for a reason the narrative gives: they threw off their veils (κρήδεμνα) to play ball (6.100). Elsewhere in the Odyssey the singular ἐϋπλόκαμος belongs mostly to goddesses and nymphs, for example Calypso (5.58), Circe (10.136) and Dawn (5.390). In Book 6 the epithet is given only to mortal girls of the household.
