summary: Odysseus' second “thrice blessed” in two books. On the raft he called the dead at Troy thrice blessed; on the beach he calls Nausicaa's parents and brothers thrice blessed for the sight of her, and her future husband the most blessed of all.

{{quote:Od. 6.153-159 | Thrice blessed}}

**The order.** The blessing follows the second half of Odysseus' question: “but if you are one of the mortals who dwell on the earth” (6.153; see [[god-or-mortal]]). The three groups are named in rising order. Her father and lady mother are “thrice blessed” (6.154), her brothers “thrice blessed” (6.155), and the man who will take her home is μακάρτατος ἔξοχον ἄλλων, “most blessed … far above all others” (6.158). δέ stands third in τρὶς μάκαρες δὲ κασίγνητοι (6.155), after the two words that are repeated from the verse before.

**The reasons.** The family is blessed because of what it sees: “surely their hearts / are always warmed with gladness because of you, / as they watch such a young shoot going into the dance” (6.155–157). The mother's joy repeats Leto's in the simile, “and Leto is glad in her heart” (6.106; see [[artemis-among-her-nymphs]]), and the dance is the one the narrator has just shown (6.101; see [[dance]]). The young shoot leads to the palm of Delos (6.162–169; see [[the-palm-shoot-on-delos]]). The husband is blessed for what he will do: “who, prevailing by the weight of his bride-gifts, leads you home” (6.159; see [[bride-price]] and [[marriage]]). A scholion calls this “very flattering”: it shows her as a prize that men will fight for (schol. P.Q. on 6.159, Dindorf p. 307).

**What Odysseus knows.** He has seen her only at play and standing her ground. The praise of the family names what a girl's rank implies: parents, brothers, suitors. It also meets what is on her mind, though he cannot know it: Athena has told her in the dream that her marriage is near and that the best of the Phaeacians are courting her (6.27, 6.33–35), and she has felt shame to name marriage to her father (6.66). Nausicaa does have brothers; her father has five sons (6.62–63; see [[sons-of-alcinous]]).

**The two blessings.** On the raft, in the storm of Book 5, Odysseus called the Greeks who fell at Troy “thrice blessed and four times” and wished he had died with them (5.306–311). In Book 6 the same words open his praise of a living girl, the first person he has met on land.
