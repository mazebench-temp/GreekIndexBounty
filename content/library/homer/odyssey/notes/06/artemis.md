summary: Artemis is the model for Nausicaa twice in Book 6. The narrator compares the girl among her handmaids to Artemis among her nymphs on the mountains (6.102–109), and Odysseus, unsure whether he speaks to a goddess or a mortal, likens her to Artemis in looks, stature and build (6.149–152).

**The simile.** After the meal the girls play ball, and Nausicaa leads the song and dance (6.99–101; see [[ball-play]] and [[dance]]).

{{quote:e782403c-e446-5763-80f0-de37e83064c1}}

The goddess moves through the mountains, ἰοχέαιρα, “of the showering arrows” (6.102; see [[of-the-showering-arrows]]), along [[taygetus]] in Laconia or [[erymanthus]] in Arcadia, “delighting in wild boars and swift deer” (6.103–104; see [[boar]] and [[deer]]). The [[nymphs]], daughters of Zeus, play with her, and her mother [[leto]] is glad in her heart (6.105–106). Artemis holds her head above them all and is “easily known, though all are beautiful” (6.107–108; see [[easily-known]]). So Nausicaa “stood out among her handmaids, the unwed maiden” (παρθένος ἀδμής, 6.109; see [[parthenos]] and [[unbroken]]). See [[artemis-among-her-nymphs]] for the simile as a whole.

The details correspond. The girls have thrown off their veils and play like the nymphs; Nausicaa leads as Artemis does; Leto's joy anticipates the joy that Odysseus will wish on Nausicaa's parents (6.154–157). A scholion calls the comparison “exact in every respect” (κατὰ πάντα ἀπαράλλακτος). The scholia on the place names say that Taygetus and Erymanthus are full of boars and deer, and that the poet does not name lions because none are born there, as Aristotle testifies. One scholion reads Leto as an allegory of night, mother of the moon (Artemis) and the sun (Apollo). The simile also sets the scene for the next event. Artemis' world is the wild country, and from its thicket a wild man is about to come out among the girls “like a mountain-bred lion” (6.130–136; see [[the-lion-and-the-girls]]).

The scholia record two readings in the simile. A scholion (H.P.) prefers κατʼ οὔρεα, “through the mountains”, the reading Murray prints, to κατʼ οὔρεος. At 6.106 the ancient grammarian Megaclides read a different line: ἀγρόμεναι παίζουσιν ἀνὰ δρία παιπαλόεντα, “gathered together, they play through the rugged thickets”.

**Odysseus' comparison.** Odysseus begins his supplication with a question: “are you some goddess, or a mortal?” (6.149; see [[god-or-mortal]]).

{{quote:6c7ac194-62d6-5913-aff1-9c58cee4e1f7}}

He names Artemis, “the daughter of great Zeus”, among the gods “who hold the wide sky”, and says that he likens Nausicaa to her “most closely, in looks and stature and build” (εἶδός τε μέγεθός τε φυήν τε). A scholion notes that the praise is made of three things: beauty, size and good condition of body, because φυή is the proportion of all the limbs. Another scholion observes that Odysseus speaks with care: to address her outright as a goddess would raise suspicion of flattery, so he leaves the question open and puts the goddess first, as if he leans that way. Odysseus does not know the narrator's simile, but his choice repeats it. The reader has just seen Nausicaa as an Artemis, and now Odysseus sees the same.

The comparison fits Nausicaa's situation. Athena's dream turned on Nausicaa's coming marriage (6.27, 6.33–35), the simile ends on παρθένος ἀδμής (6.109), and Odysseus moves straight from Artemis to the man who will marry her (6.158–159; see [[marriage]]). Homer does not call Artemis a virgin. The pairing of the goddess and the unmarried girl is made by the context.

**Later imitation.** Virgil compares Dido entering the temple at Carthage, surrounded by young men, to Diana leading her dances by the Eurotas or on Cynthus (*Aeneid* 1.498–504). Aulus Gellius reports the criticism of the grammarian Valerius Probus, that nothing in Virgil is so unhappily taken from Homer. Nausicaa playing among her girls in a lonely place suits Diana hunting among the wild goddesses, but Dido walking among the Tyrian leaders does not. Probus also says that Virgil left out the best line of the passage, that Artemis is “easily known, though all are beautiful” (*Attic Nights* 9.9.12–17).
