**The question.** The shout of the girls wakes Odysseus, and his first words test the land by the standard of hospitality: are the people “arrogant and savage and not just, / or are they kind to strangers, and is their mind god-fearing?” (6.119–121; see [[to-the-land-of-what-mortals]]).

{{quote:ab47a82e-3ebb-5ad8-a570-f31de9b49b18}}

φιλόξεινος (“kind to strangers”, literally “friend of strangers”) is the positive term. Merry and Riddell read νόος θεουδής, “a god-fearing mind”, as an explanation of it: the people who are kind to strangers are those who fear the anger of Zeus, god of guests (note on 6.121, p. 263). The three lines come back when Odysseus lands on Ithaca and does not know it (13.200–202). The last two are spoken before he enters the Cyclops' cave (9.175–176), and Alcinous asks him to tell which peoples he met were of each kind (8.575–576). On Scheria the answer comes in Books 6 to 8; in the Cyclops' cave it is the opposite one.

**The stranger.** Nausicaa addresses him as ξεῖνε, “stranger”, three times (6.187, 6.255, 6.289), and the translation keeps “stranger” for ξεῖνος in this book (see the table in the translation notes). He comes with nothing. He asks for no more than to be shown the town and given a rag to wear (6.178–179). Nausicaa promises more: he will lack nothing “of what befits a much-tried suppliant who has met with people” (6.192–193). The stranger who arrives in need is also a ἱκέτης, a suppliant, and he has just begged at a distance instead of clasping her knees (6.141–148; see [[supplication]]). The poem joins the two words again when Alcinous says that “a stranger and a suppliant takes the place of a brother” (8.546).

**From Zeus.** Nausicaa turns to her fleeing handmaids and gives the reason for the reception (6.206–210):

{{quote:869fa133-9198-58ce-a931-3bb72ec459b6}}

The scholia explain πρὸς Διός as “from Zeus”: strangers and beggars are under his protection and he pities them (schol. E.H.P.Q. on 6.207, Dindorf p. 312). Merry and Riddell compare the Zeus “who accompanies suppliants, who are to be respected” (7.165; note on 6.207, p. 271). Eumaeus speaks the same verse to Odysseus disguised as a beggar (14.57–58; see [[strangers-and-beggars-are-from-zeus]] and [[ptochos]]). δόσις δʼ ὀλίγη τε φίλη τε, “a small gift is a dear one”, is explained in the scholia as small for the giver and dear to the one who receives it (schol. B.E.P. on 6.208, Dindorf p. 312). Achilles uses the same pair, ὀλίγον τε φίλον τε, of his own small share of the spoils (*Iliad* 1.167).

**The reception.** Nausicaa orders food and drink and a bath in the river (6.209–210). The handmaids set out a cloak and a tunic and give him oil in a golden flask (6.214–216). Everything is done at a river and by girls. Odysseus will not be bathed by them. He sends the handmaids away and washes himself, because, as he says, “I feel shame / to strip naked when I have come among girls with lovely tresses” (6.218–222; see [[aidos]] and [[bathing]]). Only after the bath and Athena's gift of grace does Nausicaa repeat the order for food and drink (6.246 = 6.209), and he eats “greedily; for he had long been without a taste of food” (6.248–250; see [[gaster]]). The line 6.209 is a formula of hospitality: Nausicaa's order and its fulfilment use the same half-verse βρῶσίν τε πόσιν τε, “food and drink” (6.209, 6.246, 6.248).

**The house of the host.** A guest is received in the house of its master. Nausicaa is an unmarried daughter at the washing pools, and she will not be seen entering the city with a strange man (6.273–288; see [[phemis]]). So her part is to send him on: “Rise now, stranger, to go to the city, so that I may send you / to the house of my wise-hearted father” (6.255–256). There he is to clasp the knees of her mother, so that he may “see the day of homecoming” (6.310–315). The reception in the house comes in Books 7 and 8: Alcinous raises him from the hearth to a chair, a meal is set before him (7.167–176), and the Phaeacians give him gifts. Its last gift is the [[pompe|πομπή]], the escort home that Nausicaa promises from her father (6.290).

**The stranger as a topic of talk.** In Nausicaa's imagined gossip the word stands first in the verse, with the question that follows: “a handsome and tall / stranger? And where did she find him?” (6.276–277). Merry and Riddell note the emphatic position (note on 6.277, p. 277). For the Phaeacian speaker a ξεῖνος is a man from outside who might take a local girl as wife (6.277–284). The Phaeacians, Nausicaa says, live “far apart” and “no other mortal mingles with us” (6.204–205). In the next book Athena warns Odysseus that the people “do not much put up with strangers” (7.32–33). Nausicaa's promise and Athena's warning stand side by side.
