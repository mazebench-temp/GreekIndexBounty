summary: Book 6 is Nausicaa's book. Athena sends her a dream of her coming marriage; she asks her father for the wagon, washes the clothes and leads the ball game; when the naked stranger comes out of the thicket she alone stands her ground, gives him food, a bath and clothes, wishes for a husband like him, and plans his way into the city so that no one will talk.

**The dream.** Athena goes to the house of Alcinous to plan Odysseus' return (6.13–14), and the plan starts with a sleeping girl. Nausicaa sleeps in an intricately worked chamber, “like the immortal goddesses in build and in looks”, with two handmaids at the doorposts (6.15–19; see [[handmaids-of-nausicaa]]). Athena comes to her bed “like a breath of wind” and stands above her head in the likeness of the daughter of Dymas, a girl of her own age and her friend (6.20–24; see [[daughter-of-dymas]], [[like-a-breath-of-wind]] and [[dream-scene]]). The goddess speaks in the voice of a friend who teases:

{{quote:2d2f0962-a5f2-5167-b3cd-f993d5327e04}}

Her clothes lie uncared for, and her marriage is near. A good report of a girl rises from such things, and her father and mother rejoice in it (6.29–30; see [[phemis]]). “The best men throughout the land are courting you, of all the Phaeacians” (6.34–35). Athena promises to come along “as a fellow worker” (συνέριθος) and tells her to ask her father at dawn for the mules and the wagon (6.31–40). Nausicaa wakes and “immediately she wondered at the dream” (6.49; see [[onar]]). The dream gives her two things: an errand that takes her to the shore where Odysseus sleeps, and the thought of marriage that colors everything she says to him.

**Papa dear.** She finds her mother at the hearth with the handmaids, spinning sea-purple wool, and her father on his way to the council (6.50–55). She asks him with the child's word πάππα, “Papa dear” (6.57), for a high wagon with good wheels to take the clothes to the river. Her reasons are all for others: he should sit in council in clean clothes, and her five brothers always want freshly washed clothes for the dance (6.57–65; see [[sons-of-alcinous]]). Merry and Riddell hear “a tone of sportive seriousness” in her claim that all this weighs on her mind (on 6.65, p. 258). The narrator gives the real reason:

{{quote:72608f54-21c7-5656-9a43-f39e74f1440a}}

αἴδετο, “she felt shame”, is the first of the book's three uses of αἰδώς: hers, then Odysseus' shame to stand naked before the girls (6.221), then Athena's respect for Poseidon (6.329; see [[aidos]]). θαλερὸν γάμον is literally “the blooming marriage”, the marriage of her youth. The father grants everything without a word on the subject (6.68–70; see [[alcinous]] and [[fathers-and-daughters]]). Her mother packs food, relishes and wine, and gives her oil in a golden flask for after the bath. Nausicaa takes the whip and the reins herself (6.76–84; see [[queen-arete]] and [[chariot-journey]]).

**At the river.** The girls tread the clothes in the washing trenches, “bringing on a rivalry” (ἔριδα προφέρουσαι, 6.92), spread them on the shingle, bathe, eat, and throw off their veils to play at ball (6.85–100; see [[washing-clothes]] and [[ball-play]]). “White-armed Nausicaa began the song and dance” (ἤρχετο μολπῆς, 6.101; see [[dance]]). The narrator compares her to Artemis among her nymphs on Taygetus or Erymanthus: the goddess holds her head above them all, “and she is easily known, though all are beautiful” (6.102–109; see [[artemis-among-her-nymphs]] and [[artemis]]). The comparison sets her apart, and it names the goddess of unmarried girls. She is παρθένος ἀδμής, “the unwed maiden” (6.109), the phrase that returns when she has given Odysseus clothes (6.228; see [[parthenos]]).

**The ball.** As they are about to go home, Athena makes her next move: Odysseus must wake and see the girl, who will lead him to the city (6.110–114). The princess throws the ball to a handmaid, misses, and the ball falls into a deep eddy; the girls shout, and Odysseus wakes (6.115–117). The poem calls her βασίλεια, “princess”, at this moment (6.115; see [[basileus]]). Merry and Riddell read ἔπειτα, “so then”, as the first step in carrying out Athena's plan (on 6.115, p. 262).

**She stands.** The man who comes out of the bushes is naked but for a leafy branch. “Terrible he appeared to them, marred by the brine” (6.137), and the narrator has just compared him to a hungry mountain lion going after the sheep (6.127–137; see [[the-lion-and-the-girls]]). The handmaids scatter along the beach.

{{quote:ba260eff-d6de-56b2-8327-54d73f6d7f97}}

Her courage is a gift of Athena, as the courage of heroes is in the Iliad. Odysseus decides not to clasp her knees, lest the girl be angry, and to entreat her from a distance with “soothing words” (6.141–148; see [[supplication]] and [[soothing-words]]). His speech is built for a girl who has just dreamed of marriage. Is she a goddess, Artemis herself? If mortal, her parents and brothers are thrice blessed, “but that man in turn is most blessed in his heart, far above all others, who, prevailing by the weight of his bride-gifts, leads you home” (6.149–159; see [[god-or-mortal]], [[thrice-blessed]] and [[bride-price]]). He compares her to a young palm shoot he once saw on Delos (6.162–169; see [[the-palm-shoot-on-delos]]), asks for a rag and the way to the town, and ends with a wish for her: “a husband and a home”, and like-mindedness in marriage (6.175–185; see [[homophrosyne]] and [[odysseus]]).

**Her answer.** She answers “face to face” (ἀντίον, 6.186):

{{quote:458f2ede-61a2-5484-b9f5-d8b355c9c8cf}}

The sentence breaks off after its first clause: “since you seem like neither a base man nor a senseless one”, and then a reflection on Zeus intervenes before the main clause arrives at 6.192 (Merry and Riddell on 6.187, p. 269; see [[anacoluthon]] and [[zeus-deals-out-prosperity]]). She judges him from his words, as he judged her. She promises clothing and all that “befits a much-tried suppliant” (ἱκέτην ταλαπείριον, 6.193; see [[much-tried]]), and she gives him what he asked for, the name of the land and her own place in it: “The Phaeacians hold this city and land, / and I am the daughter of great-hearted Alcinous, / on whom the power and the strength of the Phaeacians depend” (6.195–197; see [[phaeacians]]).

**To the handmaids.** She calls back the girls: “Where are you fleeing at the sight of a man?” No enemy can reach the Phaeacians, “for we are very dear to the immortals”, and they live “far apart in the much-surging sea, the farthest away” (6.199–205). This is a wanderer, “and we must care for him now; for from Zeus are all / strangers and beggars, and a small gift is a dear one” (6.206–208; see [[strangers-and-beggars-are-from-zeus]] and [[xenia]]). She orders food, drink and a bath (6.209–210), and the handmaids lay out a cloak and a tunic and the oil (6.211–216). When Odysseus asks the handmaids to stand off while he bathes, they go and tell her (6.218–223).

**The wish.** Athena makes him taller and pours grace on him, “and the girl gazed at him” (θηεῖτο δὲ κούρη, 6.237; see [[divine-beautification]]). She speaks to the handmaids, not to him. Before, he seemed unsightly; now he is like the gods:

{{quote:068d4782-a96a-5455-a93d-c859f3bf9597}}

Ancient critics argued about these lines. Aristarchus athetized both and hesitated over the first, because Alcman had used it; another scholion found the words unfitting for a maiden, and reported that Ephorus praised them (schol. on 6.244–245, Dindorf p. 314; see [[nausicaas-wish-244-245]]). Plutarch judged them conditionally: blame, if she spoke from desire for a stranger; admiration, if she judged his character by his words and preferred him to the sailors and dancers of her own people (*How the Young Man Should Study Poetry* 27a–b; see [[plutarch-on-nausicaas-wish]]). Murray prints both lines. The wish uses πόσις, “husband” (see [[posis]]); the imagined gossip a few lines later will use the same word of him (6.277, 6.282). Her father voices the same wish in Book 7 (7.311–316).

**The plan.** She folds the clothes, yokes the mules, and tells the stranger how to come into the city (6.251–315). She calls him by name in the narrator's formula, though she does not know his name (ἔκ τʼ ὀνόμαζεν, 6.254; see [[called-him-by-name]]). Through the fields he is to walk with the handmaids behind the wagon; she will lead the way (6.258–261). At the city wall the plan changes, and she explains why with a description of the city she will not let him enter beside her: the two harbors, the ships drawn up along the road, the place of assembly around the shrine of Poseidon, a people who care for masts and oars and not for bows (6.262–272; see [[scheria]] and [[phaeacians]]).

{{quote:26581f67-f570-51d5-8756-2c981f130fa0}}

She then speaks the gossip in the man's own voice (6.276–284; see [[tis-speech]]). He would ask who this tall, handsome stranger is, guess that she found a castaway or that a god came down at her prayer, and conclude that she scorns the Phaeacians who court her, “many and good”. The last line repeats what Athena told her in the dream: the best men are courting her (6.34–35, 6.284; μνῶνται both times). An anonymous ancient athetesis struck out 6.275–288 as unfitting to her character (schol. on 6.275, Dindorf p. 317; see [[the-gossip-lines-275-288]]). She adds that she herself would blame another girl who did such things, mixing with men against her parents' will “before she has come to a public marriage” (ἀμφάδιον γάμον, 6.286–288; see [[nemesis]], [[praise-and-blame]] and [[marriage]]).

**The directions.** So he is to wait in the grove of Athena, by her father's estate, “as far from the city as a man can make himself heard when he shouts”, and come in later (6.289–299; see [[grove-of-athena]] and [[within-shouting-distance]]). The house of Alcinous is easy to find, “even a child could lead you” (6.300–303; see [[house-of-alcinous]]). Inside he must pass by her father and go to her mother:

{{quote:a1aee3ca-42b0-511f-b3d8-d6c86760b101}}

The advice reverses the order a guest would expect: the king on his throne drinking “like an immortal” is to be passed by (6.308–310; see [[alcinous]] and [[queen-arete]]). Athena repeats the last three lines almost word for word when she sends Odysseus into the palace (6.313–315 ≈ 7.75–77). The phrase “to see your dear ones and to reach your well-built house and your native land” (6.314–315) is also the phrase of the Cyclops' curse, which asks that, if Odysseus is fated to see his dear ones and reach his house, he come late and in misery (9.532–535; see [[the-wrath-of-poseidon]]).

**The drive.** She lashes the mules, and she drives with care so that the handmaids and Odysseus can follow on foot, “and she laid on the lash with judgment” (νόῳ, 6.316–320; see [[chariot-journey]]). They reach the grove at sunset, and the book leaves her there (6.321–322).

**Her words and titles.** She speaks five times in the book, more than anyone else: to her father (6.57–65), to Odysseus (6.187–197, 6.255–315), and to the handmaids (6.199–210, 6.239–246). Her longest speech, sixty-one lines, is the last one, and it carries a speech inside it (6.276–284). Her epithets in Book 6 are λευκώλενος “white-armed” (6.101, 6.186, 6.251), ἐύπεπλος “well-robed” (6.49), ἐυῶπις “fair-faced” (6.113, 6.142), and the whole-verse formula “Nausicaa, the daughter of great-hearted Alcinous” (6.17, 6.213). Odysseus calls her ἄνασσα, a title for goddesses (6.149, 6.175; see [[anax]]), and γύναι, “lady” (6.168).
