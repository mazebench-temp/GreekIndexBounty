summary: Nausicaa “spoke a word and spoke out” to Odysseus when she was ready to leave the river (6.254). She does not know his name and calls him ξεῖνε, “stranger”, so here the formula means “addressed him”.
grc: ["ἔπος τʼ ἔφατʼ ἔκ τʼ ὀνόμαζεν"]
---

**The verse.** ὤτρυνεν δʼ Ὀδυσῆα, ἔπος τʼ ἔφατʼ ἔκ τʼ ὀνόμαζεν, “she urged Odysseus on, and spoke a word and spoke out” (6.254). The form ὀνόμαζεν has the movable ν; the Odyssey has the verse-end both with and without it (ὀνόμαζε, 2.302; ὀνόμαζεν, 3.374). In many uses the first half of the verse holds a gesture, such as a stroke of the hand (4.610, 5.181); here it holds an urging. Nausicaa has folded the clothes, put them on the wagon, yoked the mules and climbed up (6.252–253; see [[thought-of-another-thing]]).

**No name.** Her speech begins ὄρσεο δὴ νῦν, ξεῖνε, πόλινδʼ ἴμεν, “rise now, stranger, to go to the city” (6.255; see [[xenia]]). She does not know who he is. He has not told her his name, and she will not learn it in the poem: Odysseus names himself only at the beginning of Book 9, to Alcinous and the Phaeacians (9.19–20). The Odyssey often uses the formula where no name follows. Athena in disguise, speaking to Odysseus at the games, says “stranger” (8.194–195); Odysseus, to Eumaeus, also says “stranger” (14.52–53); Antinous calls the beggar Irus “you ox-braggart” (18.78–79). LSJ renders the phrase “he spoke the word and uttered it aloud” (s.v. ἐξονομάζω). The translation “called him by name” at 6.254 does not fit the scene; the sense is “addressed him”.

**What she says.** The speech that follows is the longest instruction in the book: how to follow the wagon through the fields, where to wait, how to find the house of her father and whom to supplicate there (6.255–315; see [[nausicaa]] and [[supplication-type-scene]]).
