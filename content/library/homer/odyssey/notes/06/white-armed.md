---
summary: Nausicaa is “white-armed” three times in Book 6, always in the same place in the verse, and she gives the epithet herself to her handmaids (6.239), one of three Odyssey passages where it describes servants.
grc: [λευκώλενοι]
---

**Nausicaa.** The narrator calls her Ναυσικάα λευκώλενος three times (6.101, 6.186, 6.251). Each time the name and the epithet follow a short opening of one foot (τῇσι δέ, τὸν δʼ αὖ, αὐτάρ) and an action closes the verse. She “began the song and dance” among the girls at play (6.101), she “answered him face to face” after the supplication speech (6.186), and she “thought of other things” while Odysseus ate (6.251). The three lines mark her three moments of leadership: in the game, in speech, and in the plan for the journey to the city. See [[nausicaa]], [[then-answered]] and [[thought-of-another-thing]].

{{quote:Od. 6.186-187 | White-armed Nausicaa answers}}

In the Iliad the epithet belongs above all to Hera, and to Helen and Andromache among mortals. In the Odyssey it also goes with Nausicaa's mother Arete (7.233, 7.335, 11.335) and once more with Nausicaa, when Book 7 recalls her nurse (7.12). Mother and daughter share it. See [[queen-arete]].

**The handmaids.** Nausicaa opens her second speech to her maids with κλῦτέ μευ, ἀμφίπολοι λευκώλενοι, “hear me, white-armed handmaids” (6.239; [[hear-me]], [[handmaids-of-nausicaa]]). The epithet in the plural is not frequent. LSJ cites three passages for its use “of female slaves”, all in the Odyssey: this one, and two passages on Penelope's maids, who “came from the hall” (18.198, 19.60). In Book 6 it is the only use in a character's speech. The princess speaks of her attendants with the epithet of queens and goddesses. Book 6 has already said that the two maids who sleep at her door have “beauty from the Graces” (6.18), and the narrator calls the maids “with lovely tresses” in the lines around this speech (6.198, 6.238; [[with-lovely-tresses]]).

The epithet does not describe the day. These women have spent it outdoors, washing clothes in the river and spreading them on the shore in the sun (6.85–98). The formula keeps its meaning of beauty and rank, whatever the work.
