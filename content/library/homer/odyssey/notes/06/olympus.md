summary: When Athena leaves Nausicaa, the narrator describes Olympus, “where, they say, the seat of the gods is, firm forever”, untouched by wind, rain or snow, under a cloudless sky and a white gleam (6.41–47). Nausicaa later names the gods “who hold Olympus” (6.240).

**The description.** Athena finishes her speech in the dream and goes back to Olympus. The narrator pauses on the place.

{{quote:Od. 6.41-47 | The seat of the gods}}

The seat of the gods is ἀσφαλὲς αἰεί, “firm forever” (6.42; see [[seat-firm-forever]]). Three negatives follow: it is not shaken by winds, not wet by rain, not reached by snow. Then come two positive images: a clear, cloudless sky (αἴθρη ... ἀνέφελος) spread over it, and a white gleam (λευκὴ ... αἴγλη) running over it. There the blessed gods take their delight “all their days” (6.46; see [[blessed-gods]]). The last line returns to the story: “there the gray-eyed one went” (6.47).

**“They say.”** ὅθι φασί, “where, they say”, is unusual in the narrator's voice. Merry and Riddell compare the same phrase for local traditions in the Iliad, the bed of Typhoeus among the Arimi and the beds of the goddesses on Sipylus (Iliad 2.783, 24.615). A scholion (E.Q.) argues that “they say” is well put if Olympus is the mountain of that name, and badly put if it means heaven. By “they say” the poet shows that he follows a received tradition and does not invent. Merry and Riddell report that Eustathius takes it the other way. They also report that Köchly saw in ὅθι φασί a sign that the whole passage was added later. Merry and Riddell think this too strong: the lines are not needed by the context, but they do not contradict the Homeric picture of Olympus.

**Mountain or sky.** In the Iliad Olympus is a high, snowy, many-ridged mountain, whose gates of cloud the Seasons open and close (Iliad 1.420, 5.749–751). Here it has no snow and no clouds. A scholion (B.H.P.Q.T.) reconciles the two pictures: Olympus is snowless in its upper parts and “snowy” (ἀγάννιφος) in its lower parts below the clouds. Merry and Riddell give the same answer: the clouds mark a boundary, and the summit stands clear above the storms. They report that Aristarchus held that Homer always means the mountain by Olympus. Another scholion (E.P.Q.V.) explains ἀνέφελος and Homer's “heaven”, which reaches from the clouds to the region of the stars.

**Readings.** Rhianus, the Alexandrian poet and editor, read αἰθήρ for αἴθρη at 6.44 and τῇ ἔνι (“in it”, the gleam) for τῷ ἔνι at 6.46 (scholia H.P.). Merry and Riddell prefer ἀνέφελος to the variant ἀννέφελος at 6.45.

**Later imitation.** Lucretius uses the passage for the Epicurean gods. Their “quiet seats” are not shaken by winds or sprinkled by rain clouds, no white snow falls on them, and a cloudless sky covers them and smiles with spread-out light (Lucretius 3.18–22; see [[lucretius-seats-of-the-gods]]). The sequence of the images is the sequence of 6.43–45. Merry and Riddell also cite Lucan, for whom Olympus rises above the clouds and the highest things keep peace (Lucan 2.271–273), and Seneca, for whom the upper part of the world is not gathered into cloud or driven into storm (*De Ira* 3.6.1). They name Tennyson's “island valley of Avilion” in *Morte d'Arthur* as an English imitation. For the phrase ἕδος ἀσφαλὲς αἰεί they compare Pindar, who calls the bronze sky a seat “firm forever” (*Nemean* 6, opening lines).

**The gods who hold Olympus.** When Odysseus has been beautified, Nausicaa tells her handmaids that he does not come to the Phaeacians “against the will of all the gods who hold Olympus” (6.240). The Iliad ends three lines with οἳ Ὄλυμπον ἔχουσι(ν), “who hold Olympus” (Iliad 5.404, 5.890, 13.68). Compare the formula “who have their homes on Olympus” ([[homes-on-olympus]]).
