---
summary: Nausicaa is “the fair-faced girl” twice, both times at the moment when Odysseus is to see her and approach her (6.113, 6.142).
---

Both uses concern the meeting on the shore, and both are tied to Odysseus' eyes and actions.

- **6.113.** When the girls are about to go home, “the gray-eyed goddess Athena thought of something else: / that Odysseus should wake and see the fair-faced girl, / who would lead him to the city of the Phaeacian men” (6.112–114). The two words with -ῶπις stand in consecutive verses. The goddess “of the gray eyes” plans that the man should see the girl “of the fair face”. Her plan follows at once: the ball misses the handmaid and falls into the river, the girls shout, and Odysseus wakes (6.115–117). See [[athena]], [[gray-eyed]] and [[thought-of-another-thing]].
- **6.142.** Odysseus has come out of the thicket, and he wonders “whether to clasp the knees of the fair-faced girl and entreat her” (6.142). He chooses words instead ([[soothing-words]], [[decision-scene]]).

{{quote:Od. 6.112-114 | That Odysseus should see the fair-faced girl}}

The epithet stands where the narrative looks at Nausicaa from Odysseus' side. The other epithets that the narrator gives her in Book 6 are [[white-armed]] (λευκώλενος), [[well-robed]] (ἐΰπεπλος) and παρθένος [[unbroken|ἀδμής]].
