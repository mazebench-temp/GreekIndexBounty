summary: Nausicaa calls her own people “the godlike Phaeacians” (6.241), and the last verse of the book repeats the proem's words about Poseidon's anger against “godlike Odysseus” (6.331).
grc: [ἀντιθέοισι]
---

**The Phaeacians (6.241).** Nausicaa tells her handmaids that “not against the will of all the gods who hold Olympus / does this man mingle with the godlike Phaeacians” (6.240–241; [[homes-on-olympus]]). Elsewhere in the Odyssey the plural ἀντίθεοι goes with ἑταῖροι, “companions” (for example 4.571, 11.371, 14.247), and once with the suitors (14.18). It is a formula of honor and carries no moral judgment. Here a Phaeacian gives it to her own people. The claim fits what she has already said to the maids: “we are very dear to the immortals” (6.203). In Book 5 Zeus calls the Phaeacians ἀγχίθεοι, “near to the gods” (5.35). See [[phaeacians]] and [[nausicaa]].

{{quote:Od. 6.239-243 | Godlike Phaeacians, a godlike stranger}}

In the same speech the word for the gods returns twice. The stranger seemed unsightly before, but “now he is like the gods who hold the wide sky” (6.242–243). The godlike people receive a man whom Athena has just made godlike in looks (6.229–235; [[gold-poured-on-silver]]).

**Odysseus (6.331).** The book ends with Poseidon, who “raged furiously / against godlike Odysseus until he reached his own land” (6.330–331). The second verse is the same as 1.21, where the proem first states the god's anger. See [[odysseus]] and [[the-wrath-of-poseidon]].
