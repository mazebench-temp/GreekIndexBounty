---
summary: Nausicaa calms her fleeing handmaids with a disputed word. On the usual reading she says that no “living mortal” will ever bring war to the Phaeacians. On another ancient reading she says that this man is not one to be feared.
passages:
  - 6.199-205 | No living mortal
---

The handmaids have run from the naked stranger. Nausicaa calls them back:

{{quote:f197a4d1-0e42-57e9-8a73-16e8a16bb7e3}}

The sentence turns on διερὸς βροτός (6.201). How it is construed depends on the sense of διερός (see the article above).

- **“Living.”** If διερός means “living”, the words are part of the predicate, and the sentence is a general statement: “That man exists not as a living mortal, nor ever will be born, who shall come as a foeman to the Phaeacians' land.” This is Merry and Riddell's translation. They say that it is substantially the paraphrase of a scholion (schol. B on 6.201), and that οὗτος here introduces a general statement and does not point at Odysseus. They compare 16.437 and Herodotus 3.155 (note on 6.201, p. 270). The reason follows: “for we are very dear to the immortals” (6.203).
- **“To be feared.”** If διερός means “fearsome” or “a man to flee from”, a pause falls after βροτός, and οὗτος points at Odysseus: “this man is not a creature to scare us”, and no one will ever come to make war on us. Merry and Riddell report this as Lehrs' account of Aristarchus. It answers Nausicaa's question to the girls just before, “Where are you fleeing?” (πόσε φεύγετε, 6.199). They reject it.

Callistratus read δυερός, “wretched”, from δύη, “misery” (schol. E.H.P.Q.T. on 6.201, Dindorf p. 311). The text and the translation keep διερός, “living”.

Either way, the speech ends on the same point. Nausicaa's handmaids think he may be “one of the hostile men” (6.200), and she answers that the Phaeacians live far apart and that no mortal comes among them (6.204–205). Then she names what he is: “some unhappy man, a wanderer” (6.206; see [[ptochos]] and [[xenia]]).
