summary: Book 6 introduces the Phaeacians twice, once in the narrator's short history of their flight from the Cyclopes to Scheria, and once in Nausicaa's own words. They are dear to the gods and far from other men, a people of ships and not of bows, whose best men court the king's daughter and whose townsmen gossip.

**The history.** The book begins with them. Athena goes “to the land and the city of the Phaeacian men”, and the narrator stops to tell where they came from:

{{quote:a2403b75-3dac-564a-8c8d-67a8bacc728e}}

Hypereia had “wide dancing floors” (see [[with-wide-dancing-floors]] and [[hypereia-of-the-phaeacians]]). Their neighbors were the Cyclopes, ὑπερηνορέοντες, “overweening”, a word the Odyssey otherwise uses of the suitors (2.266, 4.766 and elsewhere). The Cyclopes kept harming them and were stronger (6.5–6; see [[cyclopes]]). The ancient readers located Hypereia in different ways: as Camarina in Sicily, as a land “beyond” the known world, or as a former island near the land of the Cyclopes (schol. on 6.4, Dindorf p. 294). Homer gives only its neighbors.

Nausithous “made them rise up” and settled them in Scheria, “far from grain-eating men” (ἑκὰς ἀνδρῶν ἀλφηστάων, 6.7–8; see [[nausithous]] and [[scheria]]). ἀλφηστής has two derivations: “earners”, from ἀλφάνω, the sense LSJ prefers, and “bread-eaters”, from ἄλφι “barley meal” and ἔδω “eat” (Merry and Riddell on 1.349, p. 40). The translation follows the second. Either way the phrase puts the Phaeacians apart from ordinary mortals (see [[alphestes]]). Then he does what a founder does: a wall around the city, houses, temples of the gods, and the division of the plowland (6.9–10; see [[the-phaeacians-leave-hypereia]]). Book 7 adds that Nausithous was Poseidon's son (7.56–62), so the people who fled the Cyclopes are kin of the Cyclops whom Odysseus will blind (1.68–73; see [[the-wrath-of-poseidon]]).

**The council and the suitors.** The Phaeacians have a council: the “noble Phaeacians” (Φαίηκες ἀγαυοί) call Alcinous to it, where he sits with “the famous kings” (6.54–55; see [[kings]] and [[alcinous]]). Athena's dream tells Nausicaa that “the best men throughout the land are courting you, / of all the Phaeacians, where you yourself have your lineage too” (6.34–35). The last clause makes marriage among the Phaeacians the expected thing, and a scholion on her later wish says that the Phaeacians did not seek wives from another land (schol. B on 6.244, Dindorf p. 314). The imagined gossip repeats the point: she dishonors “the Phaeacians who are courting her, many and good” (6.283–284; see [[marriage]]).

**In Nausicaa's words.** To the stranger she names the people first (6.194–197). To her handmaids she explains why a man on the beach is no danger:

{{quote:4f4d1d34-e5d6-5824-a72c-ec2b691d730d}}

She claims three things: no enemy will reach them, the gods love them, and they live “far apart in the much-surging sea, the farthest away” (ἔσχατοι, 6.204–205; see [[much-surging]]). The scholia argued over ἔσχατοι. Some took it as “last” from the point of view of Greece and placed the Phaeacians in Corcyra; others said that the poet clearly sets them far away, at the edge (schol. on 6.204, Dindorf p. 311; see [[corcyra-and-the-phaeacians]]). Her words “no other mortal mingles with us” (ἐπιμίσγεται, 6.205) return thirty-six lines later, when she says that the stranger, now made beautiful, “mingles with the godlike Phaeacians” not against the will of the gods (ἐπιμίσγεται ἀντιθέοισι, 6.240–241; see [[antitheos]]). Athena gives a harsher picture in Book 7: “these people do not much put up with strangers” (7.32–33).

**Ships, not bows.** Nausicaa's account of the city is a list of ships: two harbors, a narrow entrance, the curving ships drawn up along the road, a ship-shed for every man, the place of assembly around the shrine of Poseidon, the tackle, cables, sails and oars (6.262–269; see [[epistion]], [[posideion]], [[assembly]] and [[ships]]).

{{quote:8a7b99e0-613f-531a-b1ce-89b6b6ba8818}}

The verses define them by what they are not. Bow and quiver belong to the hunter and the warrior, and the bow is the weapon with which Odysseus will kill the suitors (see [[bow]] and [[quiver]]). In Book 8 the Phaeacians boast of ships, running, dance and song, and not of boxing or wrestling (8.246–253).

**The townsmen.** The same people have a sharp tongue. “It is their unkind talk that I shun, … and there are very overbearing men among the people” (ὑπερφίαλοι κατὰ δῆμον, 6.273–274). ὑπερφίαλος is the Odyssey's word for the suitors and for the Cyclopes (9.106). Nausicaa gives one of them a speech, a “baser man” who meets them on the road and draws the worst conclusion (6.275–284; see [[tis-speech]] and [[praise-and-blame]]). The Phaeacians as Book 6 presents them are near to the gods and also quick to judge a girl.

**The best of them.** She promises Odysseus that in her father's house he will come to know “all the Phaeacians who are the best” (6.257), and she sends him into “the city of the Phaeacians”, where no house is built like the house of Alcinous (6.298–303; see [[house-of-alcinous]]). His prayer in the grove names his aim with them: “Grant that I may come to the Phaeacians as one dear to them and pitied” (6.327; see [[dear-and-pitied]] and [[odysseus]]).
