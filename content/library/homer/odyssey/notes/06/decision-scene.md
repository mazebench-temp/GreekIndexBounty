{{quote:Od. 6.141-147 | Knees or words?}}

**The parts.** The shock is the girl who does not run: the others scatter, and Nausicaa alone stands facing him, because Athena has put daring in her mind (6.137–141; see [[athena]]). The division is μερμήριξεν, “was torn two ways in thought” (6.141), here without the διάνδιχα, “in two ways”, of the Iliad's verse (see [[pondering-two-ways]]). The alternatives are a double question, ἤ … ἦ (6.142–143): to clasp the knees of the fair-faced girl and entreat her, or to stand apart and entreat her with soothing words (see [[soothing-words]] and [[gounata]]). The resolution comes in a whole verse, ὣς ἄρα οἱ φρονέοντι δοάσσατο κέρδιον εἶναι, “So, as he pondered, this seemed to him the better way” (6.145), and the next verse repeats the chosen course in the infinitive (6.146). A reason follows: “lest the girl be angry in her mind if he clasped her knees” (6.147).

**Two nights, two decisions.** The verse of the resolution is the one with which Odysseus ended his last decision, on the river bank the night before: whether to stay by the river in the cold, or to climb into the wood and risk the beasts (5.465–474). The two decisions that bring him from the sea to the girl are thus closed by the same verse, ὣς ἄρα οἱ φρονέοντι δοάσσατο κέρδιον εἶναι (5.474, 6.145). In the Iliad the verse begins ὧδε δέ οἱ (Iliad 13.458, 14.23, 16.652); the Odyssey has both openings (ὧδε δέ at 10.153, 15.204, 18.93, 22.338, 24.239).

**The suspected line (6.144).** The third line of the choice adds the content of the entreaty: “in case she might show him the city and give him clothes” (6.144). A scholion calls the line superfluous (περιττός): Odysseus is not in doubt about what to ask, but about how to ask it, standing near or standing apart. It adds that the grammarian Athenocles also suspected the line (schol. H.P., Dindorf p. 306). Murray prints it. The two requests named in it are the ones Odysseus makes at the end of his speech, “Show me the town, and give me a rag to throw around me” (6.178), and the ones Nausicaa grants (6.192–194).

**The opposite choice.** In the slaughter of the suitors the singer Phemius faces the same pair of courses: to slip out and sit at the altar of Zeus of the courtyard, or to rush up and entreat Odysseus by his knees. His decision closes with the same resolution verse, and he chooses the knees (22.333–339). Phemius, in danger of death, needs the full gesture; Odysseus, a naked stranger before a girl, gives it up.
