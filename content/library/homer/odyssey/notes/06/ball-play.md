summary: While the washing dries, Nausicaa and her handmaids throw off their veils and play at ball, and Nausicaa leads the game. Athena turns the game into the means of waking Odysseus. Nausicaa's throw misses, the ball falls into a deep eddy, and the girls' shout wakes him.
passages:
  - 6.99-109 | The game and the Artemis simile
  - 6.112-117 | The ball goes astray
---

**The game.** The washing is spread out, the girls have bathed and eaten, and they wait for the clothes to dry (6.93–98; see [[washing-clothes]]). Then “they played at ball, having thrown off their veils” (ἀπὸ κρήδεμνα βαλοῦσαι, 6.100; see [[veil]]). Eustathius takes the phrase to mean that the girls played “simply, with bare heads” (p. 241). They are far from the town, and no man is in sight, as far as they know.

Nausicaa “began the song and dance” (ἤρχετο μολπῆς, 6.101). The scholia argue about μολπή. One scholion says that the poet calls the whole game μολπή, “but the younger critics take it as song”. It adds that Nausicaa did not sing but played ball, as the later line about her throw shows (schol. B.E.H.P.Q. on 6.101, Dindorf p. 302). Another scholion glosses μολπή as “the game” (schol. P). Merry and Riddell think there was chant and dance with the throwing (note on 6.101, p. 261; see [[dance]]). The narrator then compares her to Artemis among her nymphs: all are beautiful, but she stands out above them (6.102–109; see [[artemis-among-her-nymphs]]).

**The reading of 6.100.** The verse σφαίρῃ ταὶ δʼ ἄρʼ ἔπαιζον has the particle δέ in the third place of its clause, which is very rare in Homer. A scholion records that all the copies have it with δέ (πᾶσαι διὰ τοῦ δ, schol. H.P. on 6.100, Dindorf p. 302). Merry and Riddell cite La Roche, who takes “all” to mean all the copies of Aristarchus' edition, and Schnorr von Carolsfeld, who notes the other readings σφαίρῃ ταί γʼ ἄρʼ and ταί τʼ ἄρʼ (critical note on 6.100, p. 261). Murray prints the Aristarchean text.

**The throw.** Athena now “thought of something else”: that Odysseus should wake and see the girl who would lead him to the city (6.112–114; see [[thought-of-another-thing]]). The game does it.

Nausicaa throws the ball to a handmaid, misses her, and throws it into a deep eddy of the river (6.115–116; see [[river-of-scheria]]). The girls shout, and Odysseus wakes (6.117). A scholion counts the passage “among those celebrated for brevity”, because it has nothing superfluous. “Deep” explains why the girls cry out: the ball has not fallen by the bank, where it would be easy to recover, but into the middle of the stream. “Shouted aloud” makes the waking of Odysseus plausible (schol. H.P.Q. on 6.116, Dindorf p. 303). Eustathius repeats the point and adds that Odysseus wakes from the long cry, not from the song (p. 241).

**What Odysseus hears.** He hears “a female cry … as of girls” and wonders whether it comes from nymphs of the mountains and springs or from human beings (6.122–125; see [[to-the-land-of-what-mortals]] and [[nymphs]]). The game that likened Nausicaa to Artemis among the nymphs is heard by the stranger as the voices of nymphs. He comes out to the girls, and all but Nausicaa run away (6.127–140; see [[the-lion-and-the-girls]]).
