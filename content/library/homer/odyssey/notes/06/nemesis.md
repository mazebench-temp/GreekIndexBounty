---
summary: Nausicaa fears the blame of the townspeople, and she says that she shares their judgment. She too would be indignant with a girl who mingled with men before her marriage.
passages:
  - 6.285-288 | I too am indignant
---

After acting out the gossip she fears (6.275–284), Nausicaa turns to her own judgment:

{{quote:Od. 6.285-288 | I too am indignant}}

The verb is νεμεσῶ, “I am indignant” (from νεμεσάω). καὶ … ἄλλῃ, “with another woman too”, puts Nausicaa among those who blame. The indignation she would feel is the same that the town would feel toward her. In this book the speaker of νέμεσις and the one who fears it are the same person (see the article above for the pair νέμεσις and [[aidos|αἰδώς]]).

The object of the indignation is defined with care. The girl in question acts “against the will of her dear father and mother, while they are living” (6.287), and “mingles with men before she has come to a public marriage” (ἀμφάδιον γάμον, 6.288). LSJ glosses ἀμφάδιος “public” and cites this line. The two conditions are the consent of the parents and a marriage made in public (see [[marriage]]). The words just before are the people's reproaches (ὀνείδεα, 6.285; see [[oneidos]]), and the speech begins with the “unkind talk” she shuns (6.273; see [[phemis]]).

Some ancient critics marked 6.275–288 as spurious, because the lines did not suit the character of the speaker (schol. H.Q. on 6.275, Dindorf p. 317). The base text keeps them.
