Book 6 is the first supplication that Odysseus makes in the Odyssey, and the poem shows the rite with one part held back. The order of the scene is told in [[supplication-type-scene]]. This note follows the words of the rite.

**The words of the rite.** Every word of supplication in the book occurs here:

- λίσσομαι, “entreat”, three times as Odysseus weighs his choice (λίσσοιτο, λίσσοιτʼ, λίσσεσθαι, 6.142–146).
- γοῦνα, “knees”, the part of the body that a suppliant takes hold of (6.142, 6.147, 6.169, 6.310; see [[gounata]]).
- γουνοῦμαι, “I clasp your knees, I beseech you”, the first word of his speech (6.149).
- ἱκέτης, “suppliant”, Nausicaa's word for him (6.193).

**The gesture held back.** Odysseus can take the girl's knees, or he can stand apart and entreat her “with soothing words” (ἐπέεσσιν … μειλιχίοισι, 6.143). He chooses the words, for fear that she will be angry if he takes her knees (6.145–147). The narrator then calls his speech “soothing and shrewd” (μειλίχιον καὶ κερδαλέον, 6.148).

The full rite needs contact: the suppliant holds the knees and does not let go until he is answered. Odysseus is a naked man coming out of the bushes (6.127–137; see [[nakedness]]), and the person he must supplicate is an unmarried girl. So he says the verb of the gesture, γουνοῦμαι, and he does not make the gesture. Later in the speech he names his reason: “I am terribly afraid / to touch your knees” (6.168–169).

**The claims of the suppliant.** Odysseus states his claim in the usual terms. He has suffered at sea for twenty days, he is alone, and she is the first person he has met. Then he asks for pity: ἀλλά, ἄνασσʼ, ἐλέαιρε, “But, queen, have pity” (6.175; see [[pity]]). His two requests are small, a guide to the town and a rag to put on (6.178–179). He cannot offer a gift in return, so he offers a prayer for her marriage (6.180–185; see [[nothing-better-than-like-mindedness]]).

**The answer.** Nausicaa accepts him in the terms of the rite. He will lack nothing “of what befits a much-tried suppliant who has met with people” (ὧν ἐπέοιχʼ ἱκέτην ταλαπείριον ἀντιάσαντα, 6.193). Merry and Riddell take ἀντιάσαντα to suggest a sudden emergency: the suppliant has come upon someone who can help him (note on 6.193, p. 269). She gives two reasons:

1. Zeus gives fortune to good and bad men as he wishes, so the stranger's misery says nothing against him (6.188–190; see [[zeus-deals-out-prosperity]]).
2. Strangers and beggars are “from Zeus” (6.207–208; see [[strangers-and-beggars-are-from-zeus]]).

Elsewhere the Odyssey names Zeus as the god who protects suppliants. Odysseus tells the Cyclops that Zeus is “the avenger of suppliants and strangers” (Ζεὺς δʼ ἐπιτιμήτωρ ἱκετάων τε ξείνων τε, 9.270). In the palace the old Phaeacian Echeneus asks for wine to be poured to Zeus, “who attends on reverend suppliants” (7.165), and Alcinous repeats the words (7.181). On Ithaca Odysseus calls on “Zeus of suppliants” (Ζεὺς … ἱκετήσιος, 13.213). Nausicaa's maxim belongs to the same belief. Her orders to the handmaids then turn the acceptance into acts: food, drink and a bath (6.209–210; see [[xenia]] and [[bathing]]).

**The rite completed elsewhere.** Nausicaa's last instructions give the full form of the rite. In the palace Odysseus is to pass by her father and “throw your hands around the knees / of our mother” (6.310–311). The reward is the return home, if the queen is well disposed (6.311–315; see [[queen-arete]] and [[day-of-homecoming]]). Athena repeats the advice in Book 7 with the same lines (7.75–77), and Odysseus does it: “Odysseus threw his hands around the knees of Arete” (7.142). The knee-clasp that he refused to the daughter at the river, he gives to the mother at the hearth.
