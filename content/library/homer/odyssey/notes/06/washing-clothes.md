**The dream.** Athena comes to Nausicaa in the likeness of a friend of her own age and reproaches her: “Your glossy clothes lie uncared for, / and your marriage is near” (6.26–27). A bride must wear fine clothes herself and give others “to those who will lead you home”, and from such things a good report (φάτις) goes up among men, and her parents rejoice (6.27–30; see [[marriage]] and [[phemis]]). The washing is therefore tied to the marriage and to the girl's good name from its first mention.

{{quote:f10d3915-75c0-51ae-adad-db5ba5a17c91}}

The scholia explain “those who will lead you home” as the bridegroom, the plural standing for the singular, or as the bridegroom's people, to whom by custom the bride gave clothes (schol. B and Q.T. on 6.28, Dindorf p. 296). Merry and Riddell think rather of the procession that led a bride to her new home, as on the shield of Achilles (Iliad 18.491–493), to which the bride could add splendor by her gifts of clothing (note on 6.28, p. 254). Another scholion gives the narrative reason: the poet is preparing the men's clothing, “so that Odysseus may get something from it” (schol. P.T. on 6.28, Dindorf p. 296). Eustathius says the same of the coverlets in 6.38: they are mentioned so that Odysseus can be dressed from them, for he would not put on a woman's robe (p. 236).

**The fellow worker.** Athena offers to come too, as a συνέριθος, a “fellow worker” (6.32). LSJ glosses the word “helpmate, esp. one who is hired to assist in domestic work, as spinning or sewing” (s.v. συνέριθος). A scholion derives it from ἔρια, “wool”: properly a woman who works with another at the wool, and from this any helper. A second scholion derives it from ἔρις, “the good strife” (Dindorf p. 296, on 6.32). Eustathius finds the same pun in the washing itself, where the girls tread the clothes “quickly, bringing on a rivalry” (ἔριδα προφέρουσαι, 6.92). He thinks that a woman who brings on a rivalry could well be called ἔριθος, even if the word is said to come from ἔριον (p. 240, on 6.92). These derivations are ancient guesses. Merry and Riddell prefer to refer the word to the root ἐρ- or ἀρ- of ἀρτύω, “prepare” (note on 6.32, p. 254). Athena does not in fact come to the river. The friend's offer is part of the disguise.

**The request to her father.** Nausicaa asks her father for the mule wagon, to take “the glorious clothes / to the river to wash them, which lie here soiled” (6.57–59; see [[mules]]). She gives two reasons: he needs clean clothes for the council, and her five brothers want freshly washed clothes for the dance (6.60–65; see [[sons-of-alcinous]] and [[dance]]). She does not name the third reason, “for she felt shame to name the marriage of her prime / to her dear father”, and he understands everything (6.66–67; see [[fathers-and-daughters]] and [[aidos]]). The washing is a duty she can name. The marriage is the reason she cannot.

**At the pits.** The washing scene is a short work narrative in order:

1. The arrival at the never-failing pits, where water wells up and flows through (6.85–87).
2. The mules are turned loose to graze by the river (6.88–90).
3. The girls carry the clothes into the water and tread them in the pits, competing in speed (6.91–92).
4. They spread the clothes in a row on the shingle, where the sea has washed the pebbles clean (6.93–95).
5. They bathe, anoint themselves and eat, and wait for the clothes to dry in the sun (6.96–98; see [[bathing]]).

At 6.87 Murray prints ὑπεκπρόρεεν, “flowed up from beneath and out”. Merry and Riddell report that the manuscripts read the present ὑπεκπρορέει, and that Friedländer and Nauck corrected it to the imperfect (critical note on 6.87, p. 260). A scholion explains the prefix ὑπό as the water rising from the depth (schol. B.E. on 6.87, Dindorf p. 301).

The interval while the clothes dry is filled by the ball game (6.99–109; see [[ball-play]]). When Nausicaa is about to go home, “after yoking the mules and folding the fine clothes” (6.111), Athena makes the ball go astray and Odysseus wakes (6.112–117).

**The clothes for the stranger.** The washing ends by clothing Odysseus. He asks for “a rag to throw around me, / if perhaps you had some wrapper for the cloths when you came here” (6.178–179; see [[nakedness]]). Nausicaa promises that he will not lack clothing (6.192), and the handmaids lay a cloak and a tunic beside him (6.214; see [[cloak]] and [[tunic]]). He puts on “the clothes that the unwed maiden had given him” (6.228). Then Nausicaa folds the rest of the washing and puts it on the wagon (6.252), and the procession sets out for the town with the clean clothes and the stranger dressed in some of them.
