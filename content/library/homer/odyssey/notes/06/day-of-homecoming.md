summary: Nausicaa is the first speaker in the poem who uses the phrase to put the homecoming within Odysseus' reach. If he clasps her mother's knees, he will see it “rejoicing, and quickly” (6.310–312). Odysseus gives her the phrase back when he takes leave of her in Book 8.

**The promise.** Nausicaa ends her directions with the supplication of the queen: pass by her father, “and throw your hands around the knees / of our mother, so that you may see the day of homecoming / rejoicing, and quickly, even if you are from very far away” (6.310–312; see [[queen-arete]], [[gounata]] and [[supplication-type-scene]]). In the earlier books the phrase is a thing lost or taken away: the companions lose it in the proem (1.9), and Telemachus believes that his father's day of return has perished (1.168). In Book 5 Odysseus told Calypso that he longed every day to “see the day of return” (5.220). Here a speaker tells him how to reach it and says that it will come soon.

**The homecoming in Book 6.** The phrase is the third expression for the return in the book. Athena came to Scheria “planning a homecoming (νόστον) for great-hearted Odysseus” (6.14), and Nausicaa promised him that he would gain “an escort and a homecoming (πομπῆς καὶ νόστοιο) from my father” (6.289–290; see [[nostos]] and [[pompe]]). The goddess plans it, the girl promises it, and the goodwill of her mother is the condition (6.313–315).

**The reply in Book 8.** When Nausicaa takes leave of him at the feast and asks him to remember her, Odysseus answers with the half-verse of 5.220: may Zeus grant him “to come home and see the day of return; / then there I would pray to you as to a god” (8.464–468). The words of her promise return in his farewell.
