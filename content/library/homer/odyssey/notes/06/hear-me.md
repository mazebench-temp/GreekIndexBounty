---
summary: Two “hear me” openings in Book 6. Nausicaa begins her speech to her handmaids with the plural κλῦτέ μευ (6.239), and Odysseus begins his prayer to Athena with the singular κλῦθί μευ (6.324), a prayer that is about hearing from its first word to its close.
grc: ["κλῦτέ μευ"]
---

**Nausicaa to her handmaids (6.239).** κλῦτέ μευ, ἀμφίπολοι λευκώλενοι, ὄφρα τι εἴπω, “hear me, white-armed handmaids, so that I may tell you something” (see [[handmaids]] and [[white-armed]]). κλῦτε is the plural of the aorist imperative κλῦθι. Speakers to a group in the Odyssey more often use the reduplicated κέκλυτε, and the second half of her verse is the close of the line in which Antinous and Ctesippus address the suitors: κέκλυτέ μευ, μνηστῆρες ἀγήνορες, ὄφρα τι εἴπω (18.43, 20.292). The plain κλῦτέ μευ occurs elsewhere in the Odyssey only once, when Helen interprets an omen for the departing Telemachus (15.172). Nausicaa addresses her maids in the form of a short public speech. The manuscripts read κλῦτέ μοι, and the reading μευ comes from a scholion: “κλῦτέ μευ, so” (schol. H on 6.239, Dindorf p. 313), which Merry and Riddell follow.

What she tells them is not for the stranger's ears. He sits apart on the shore (6.236), and she says that he has come by the will of the gods, that before he looked unsightly and now he is like the gods, and that she wishes such a man might be called her husband (6.240–245). Eustathius remarks that she speaks freely because she is talking to her maids in private and he is not listening (on 6.242–245).

**Odysseus to Athena (6.324).** At sunset in Athena's grove Odysseus opens his prayer with the whole invocation verse κλῦθί μευ, αἰγιόχοιο Διὸς τέκος, Ἀτρυτώνη, “hear me, child of aegis-bearing Zeus, Atrytone”, as Diomedes (Iliad 5.115) and Penelope (4.762) do (see [[prayer-type-scene]]). The prayer is built on the verb of hearing. The next verse uses another: “now at least listen to me (ἄκουσον), since before you never listened (ἄκουσας)” (6.325). The close answers with the verb of the invocation: “so he spoke in prayer, and Pallas Athena heard him” (τοῦ δʼ ἔκλυε, 6.328). The narrator then says that she did not show herself to him, out of respect for her father's brother (6.329–331). She hears, but she does not appear.
