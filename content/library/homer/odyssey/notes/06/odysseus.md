summary: Naked, salt-caked and nameless, Odysseus wakes on Scheria to the shout of girls. He comes out of the thicket like a hungry lion, decides to supplicate Nausicaa with words and not by her knees, wins her help with a speech of calculated tact, bathes himself, is made beautiful by Athena, follows the wagon to the grove of Athena and prays to be received by the Phaeacians as one dear and pitied.

**Asleep.** “So he lay sleeping there, much-enduring brilliant Odysseus, / worn down by sleep and weariness” (6.1–2; see [[much-enduring]]). The line picks up the end of Book 5, where he buried himself in a bed of leaves like a brand kept alive in the ashes, and Athena poured sleep on his eyes (5.487–493; see [[hypnos]]). While he sleeps the goddess goes to the city, “planning a homecoming for great-hearted Odysseus” (6.14; see [[nostos]], [[athena]] and [[great-hearted]]). He does nothing for the first hundred lines of the book.

**Awake.** The girls' shout over the lost ball wakes him (6.117). He sits up and talks to himself:

{{quote:7bc065fe-0821-5429-9832-0017f65ab725}}

The question is the test of a land in the Odyssey: are its people savage, or kind to strangers and god-fearing? The same three lines come back when he wakes on Ithaca and does not know it (13.200–202), and two of them before he enters the Cyclops' cave (9.175–176; see [[to-the-land-of-what-mortals]] and [[xenia]]). The female cry might be that of nymphs of the mountains, springs and meadows, or of human beings “who have speech” (6.122–126; see [[nymphs]] and [[the-nymphs-cry-122-124]]). He resolves to go and see for himself.

**The lion.** He breaks off a leafy branch to cover himself (6.127–129) and goes out:

{{quote:2ac03d54-1715-5acf-a800-bb1aa9afc461}}

The simile is the one that sent Sarpedon against the Achaean wall: the same opening words, “he went on like a mountain-bred lion”, and the same hungry lion going “even into the close-built fold” (6.130–134 ≈ Il. 12.299–301; see [[the-lion-and-the-girls]] and [[the-mountain-lion-and-the-fold]]). A raiding warrior's simile is set on a naked castaway among girls, and the point of comparison is his need: “his belly bids him”, and “need had come upon him” (χρειώ, 6.133, 6.136; see [[gaster]]). To the girls he is σμερδαλέος, “terrible”, “marred by the brine” (6.137). They scatter; Nausicaa alone stays (6.138–141; see [[nausicaa]]).

**The choice.** He is “torn two ways in thought” (μερμήριξεν, 6.141; see [[decision-scene]]): clasp her knees, as a suppliant should, or entreat her from a distance with soothing words. “This seemed to him the better way” (κέρδιον, 6.145), lest the girl be angry if he took her knees (6.141–147; see [[supplication]] and [[gounata]]). The narrator then names the kind of speech he makes: μειλίχιον καὶ κερδαλέον μῦθον, “a soothing and shrewd word” (6.148; see [[soothing-words]]). κερδαλέος comes from κέρδος, “gain, advantage”; Merry and Riddell note that its sense ranges from the wise counsel of a goddess to the craft of Sisyphus (on 6.148, p. 265). The phrase occurs only here in the Odyssey and nowhere in Iliad 1–13.

**The speech.** He begins with the suppliant's verb and the highest flattery:

{{quote:c768eb1b-e367-5df9-b908-8fe0f7424281}}

The speech runs thirty-seven lines (6.149–185), the longest he makes in the book. It moves in steps. Goddess or mortal? If mortal, her parents and brothers are “thrice blessed”, and most blessed the man who wins her with bride-gifts (6.153–159; see [[god-or-mortal]], [[thrice-blessed]] and [[bride-price]]). He has never seen her like, except once, a young palm shoot by the altar of Apollo on Delos, which he saw when “a great army followed me, on that journey on which evil sorrows were to come upon me” (6.160–169; see [[the-palm-shoot-on-delos]] and [[odysseus-at-delos]]). Only then does he speak of himself: yesterday, on the twentieth day, he escaped the sea from the island of Ogygia, and a god has cast him here to suffer more (6.170–174; see [[ogygia]]). Zeus had foretold that he would reach Scheria “on the twentieth day” (5.34). He asks for little, the way to the town and a rag to wear, “if perhaps you had some wrapper for the cloths” (6.178–179). He ends with a wish fitted to a girl of marriageable age: a husband, a home, and like-mindedness, “for there is nothing stronger and better than this, / than when two who are like-minded in their thoughts keep a house, / a man and a woman” (6.180–185; see [[homophrosyne]] and [[nothing-better-than-like-mindedness]]).

He gives no name, no father and no country, only Delos, an army and Ogygia. He will not give his name until Book 9 (9.19). He is “the stranger” (ξεῖνε) to Nausicaa (6.187, 6.255, 6.289) and “a much-tried suppliant” (6.193; see [[much-tried]]). Aristotle quotes the last verse of his prayer as a model for the opening of a speech (see below), and the whole supplication works the same way: it makes the hearer well disposed before it asks for anything.

**The bath.** The handmaids set him in the shelter with a cloak, a tunic and oil. He asks them to stand aside: “I feel shame / to strip naked when I have come among girls with lovely tresses” (6.218–222; see [[aidos]], [[nakedness]] and [[bathing]]). He washes himself in the river, scrubbing from his back, shoulders and head “the scurf of the barren sea” (6.224–227). Elsewhere the women of the house bathe a guest: in the palace of Alcinous the slave women bathe and anoint him (8.449–456). Here he washes alone.

**Made new.** When he has dressed, Athena makes him taller and sturdier, lets his hair fall in curls “like the hyacinth flower”, and pours grace on his head and shoulders as a craftsman pours gold over silver (6.229–235; see [[divine-beautification]], [[like-the-hyacinth-flower]] and [[gold-poured-on-silver]]). He sits apart on the shore, “gleaming with beauty and graces” (6.237; see [[charis]]). Nearly the same verses return when Athena beautifies him for Penelope after the killing of the suitors (6.230–235 ≈ 23.157–162). Nausicaa sees the change: before he seemed “unsightly” (ἀεικέλιος), now “like the gods who hold the wide sky” (6.242–243; see [[deato-or-doato]]). He eats and drinks “greedily, for he had long been without a taste of food” (6.249–250).

**Following.** He does what she says. He walks behind the wagon with the handmaids (6.319–320) and sits down in the grove of Athena at sunset (6.321–322; see [[grove-of-athena]]). In Book 7 he covers for her. When Alcinous blames his daughter for not bringing the stranger home with her handmaids, Odysseus says that she told him to come but he refused “from fear and shame” (7.298–307), though she had told him to wait in the grove (6.295–299; see [[alcinous]]).

**The prayer.** In the grove he prays to Athena:

{{quote:40175aea-db4c-5ca3-9ef0-017b024bedb9}}

The opening verse is the verse of Diomedes' prayer to Athena (6.324 = Il. 5.115), and its first half begins Odysseus' own prayer to her before the night raid at Troy (Il. 10.278; see [[hear-me]] and [[prayer]]). Then comes a reproach: she did not listen when the earth-shaker “shattered” him (ῥαιομένου … ἔρραιε, 6.326; see [[the-wrath-of-poseidon]]). His last verse asks for the two things he needs from the Phaeacians, to be φίλος, “dear”, and ἐλεεινός, “pitied” (see [[dear-and-pitied]] and [[pity]]). Aristotle quotes this verse in the *Rhetoric*: since it is well said, “grant that I come to the Phaeacians as one dear to them and pitied”, the speaker in an introduction must aim at these two things (3.14.11, 1415b25–27; see [[aristotle]]). Athena hears, “but she did not yet appear to him face to face”, out of respect for her father's brother Poseidon, who “raged furiously / against godlike Odysseus until he reached his own land” (6.328–331; see [[seeing-the-gods]] and [[poseidon]]). The last verse repeats the poem's first statement of Poseidon's anger (6.331 = 1.21).

**His titles.** πολύτλας δῖος Ὀδυσσεύς, “much-enduring brilliant Odysseus” (6.1, 6.249); δῖος Ὀδυσσεύς, “brilliant Odysseus” (6.117, 6.127, 6.217, 6.224, 6.322); μεγαλήτωρ, “great-hearted” (6.14); ἀντίθεος, “godlike” (6.331; see [[antitheos]]). The narrator gives him no epithet of cunning in this book, though his speech is called κερδαλέος (6.148).
