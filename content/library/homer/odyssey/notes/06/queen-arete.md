summary: Book 6 never names the queen. She is “the mother” at the hearth with the sea-purple wool, who packs the food and the golden flask of oil for her daughter's outing, and in Nausicaa's last instructions she becomes the person on whose goodwill the stranger's homecoming depends.

**Unnamed.** In Book 6 the queen is only μήτηρ, “mother”. Athena's dream opens with her: “Nausicaa, why did your mother bear you so slack?” (6.25), and it names the reward of a good report as the joy of “your father and lady mother” (πότνια μήτηρ, 6.30; see [[phemis]]). Odysseus counts her among those who are “thrice blessed” in such a daughter (6.154; see [[thrice-blessed]]). Her name, Arete, comes only in Book 7, when Athena tells Odysseus who she is (7.53–55).

**At the hearth.** Nausicaa finds her parents indoors, and the poet sets them apart in two images. The mother sits “at the hearth with her handmaid women, / turning the sea-purple wool on her distaff”, while the father goes out to the council (6.52–55; see [[hearth]], [[distaff]], [[sea-purple]] and [[weaving]]). ἠλάκατα is the wool on the distaff, and ἁλιπόρφυρος, “sea-purple”, is a dye fit for a queen. A scholion draws both points: although the Phaeacian state lives in great luxury, the queen does not neglect her work, and the sea-purple wool suits a queen, as Helen too works violet wool (schol. Q.T. on 6.53, Dindorf p. 298; Odyssey 4.135).

**The provisions.** She has no speech in the book, but she does what the father only orders. She puts “satisfying food of every kind” and relishes into a chest, pours wine into a goatskin, and gives her daughter olive oil in a golden flask, so that she and her handmaids may anoint themselves after bathing (6.76–80; see [[olive-oil]] and [[wineskin]]). The same golden flask passes from the girls to Odysseus at the river (6.215).

The mother sits “in the light of the fire”, spinning the same sea-purple wool, “a wonder to see”, leaning against a pillar, with her slave women behind her (see [[handmaids]] and [[thambos]]). The king's throne leans against the same pillar, but the stranger is to pass it by (6.308–310; see [[alcinous]]):

{{quote:b0b53206-9310-5fbc-bc6a-54b215362582}}

The scholia ask why he should go to the mother. One note, citing Callistratus, gives three answers: as a woman, Nausicaa puts a woman first; Arete is handed down as the wisest, and women are more ready to pity (schol. E.H.Q.V. on 6.310, Dindorf p. 319). Another note takes ἐπʼ ἐσχάρῃ, “at the hearth”, as a sign of the season: she sits by the fire because it is winter (schol. B on 6.305, Dindorf p. 319). Homer gives no reason in Book 6. In Book 7 Athena repeats the last three verses (6.313–315 ≈ 7.75–77) and adds the explanation of her standing: Alcinous honors her as no other wife is honored, the people greet her like a goddess, and she settles quarrels even among men (7.66–74).

The conditional matters. “If she is minded in friendship toward you” (εἴ κέν τοι κείνη γε φίλα φρονέῃσʼ, 6.313) makes the homecoming depend on her goodwill, and Odysseus follows the advice to the letter: he passes through the hall, clasps her knees, and sits down in the ashes (7.139–154; see [[supplication]] and [[supplication-type-scene]]). Book 6 thus ends its picture of the queen where Book 7 begins its action.
