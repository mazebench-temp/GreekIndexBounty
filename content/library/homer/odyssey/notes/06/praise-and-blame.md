In the Iliad praise and blame are mostly words between men about courage. In Book 6 they concern the reputation of an unmarried girl, and their vocabulary is the vocabulary of public talk.

**Good report.** Athena's first argument for the washing is reputation. Fine clothes for the bride and for those who lead her home win a good report: “For it is from these things that a report goes up among men, / a good one, and your father and lady mother rejoice” (φάτις … ἐσθλή, 6.29–30; see [[phemis]] and [[washing-clothes]]). The good name of the daughter is also the joy of the parents. A scholion reports that Callistratus read χάρις, “pleasure”, for φάτις, and said that Aristophanes of Byzantium had changed it to φάτις (schol. H.P. on 6.29, Dindorf p. 296).

**Praise.** Odysseus' supplication is a speech of praise. He likens Nausicaa to Artemis, calls her family thrice blessed and her future husband most blessed, and compares her to the palm shoot on Delos (6.149–169; see [[god-or-mortal]], [[thrice-blessed]] and [[the-palm-shoot-on-delos]]). Nausicaa returns the judgment in a short form: he seems “neither a base man nor a senseless one” (6.187), and later “you seem to me not to lack sense” (6.258).

{{quote:Od. 6.273-275 | The talk she shuns}}

The words are those of blame. She shuns their φῆμις ἀδευκής, “unkind talk”, so that no one “afterward may blame me” (μωμεύῃ, 6.273–274). Merry and Riddell take ὀπίσσω as “hereafter”, not “behind my back” (note on 6.273, p. 277). The verb μωμεύω, “blame”, belongs with μῶμος, “blame, reproach”. The imagined speaker is “some baser man” (κακώτερος), and the people are “very overbearing” (ὑπερφίαλοι, 6.274–275). His speech mocks her as a girl who has found a husband from abroad, since she “dishonors” (ἀτιμάζει) the many good Phaeacians who court her (6.276–284; see [[tis-speech]]). She names the result: “these things would become reproaches against me” (ὀνείδεα, 6.285; see [[oneidos]]).

**Her own judgment.** Nausicaa does not reject the townsmen's standard. She adds: “And I too am indignant with another woman who does such things” (καὶ δʼ ἄλλῃ νεμεσῶ, 6.286; see [[nemesis]]), and states the rule of a public marriage with the parents' consent (6.287–288; see [[marriage]]). The fear of blame and the feeling of blame are the same standard, seen from two sides. Earlier in the book the same standard keeps her silent before her father: she feels shame to name her marriage (6.66; see [[aidos]]).

**An ancient objection.** In antiquity the fourteen lines of the imagined speech and the rule (6.275–288) were athetized as unfitting to the girl. The scholion that reports it names no critic, and it says that 6.273–274 had already said what was needed (schol. H.Q. on 6.275, Dindorf p. 317). Murray prints them.
