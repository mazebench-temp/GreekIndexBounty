summary: In Book 6 Alcinous is seen through his daughter. He gives her the mules and the wagon without a question, because he understands what she does not say, and she describes him to the stranger as the man on whom the power of the Phaeacians depends, whose house is easy to find, and whose throne the stranger must pass by.

**The new king.** The book names him in its twelfth verse. Nausithous, the founder, “had already been overcome by fate and had gone to Hades, / and Alcinous was ruling then, who knew counsels from the gods” (6.11–12; see [[nausithous]] and [[phaeacians]]). A scholion reads the phrase θεῶν ἄπο μήδεα εἰδώς as a promise to the listener: the poet hints already that Odysseus will meet a man who is kind to strangers (schol. B.P.Q. on 6.12, Dindorf p. 295; see [[xenia]]). Athena then goes “to his house” (6.13; see [[house-of-alcinous]]), and the plan for Odysseus' return begins in his daughter's bedroom.

**Papa dear.** At dawn Nausicaa finds him at the door, going out “to join the famous kings in council, to which the noble Phaeacians were calling him” (6.53–55; see [[kings]] and [[assembly]]). She stands very close and asks for the wagon (6.56–65). She calls him πάππα, “papa”. A scholion calls it a lisping word, an affectionate address of a younger person to an older one, and another note lists it with τέττα (to a friend), ἄττα (to a foster father) and θεῖε (to an uncle) (schol. on 6.57, Dindorf pp. 298–299). Her reasons are about him and her brothers: a man “among the foremost” must sit in council with clean clothes on his skin, and the five sons want fresh clothes for the dance (6.60–65; see [[sons-of-alcinous]]). A scholion notes the shift: Athena spoke of marriage openly, and Nausicaa turns the need to the men who will wear the clothes, though her words still hint at the truth because she is of an age to marry (schol. P.Q.T. on 6.60, Dindorf p. 299).

{{quote:Od. 6.66-70 | He understood everything}}

The narrator gives him one line of insight, ὁ δὲ πάντα νόει, “but he understood everything” (6.67), and his answer shows it by what it leaves out. He does not name the marriage. He grants “the mules, child, nor anything else”, and repeats her own description of the carriage, “high, with good wheels” (ὑψηλὴν ἐύκυκλον, 6.58, 6.70), with one practical addition: it will have an “upper frame” (ὑπερτερίῃ) for the load. The scholia explain the word as a box or a squared timber set on the wagon to hold what is put in it (schol. B.E.Q.V. on 6.70, Dindorf p. 300). He then calls the slaves, and they obey (6.71; see [[doulios]]). This is the only speech he makes in the book, three verses long (see [[fathers-and-daughters]]).

**In his daughter's words.** To the stranger, Nausicaa defines herself by him: “I am the daughter of great-hearted Alcinous, / on whom the power and the strength of the Phaeacians depend” (6.196–197; see [[great-hearted]]). A scholion glosses the verb: the affairs of the Phaeacians “hang from” him (schol. D.E.P. on 6.197, Dindorf p. 310). She calls him δαΐφρων, “wise-hearted” (6.256; see [[wise-hearted]]), and she makes him the source of what Odysseus needs: at his house the stranger will meet the best of the Phaeacians (6.255–257), and from him he may gain “an escort and a homecoming” (πομπῆς καὶ νόστοιο, 6.290; see [[pompe]] and [[nostos]]).

**His land and his house.** His τέμενος, the land set apart for him, and his flourishing orchard lie beside the grove of Athena, as far from the city as a man's shout carries (6.291–294; see [[temenos]], [[grove-of-athena]] and [[within-shouting-distance]]). His house in the city is “easily known”, and “even a child could lead you”, because no house of the Phaeacians is built like “the house of Alcinous / the hero” (6.299–303). The last word, ἥρωος, stands at the start of verse 303. Ancient readers knew a variant ἥρως, which would make it a vocative addressed to Odysseus, “O hero” (schol. B on 6.302, Dindorf p. 318; Merry and Riddell on 6.303, p. 279; see [[heros]]).

**The throne to pass by.** Nausicaa's last instructions place him beside her mother:

{{quote:Od. 6.308-310 | He drinks his wine like an immortal}}

The throne leans against the same pillar as the queen's chair (Merry and Riddell on 6.307–308, p. 279; see [[throne]]). He sits there “and drinks his wine like an immortal” (οἰνοποτάζει … ἀθάνατος ὥς, 6.309). The short comparison gives the picture of a king at ease. The point of the verses is the order of approach: the stranger is to pass by the king and clasp the knees of the queen (6.310–315; see [[queen-arete]] and [[supplication]]). Athena gives the same order in Book 7 and adds that Arete is honored by her husband as no other woman on earth (7.53–77). When Odysseus does as he is told, it is Alcinous who at last raises him from the hearth by the hand (7.167–171).
