---
summary: In Athena's grove at sunset Odysseus addresses the goddess as “Atrytone”, the Unwearied, in the invocation verse that Penelope used in Book 4. He prays as a man worn out, and he complains that she did not hear him before.
---

The verse κλῦθί μευ, αἰγιόχοιο Διὸς τέκος, Ἀτρυτώνη, “hear me, child of aegis-bearing Zeus, Atrytone” (6.324), occurs twice in the Odyssey. Penelope uses it when she prays for her son (4.762), and here Odysseus uses it. In the Iliad Diomedes prays with the same verse (Il. 5.115), and once with a different first half, κέκλυθι νῦν καὶ ἐμεῖο (Il. 10.284). The narrator introduces the prayer with a different title: Odysseus “prayed to the daughter of great Zeus” (Διὸς κούρῃ μεγάλοιο, 6.323; [[daughter-of-zeus]]). At the close she is “Pallas Athena” (6.328). See [[athena]], [[prayer-type-scene]], [[hear-me]] and [[child-of-aegis-bearing-zeus]].

LSJ takes the name as a lengthened form of ἀτρύτη, “the Unwearied”, from ἄτρυτος (“not worn out, untiring”). If that is right, the title stands against the state of the man who uses it. Book 6 opened with Odysseus “worn down by sleep and weariness” (ὕπνῳ καὶ καμάτῳ ἀρημένος, 6.2), and in this prayer he speaks of himself as the man whom the earth-shaker “shattered” (6.326; [[shaker-of-the-earth]]). This contrast is an observation of this note. No ancient source consulted comments on it.

The prayer is also a reproach. Odysseus asks her to listen “now at least”, “since before you never listened” (6.325). He holds that she was absent through his sufferings at sea. In Book 13 he says the same to her face: after Troy he did not see her board his ship until she encouraged him among the Phaeacians and led him into the city (13.316–323). The narrator gives her reason for not appearing yet: she “stood in awe of her father's brother”, Poseidon, who raged against Odysseus (6.329–331; [[the-wrath-of-poseidon]]). She hears the prayer (6.328), and she answers it in Book 7, where she meets him in disguise and leads him to the palace.
