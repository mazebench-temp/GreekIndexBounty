---
summary: Book 6 has no whole dawn verse. It has three shorter dawn expressions, two in Athena's dream speech and one in the narrative, and its single day runs from that dawn (6.48) to the sunset at the grove (6.321).
grc: ["ἠοῖ φαινομένηφι", "ἠῶθι πρὸ", "Ἠὼς ἦλθεν ἐύθρονος"]
en: [when dawn appears, in the early dawn]
---

**“When dawn appears” (6.31).** In the dream Athena tells Nausicaa: “let us go to wash them when dawn appears” (ἅμʼ ἠοῖ φαινομένηφι). The phrase is a verse-end formula: ἅμα, “together with, at”, + ἠοῖ, dative of ἠώς, “dawn”, + φαινομένηφι, a present participle with the old case ending -φι. It is common in both poems in speeches and in the narrative (4.407, 7.222, 12.24, 16.270; Iliad 9.682, 11.685, 24.600). Merry and Riddell, after Classen, note that Homer uses the present tense for sunrise, as here, and the aorist for sunset (ἅμʼ ἠελίῳ καταδύντι), because the growing light has no sharp limit and the last ray of sunset does (on Od. 1.24). A scholion says that the haste is needed so that the care of Odysseus may come sooner (schol. P.Q.T. on 6.31, Dindorf p. 296). Nausicaa does not know of him; the goddess's urgency is for him.

**“In the early dawn” (6.36).** Athena goes on: “urge your famous father in the early dawn” (ἠῶθι πρό) to give her the mules and the wagon. ἠῶθι is ἠώς with the suffix -θι, “at”, and πρό is an adverb, “early”. Merry and Riddell compare Ἰλιόθι πρό, “before Ilium”, and οὐρανόθι πρό, where πρό is local, and take ἠῶθι πρό as “in the morning, early” (on 5.469). The phrase is rare: in the Odyssey it occurs only here and at 5.469, and in the Iliad once (Iliad 11.50). At 5.469, the night before, Odysseus feared lying by the river, “for a cold breeze blows from a river in the early dawn”, and he went up into the wood to sleep (5.466–473). The next “early dawn” in the poem is the dawn Athena arranges for his rescue.

**“At once came Dawn of the fair throne” (6.48).** The narrative dawn follows Athena's return to Olympus without a pause: αὐτίκα δʼ Ἠὼς ἦλθεν ἐύθρονος, ἥ μιν ἔγειρε, “at once came Dawn of the fair throne, who woke her” (see [[eos]] and [[of-the-fair-throne]]). The same half-verse ends a night in the hut of Eumaeus (αἶψα γὰρ Ἠὼς ἦλθεν ἐΰθρονος, 15.495). A scholion says that the “throne” here is Dawn's chariot seat, because the goddess as described here is not seated (schol. E.P.V. on 6.48, Dindorf p. 298). Here Dawn has a task in the plot: she wakes the dreamer, and Nausicaa at once wonders at the dream (6.49; see [[dream-scene]]).

**One day.** The book is one day long. The dawn of 6.48 begins it, and it ends when “the sun set” as the party comes to the grove of Athena (6.321; see [[sunset-lines]]). The night of 6.1–47 continues the night that began when Odysseus fell asleep at the end of Book 5.
