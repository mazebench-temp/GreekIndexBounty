**A people in flight.** In the Iliad exile is the fate of one man, usually a killer who leaves his kin and is received by another lord. Book 6 begins with the flight of a whole people. The Phaeacians lived in Hypereia near the Cyclopes, who “kept doing them harm, and were greater in strength” (6.5–6). Nausithous “made them rise up and led them away” and settled them in Scheria, “far from grain-eating men” (6.7–8). There they built a wall, houses and temples and divided the land (6.9–10; see [[the-phaeacians-leave-hypereia]] and [[nausithous]]). A scholion explains the move as the sending out of a colony (ἀποικίαν στειλάμενος, schol. H on 6.7, Dindorf p. 294).

**Safety by distance.** The refuge is secured by remoteness. Nausicaa tells her handmaids that no man will come to the land of the Phaeacians “bringing combat”, because they are dear to the immortals, and “we live far apart in the much-surging sea, / the farthest away, and no other mortal mingles with us” (6.201–205; see [[scheria]]). The people who fled their neighbors now have none.

**The wanderer.** The same speech names the stranger: “But this is some unhappy man, a wanderer (ἀλώμενος) who has come here, / and we must care for him now” (6.206–207). Odysseus is no exile; he is trying to go home. But he comes as the exile does, alone, without possessions, from a far land, and he depends on the goodwill of the people he reaches (see [[supplication]] and [[strangers-and-beggars-are-from-zeus]]). The Phaeacians' own story of flight and settlement is told before he arrives; the poem does not say that they remember it when they receive him.
