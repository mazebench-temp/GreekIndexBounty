summary: Odysseus supplicates Nausicaa without the gesture. He weighs the knee-clasp and rejects it, keeps his distance, and makes the whole appeal in words; she accepts him as a suppliant. At the end of the book she tells him how to make the full supplication, with his hands around her mother's knees.

**The gesture weighed and dropped.** Odysseus is naked, salt-stained and alone, and the girl in front of him is a king's daughter (6.135–140). Before he speaks, the narrator gives his choice between the two forms of the scene. He can clasp her knees, the full gesture, or stand apart and entreat her with words alone (6.141–144). He chooses words, “lest the girl be angry in her mind if he clasped her knees” (6.145–147; see [[decision-scene]]). The scene therefore keeps its approach, its appeal and its answer, and loses its contact.

{{quote:efa5178b-f16f-5f26-9c45-ea422a14086f}}

**The appeal.** The speech opens with the verb of the gesture that he did not make: γουνοῦμαί σε, ἄνασσα, “I beseech you, queen” (6.149). γουνοῦμαι is formed from γόνυ, “knee”; the same word opens the knee-clasping appeals of Leodes and Phemius to Odysseus (22.310–312, 22.342–344) and of Lycaon to Achilles (Iliad 21.74). A scholion on 6.149 notes the point: he declined to touch her knees, and what he did not do in deed he puts forward in word, and makes plain why he declined (schol. H.Q., Dindorf p. 306). Later in the speech he says it himself: “I am terribly afraid / to touch your knees” (6.168–169; see [[gounata]]).

The appeal then takes the parts of the type-scene in its own order:

1. **Praise.** The question whether she is a goddess or a mortal (6.149–153; see [[god-or-mortal]]), the blessing of her family and of her future husband (6.154–159; see [[thrice-blessed]]), and the comparison with the palm shoot on Delos (6.160–169; see [[the-palm-shoot-on-delos]]). The praise stands where an Iliadic suppliant names his claim, the past service or the ransom.
2. **The suppliant's state.** Twenty days on the sea from Ogygia, and the fear of more evil to come (6.170–174).
3. **The request.** “But, queen, have pity” (ἐλέαιρε, 6.175), then the two things he needs, to be shown the town and to be given a rag to put on (6.178–179; see [[pity]]).
4. **The wish.** A husband, a home and like-mindedness for her (6.180–185; see [[homophrosyne]] and [[nothing-better-than-like-mindedness]]). The prayer for the one supplicated takes the place of the promise of a reward.

**The answer.** Nausicaa accepts. She names his condition with the word of the ritual: he will not lack clothing “or anything else / of what befits a much-tried suppliant” (ἱκέτην ταλαπείριον, 6.192–193; see [[much-tried]]). She grants both requests, the town and the clothing, and adds food and a bath (6.194–210). Her own reason is the rule that strangers and beggars come from Zeus (6.207–208; see [[strangers-and-beggars-are-from-zeus]] and [[xenia]]).

**The full form, to come.** At the end of the book Nausicaa gives him the order of the scene he must perform in the palace. He is to pass her father by and “throw your hands around the knees / of our mother”, so that he may see the day of his homecoming (6.310–315; see [[queen-arete]] and [[day-of-homecoming]]). The gesture refused on the beach is thus postponed, and the woman to be clasped is the queen. In the next book he does it: “and Odysseus threw his hands around the knees of Arete” (7.142), and he speaks his plea to her (7.146–152).

The two supplications of Odysseus frame the Phaeacian episode. The first is made at a distance and in words, to an unmarried girl; the second is made in full, before the whole court. See [[supplication]].
