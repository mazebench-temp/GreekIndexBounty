summary: Odysseus comes out of the thicket naked except for a leafy branch, and frightens the girls. He asks Nausicaa for a rag, and when the handmaids offer to bathe him he refuses to strip in front of them, “for I feel shame”.

**The branch.** Odysseus wakes, decides to “try and see” who the people are, and comes out from the thicket. He has nothing on. He breaks off a leafy branch “to shield the man's genitals about his body” (ὡς ῥύσαιτο περὶ χροῒ μήδεα φωτός, 6.127–129). Merry and Riddell translate “that girt about his body it might cover his nakedness” (note on 6.129, p. 264).

**Naked among the girls.** The simile of the mountain lion follows, and it ends on the point of his state: “so Odysseus was about to go among the girls with lovely tresses / and mingle with them, naked though he was; for need had come upon him” (γυμνός περ ἐών, 6.135–136; see [[the-lion-and-the-girls]]). χρειώ, “need”, is the reason given for the breach of shame. To the girls he looks “terrible … marred by the brine”, and all but Nausicaa run (6.137–138). Athena gives her courage to stay (6.139–140).

**The plea.** Odysseus' nakedness shapes his supplication. He does not clasp the girl's knees, for fear that she will be angry (6.141–147; see [[supplication]]). His request is the smallest thing: “give me a rag to throw around me, / if perhaps you had some wrapper for the cloths when you came here” (ῥάκος, εἴλυμα, 6.178–179). Nausicaa promises that he will not lack clothing (6.192), and the handmaids lay a cloak and a tunic beside him (6.214; see [[washing-clothes]]).

**Ashamed to strip.** Nausicaa tells the handmaids to bathe him in the river (6.210). In the Odyssey a guest is usually bathed by women of the house (see [[bathing]]). Odysseus refuses:

{{quote:Od. 6.218-222 | Ashamed to strip}}

He gives his reason in the word of shame: αἰδέομαι γάρ, “for I feel shame / to strip naked when I have come among girls with lovely tresses” (6.221–222; see [[aidos]]). The girls go apart, and he washes himself (6.223–227). The refusal reverses the scene on the beach. There need made him go naked among the girls. Here, with the need met, he will not be naked in front of them. LSJ cites 6.222 as an example of the middle γυμνοῦσθαι, “strip oneself” (s.v. γυμνόω).

**After the bath.** Once he is washed and dressed, Athena makes him taller and more beautiful, and Nausicaa says that he who seemed “unsightly” now looks like the gods (6.229–243; see [[divine-beautification]]). The book thus moves Odysseus from a naked man with a branch to a man clothed by the household and adorned by a goddess.
