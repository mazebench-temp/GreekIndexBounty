summary: The first use of the castaway's question in the poem. Woken by the girls' shout, Odysseus asks whether the people are savage or kind to strangers, then adds a second doubt found only here, whether the cry came from nymphs or from “human beings who have speech”, and resolves to try and see.

**The setting.** Nausicaa throws the ball at a handmaid, misses, and it falls into a deep eddy; the girls shout, and Odysseus wakes (6.115–117). Athena had planned that he should wake and see the girl who would lead him to the city (6.112–114; see [[athena]]). He sits up and ponders “in his mind and in his heart” (6.118). The three verses of the question follow (6.119–121), in their first occurrence in the poem.

**The second doubt.** In Book 6 the question goes on, and the continuation is found only here (6.122–126):

{{quote:1f266cfc-7549-55ec-ba73-ae010ac4ddc9}}

The cry is θῆλυς ἀυτή, “a female cry” (see [[aute]]). A scholion remarks that the poet says θῆλυς, not the feminine form θήλεια (schol. P on 6.122, Dindorf p. 304). The cry sounds “as of girls” (ὥς τε … κουράων), and the girls might be nymphs of the mountains, springs and meadows (see [[nymphs]]). Merry and Riddell suggest that the voice of nymphs might itself point to men nearby, since nymphs receive altars and sacrifices from mortals (13.350, 17.210; note on 6.122, p. 263). They also report that some modern editors suspected or rejected 6.123–124: Nitzsch observed that 6.124 is identical with *Iliad* 20.9 and the *Hymn to Aphrodite* 99, and that a cry of nymphs tells Odysseus little about mortals. The scholia accept the lines (the same note).

The alternative is ἀνθρώπων … αὐδηέντων, “human beings who have speech” (6.125). LSJ glosses αὐδήεις “speaking with human voice”. The Odyssey uses it of goddesses who speak with a human voice, Circe and Calypso (10.136, 11.8, 12.150, 12.449), and of Ino, who “was once a mortal with speech” (5.334). Here it marks the difference between a land of men and a land of spirits. On his travels Odysseus meets both.

**Try and see.** The speech ends in a resolve: “I will try for myself and see” (πειρήσομαι ἠδὲ ἴδωμαι, 6.126). On the Goat Island the same question was followed by the same kind of decision, to go “and test these men” (9.174), and that test led to the Cyclops' cave. Here the answer is given at once. Nausicaa promises that he will lack nothing “of what befits a much-tried suppliant” (6.192–193). She tells the fleeing handmaids that “strangers and beggars are all from Zeus” (6.207–208; see [[xenia]]). Nausicaa speaks for herself, though. Her speech on the town gossip (6.273–288) and Athena's warning in the next book (7.32–33) show that the Phaeacians as a whole are less simply “kind to strangers”.
