Woken by the girls' shout, Odysseus asks himself what people live in this land:

{{quote:Od. 6.119-121 | The castaway's question}}

The noun is ὑβριστής, “a man of outrage”. LSJ glosses it “violent, wanton, licentious, insolent man”. The translation renders the plural “arrogant”. The word stands first in a list of three negative terms, ὑβρισταί, ἄγριοι (“savage”) and οὐδὲ δίκαιοι (“not just”), and the list is answered by two positive ones, φιλόξεινοι (“kind to strangers”) and θεουδής (“god-fearing”). So the opposite of the man of ὕβρις is the host who respects strangers and the gods (see [[xenia]] and [[to-the-land-of-what-mortals]]).

The three verses recur twice. Odysseus speaks them before he goes to the cave of the Cyclops (9.174–176), and again when he wakes on Ithaca and does not know where he is (13.200–202). In Book 9 the answer is the first alternative. The narrator has already called the Cyclopes “overbearing and lawless” (ὑπερφιάλων ἀθεμίστων, 9.106). In Book 6 the answer is the second. Nausicaa receives him and declares that strangers and beggars are from Zeus (6.207–208). Elsewhere in Homer ὑβριστής is used of the Trojans by Menelaus (*Iliad* 13.633) and of the suitors, who “hold” Ithaca, by Laertes (24.282).

ὕβρις does not appear again in the book, though Nausicaa describes some of her own people as ὑπερφίαλοι, “overbearing”, men who would talk ill of her (6.274; see [[phemis]]).
