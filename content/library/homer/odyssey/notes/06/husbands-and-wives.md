summary: Book 6 shows a marriage only in words and in one picture. Odysseus, on his way home to his wife, blesses Nausicaa's future marriage with the ideal of a like-minded husband and wife; Nausicaa wishes for such a husband; and her directions show her father and mother side by side at their hearth.
refs: [6.180-185, 6.244, 6.305-309]
passages:
  - 6.180-185 | The like-minded couple
  - 6.305-309 | The king and queen at the hearth
---

**The ideal.** The Iliad's marriages are mostly marriages under threat. Book 6 gives the Odyssey's ideal of marriage in a maxim. Odysseus wishes Nausicaa “a husband and a home”, and like-mindedness, “for there is nothing stronger and better than this, / than when two who are like-minded in their thoughts keep a house, / a man and a woman” (6.180–185; see [[nothing-better-than-like-mindedness]] and [[homophrosyne]]). The participle and the verb are in the dual. Husband and wife are a pair who share one house and one mind.

**The speaker.** The man who speaks the blessing is himself a husband trying to reach his wife. Calypso offered to make him immortal if he stayed with her, though he longed to see his wife (5.203–210), and he chose to go home (5.215–224). Penelope is not named in Book 6, but the blessing describes the marriage he is going back to.

**The wish of the girl.** After the beautification Nausicaa wishes that “such a man might be called my husband” (πόσις, 6.244; see [[posis]] and [[marriage]]). The imagined townsman repeats the word with scorn: “Surely he will be a husband for her”, “she herself has gone about and found a husband” (6.277, 6.282; see [[tis-speech]]). The poem's audience knows that he is already a husband.

**The king and queen.** Nausicaa's directions show the marriage of her parents in one picture. Her mother sits at the hearth in the firelight, spinning sea-purple wool, leaning against a pillar, and “there my father's throne leans against it”, where he sits and drinks his wine (6.305–309; see [[queen-arete]] and [[alcinous]]). The pronoun αὐτῇ, “against it”, can refer to the pillar or to the queen herself. Either way the two seats stand together. The suppliant is to pass the king and clasp the queen's knees (6.310–311; see [[supplication]]). In Book 7 Athena tells Odysseus how Alcinous honors Arete, “as no other woman on earth is honored” (7.66–68).
