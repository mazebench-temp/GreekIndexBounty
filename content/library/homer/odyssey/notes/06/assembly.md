summary: Nausicaa describes the Phaeacian place of assembly as a built space by the harbor, around the shrine of Poseidon, paved with great hauled stones, where the men tend the tackle of their ships. The king's council (βουλή) meets elsewhere.
heading: The Phaeacian agora
refs: [6.267, 6.268, 6.269]
passages:
  - 6.262-269 | The harbor and the agora
---

**The place.** Nausicaa's description of the city moves from the wall to the two harbors, the narrow entrance and the ships drawn up along the road, and then to the agora: “And there they have their place of assembly, around the fine shrine of Poseidon, / fitted with hauled stones bedded deep in the earth” (6.266–267; see [[posideion]] and [[polis]]). Here ἀγορή is a place, not a meeting, and the place is built and paved.

**The stones.** ῥυτοῖσιν λάεσσι are stones “hauled”, too big to carry. Merry and Riddell quote a scholion to this effect and compare the wall of Eumaeus' yard, built of the same kind of stones (14.10), and the yard of the Cyclops' cave, “built high with stones bedded deep” (9.185) (note on 6.267, p. 276). They explain ἀμφίς, “around”, to mean that the shrine of Poseidon divides the agora, so that the agora lies on both sides of it (note on 6.266, p. 276).

**The use.** The agora of the Phaeacians is also their shipyard. “There they tend the tackle of the black ships, / cables and sails, and they taper the oars” (6.268–269). The people of the place are named by their work: they care for masts, oars and ships, not for bows and quivers (6.270–272; see [[phaeacians]]). The assembly place stands by the sea god's shrine and among the ships.

**Council and assembly.** At the start of the book Alcinous goes out “to join the famous kings / in council” (ἐς βουλήν, 6.54–55). A scholion explains βουλή here as the council chamber (schol. B on 6.55, Dindorf p. 298). The council of the kings and the assembly of the people are two different gatherings. In Book 8 Alcinous leads the Phaeacians to their agora, “which was built for them beside the ships”, where they sit on polished stones, and there he proposes the escort of the stranger (8.4–6, 8.24–45).
