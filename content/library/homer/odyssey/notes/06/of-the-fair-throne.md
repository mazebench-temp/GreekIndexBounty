summary: Dawn “of the fair throne” comes at once after Athena leaves for Olympus and wakes Nausicaa (6.48). The sense of the epithet is disputed. An ancient note reads the throne as Dawn's chariot seat, and a modern view derives -θρονος from θρόνα, “embroidered flowers”.
grc: [ἐΰθρονος]
---

**The line.** Athena returns to Olympus after the dream (6.41–47), and the next verse brings the day: αὐτίκα δʼ Ἠὼς ἦλθεν ἐύθρονος, ἥ μιν ἔγειρε, “at once came Dawn of the fair throne, who woke her” (6.48). The verse has the shape of the Odyssey's short dawn verse αὐτίκα δὲ χρυσόθρονος ἤλυθεν Ἠώς, “at once golden-throned Dawn came” (10.541, 12.142, 15.56, 20.91; [[golden-throned]]), but it names Dawn before the epithet and adds a relative clause. A close parallel is 15.495, αἶψα γὰρ Ἠὼς ἦλθεν ἐΰθρονος, when Telemachus reaches Ithaca. Here Dawn does more than mark the time: she wakes the girl to whom the goddess has just spoken, and the day of the washing begins. See [[eos]], [[dawn-line]] and [[nausicaa]].

**What the throne is.** LSJ gives the standard sense, “with beautiful seat or throne” (s.v. εὔθρονος), and cites this line. Two other readings exist.

- **The chariot seat.** A scholion on this line (mss. E, P, V; Dindorf p. 298) says that here the poet means Dawn's chariot seat (θρόνον … τὸν ἁρμάτειον), “for the goddess described here is not seated”. The scholiast reasons that a goddess who “came” cannot be on a throne. The Odyssey does give Dawn a team: in Book 23 Athena stops her at Ocean and does not let her yoke her swift horses, Lampus and Phaethon (23.243–246).
- **Flowers.** θρόνα, a plural noun found once in Homer, means “flowers embroidered on cloth”: Andromache at her loom ἐν δὲ θρόνα ποικίλʼ ἔπασσε, “and in it she worked flowers of many colors” (Il. 22.441; LSJ s.v. θρόνον). In 1868 Gustav Wustmann proposed that the second half of the -θρονος compounds comes from θρόνα (RhM 23, p. 238). V. A. Chernysheva reports that Aly (1920) and Burnett (1983) support this, and that Wilamowitz (1913) rejects it. Her own article argues the point for Sappho's ποικιλόθρονος (*Philologia Classica* 12.2, 2017, pp. 231–233). On this view ἐΰθρονος would mean “with fair embroidered flowers”, an epithet of Dawn's robe like [[saffron-robed]] (κροκόπεπλος). That application to this line is our inference. No source consulted applies the θρόνα theory to Od. 6.48.

The Odyssey also calls Artemis and Dawn χρυσόθρονος (5.123, 10.541), and in the Iliad Hera has the same epithet (Il. 1.611). The translation keeps the standard sense.
