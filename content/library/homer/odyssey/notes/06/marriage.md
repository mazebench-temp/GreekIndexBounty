The marriage of Nausicaa is spoken of in every part of Book 6, by a goddess, by the girl, by the stranger and by imagined townsmen, and it never takes place. The book shows the stages of a girl's marriage as the Odyssey knows them: the suitors, the gifts, the leading home, the house, and the public consent of the parents.

**The words.** The book uses most of the Homeric vocabulary of marriage:

- γάμος, “marriage, wedding” (6.27, 6.66, 6.288).
- μνάομαι, “court, woo”: the best men of the Phaeacians “are courting you” (μνῶνται, 6.34, 6.284).
- ἄγομαι, “lead home” as a bride: “those who will lead you home” (6.28), the man who “leads you home” (ἀγάγηται, 6.159).
- ἔεδνα, the bride-gifts with which a suitor wins the bride (ἐέδνοισι, 6.159; see [[bride-price]]).
- παρθένος, “maiden”, the state that marriage will end: “you will not be a maiden much longer” (6.33; see [[parthenos]]).
- ὀπυίω, “have to wife”, and ἠίθεος, “unmarried young man”: two of Alcinous' five sons are married and three are not (6.63; see [[sons-of-alcinous]]).
- πόσις, “husband” (6.244, 6.277, 6.282; see [[posis]]).
- ἀμφάδιος γάμος, the “public marriage” (6.288). LSJ gives ἀμφάδιος as “public” and cites only this line for it with γάμος (s.v. ἀμφάδιος).

**The goddess.** Athena's dream speech makes the marriage the reason for the washing. “Your marriage is near”, the bride must have fine clothes for herself and for those who lead her home, the good report pleases her parents, and the best men of all the Phaeacians, her own people, are courting her (6.27–35; see [[washing-clothes]] and [[phemis]]). The suitors are local. Athena stresses that they are the Phaeacians “where you yourself have your lineage too” (6.35).

**The girl and her father.** Nausicaa asks her father for the wagon and gives every reason but one: “she felt shame to name the marriage of her prime / to her dear father” (θαλερὸν γάμον, 6.66). Merry and Riddell take θαλερός either as “in her maiden prime” or as a fixed epithet, “fruitful”, which would further explain her shame (note on 6.66, pp. 258–259). “But he understood everything” (6.67; see [[fathers-and-daughters]] and [[aidos]]).

**The stranger.** Odysseus knows nothing of the dream, but his praise turns to the same subject. In his supplication he calls the girl's parents and brothers thrice blessed, and the most blessed of all is the man “who, prevailing by the weight of his bride-gifts, leads you home” (6.158–159; see [[thrice-blessed]]). At the end of the speech he wishes her “a husband and a home” and the like-mindedness of husband and wife (6.180–185; see [[nothing-better-than-like-mindedness]] and [[husbands-and-wives]]).

**The wish.** When Athena has made Odysseus beautiful, Nausicaa says to her handmaids: “If only such a man might be called my husband, / living here, and if only it pleased him to stay here” (6.244–245). Aristarchus athetized both lines, with hesitation about the first, and another scholion calls them unfitting for a maiden (Dindorf p. 314). Plutarch makes his judgment conditional: if she speaks from passion for a stranger, her boldness is to be blamed; if she sees his character and prefers him to a sailor or a dancer of her own people, she deserves admiration (*How the Young Man Should Study Poetry* 27A–B). See [[nausicaas-wish-244-245]] and [[plutarch-on-nausicaas-wish]].

**The gossip and the rule.** Before they set out, Nausicaa tells Odysseus to follow the wagon through the fields but to stay behind when they come near the city. She gives her reason in a speech within a speech. Some baser man will see them and say: “who is this handsome and tall stranger following Nausicaa? … Surely he will be a husband for her”. He will be a castaway from a far land, or a god come down in answer to her prayers, and she scorns the many good Phaeacians who court her (6.273–285; see [[tis-speech]] and [[praise-and-blame]]). The rule names the conditions of a proper marriage: the parents' will, and a “public” wedding, before which a girl does not “mingle with men”. Merry and Riddell, following a scholion's punctuation, take the parents “still living” as the point: a girl with a father and mother to guide her has no excuse (note on 6.287, p. 278). The fourteen lines 6.275–288 were athetized in antiquity as unfitting to the speaker, on the ground that 6.273–274 had already said it (schol. H.Q. on 6.275, Dindorf p. 317). The scholion names no critic.

**After Book 6.** The marriage that Book 6 imagines is offered in Book 7. Alcinous wishes that a man such as Odysseus would take his daughter and be called his son-in-law, “staying here”, and offers a house and possessions; but no one will keep him against his will (7.311–315). Odysseus is going home to his own wife. The word αὐτόθι μίμνειν, “to stay here”, in Nausicaa's wish (6.245) returns as αὖθι μένων in her father's offer (7.314).
