The narrator's history of the Phaeacians names their old enemies at once:

{{quote:8beadafd-04f7-507e-876c-de35016ba430}}

**What the verses say.** The Phaeacians once lived in Hypereia “near the Cyclopes, overweening men, / who kept doing them harm, and were greater in strength” (6.5–6; see [[hypereia-of-the-phaeacians]]). σινέσκοντο is iterative: the harm was repeated, not a single raid (a scholion glosses it ἔβλαπτον, “they harmed”, and derives it from σίνω, schol. B on 6.6, Dindorf p. 294). The Phaeacians did not fight back. Their founder “made them rise up” and took them to Scheria (6.7–8; see [[nausithous]] and [[the-phaeacians-leave-hypereia]]). A scholion offers a third view of Hypereia as a former island “near the land of the Cyclopes” (schol. on 6.4, Dindorf p. 294).

**The words.** The Cyclopes are ἄνδρες ὑπερηνορέοντες, “overweening men”. Homer uses the participle of the Trojans in the Iliad and, in the Odyssey, mostly of the suitors (2.266, 4.766; LSJ s.v. ὑπερηνορέων). Their strength is βίηφι … φέρτεροι, and the Cyclops in Book 9 boasts in the same terms: “we are much stronger” than Zeus and the gods (9.275–276). The Phaeacians are settled “far from grain-eating men” (6.8; see [[alphestes]]), and the Cyclops in Book 9 “did not look like a grain-eating man” (9.190–191).

**Two peoples.** Book 6 does not describe the Cyclopes further, and the poem has not yet told the story of Polyphemus, which Odysseus will tell the Phaeacians in Book 9. The two peoples are set side by side from the start: one strong, lawless and harmful, the other driven out, settled behind walls, and, in Nausicaa's words, “very dear to the immortals” (6.203; see [[phaeacians]]). Both are kin of Poseidon (1.68–73, 7.56–62), and Alcinous will name the Cyclopes as the other people who are “near” the gods (7.205–206).
