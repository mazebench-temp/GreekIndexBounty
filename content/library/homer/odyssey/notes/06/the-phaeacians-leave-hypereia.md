---
summary: The narrator tells the Phaeacians' history inside Athena's journey to their city. The account is a ring, from “the Phaeacian men” to the house of Alcinous, and it gives the city's past before Odysseus sees any of it.
passages:
  - 6.2-14 | Athena's journey and the Phaeacian past
---

**A history inside a journey.** Athena “went to the land and the city of the Phaeacian men” (6.3). A relative clause, οἳ πρὶν μέν ποτʼ ἔναιον, “who once in former days lived …”, opens the history (6.4), and the history runs through four stages in nine verses: the old home, the move, the foundation, and the present king (6.4–12). Then the journey resumes: “To his house went the gray-eyed goddess Athena” (6.13). The house is the house of Alcinous, the last name in the history. The digression thus begins and ends with the goddess going to the Phaeacians.

**Tenses.** The verbs mark three layers of time. The imperfect ἔναιον, “they lived”, and the iterative σινέσκοντο, “kept doing them harm”, describe the old state (6.4–6). The aorists of Nausithous' acts tell the foundation as single events (6.7–10). The pluperfect βεβήκει, “he had gone” to Hades, and the imperfect ἦρχε, “was ruling then”, bring the story up to the time of the action (6.11–12; see [[nausithous]] and [[alcinous]]).

**What Book 6 leaves out.** Book 6 gives neither the founder's father nor his prophecy. These come in Books 7 and 8 (7.56–63, 8.564–571; see the article above). In this book Nausicaa states the result of the migration: no enemy comes to the Phaeacians, for they are dear to the gods, and they live “far apart … the farthest away” (6.200–205; see [[exile-and-refuge]]).
