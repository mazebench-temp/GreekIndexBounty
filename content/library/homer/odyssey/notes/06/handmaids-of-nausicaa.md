summary: Nausicaa's handmaids are with her through the whole book. They guard her door, wash and play with her at the river, run from the naked stranger, and then, at her order, clothe, oil and feed him, and walk behind the wagon with him to the grove. Three of the book's speeches are addressed to them.

**At the door.** The first of them appear before Nausicaa speaks: two handmaids “who had beauty from the Graces” sleep one by each doorpost of her chamber, and the doors are shut (6.18–19). Athena passes them “like a breath of wind” (6.20). A scholion explains that the goddess slipped in through the opening for the bolt-strap, as in the dream of Penelope (schol. P.Q. on 6.20, Dindorf p. 295; Odyssey 4.802; see [[like-a-breath-of-wind]] and [[dream-scene]]). The guards wake to nothing.

**At the river.** They go with the wagon: “not alone; other handmaids also went along with her” (6.84). At the pools they loose the mules, carry the clothes into the water, and tread them in the trenches “quickly, bringing on a rivalry” (6.88–92; see [[washing-clothes]] and [[eris]]). They bathe, anoint themselves, eat, and play at ball with their veils thrown off (6.96–100; see [[ball-play]]). Nausicaa leads the song and dance, and the simile sets her above them as Artemis stands above her nymphs, “and she is easily known, though all are beautiful” (6.101–109; see [[artemis-among-her-nymphs]]). The last throw goes wrong on Athena's plan. The princess throws the ball toward one of them, misses, and the ball falls into the eddy; the shout that follows wakes Odysseus (6.115–117). He takes it for the cry of nymphs, or of human beings (6.122–126; see [[nymphs]]).

**The flight.** When the salt-caked man comes out of the bushes, “terrible he appeared to them, … and they fled in fear, this way and that, over the jutting beaches” (6.137–138). Their flight is the background for Nausicaa, who alone stays, because Athena has put daring in her mind (6.139–141; see [[nausicaa]]).

**Called back.** Nausicaa's first speech to them is a rebuke and a lesson in hospitality:

{{quote:Od. 6.199-203 | Stand still for me, handmaids}}

She tells them that no enemy comes to the Phaeacians, that “from Zeus are all strangers and beggars”, and that they must give the stranger food and drink and bathe him in the river (6.199–210; see [[strangers-and-beggars-are-from-zeus]] and [[xenia]]). They obey in part. They stand, urge one another on, seat him in the shelter, lay out a cloak and a tunic, give him the golden flask of oil, and tell him to bathe (6.211–216). The bath itself he refuses. He asks them to stand some way off, because he feels shame “to strip naked when I have come among girls with lovely tresses” (6.217–222; see [[aidos]] and [[nakedness]]). They go apart and tell Nausicaa (6.223).

**Her confidantes.** Her second speech to them is about him. Now that Athena has made him beautiful, she says, he looks like the gods: “If only such a man might be called my husband, / living here” (6.239–245; see [[nausicaa]] and [[posis]]). The wish is said to the handmaids, and not to the stranger, and it ends in an order: “But, handmaids, give the stranger food and drink” (6.246). This time they listen closely and obey, and set food and drink beside him (6.247–248; see [[food-and-drink]]).

**On the road.** Nausicaa tells Odysseus to walk with the handmaids behind the mules and the wagon while they cross the fields (6.259–261), and she drives with judgment, “so that they could follow on foot, / the handmaids and Odysseus” (6.319–320). Her plan keeps him in the company of the girls until the grove of Athena, and out of sight of the town (6.262–296; see [[grove-of-athena]] and [[tis-speech]]).

**Words.** The narrator calls them ἀμφίπολοι throughout, and δμῳαί once (6.99; see [[handmaids]]). Nausicaa addresses them as ἀμφίπολοι in all her speeches to them (6.199, 6.209, 6.239, 6.246), and Odysseus uses the same word (6.218).
