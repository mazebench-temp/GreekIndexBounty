---
summary: Both of Nausicaa's long speeches to Odysseus contain a broken sentence. Her first words, “Stranger, since you seem like neither a base man nor a senseless one”, have no main clause (6.187), and her directions for entering the city turn into a description of the harbor and never return to their “when” (6.262–272).
refs: [6.187-193, 6.262-272]
passages:
  - 6.186-193 | “Stranger, since …”
  - 6.262-272 | “But when we set foot in the city …”
---

**“Since you seem …” (6.187).** Nausicaa answers the suppliant with a ἐπεί clause, “since you seem like neither a base man nor a senseless one” (6.187). The next verse begins a new thought, the maxim that Zeus deals out prosperity to good and bad as he wishes, and that Odysseus must endure what he has been given (6.188–190; see [[zeus-deals-out-prosperity]]). Then a second ἐπεί clause starts, “But now, since you have come to our city and land” (6.191), and this one has its main clause: “you will not lack clothing, then, or anything else” (6.192).

{{quote:9d86fb3d-28ee-580c-8593-cc6dbaf604dc}}

The ancient and modern readings of the break differ.

- A scholion says the poet “gives nothing in reply to the ἐπεί” (οὐδὲν ἀποδίδωσι τῷ ἐπεί, schol. P.Q., Dindorf p. 310). Another note (P) supplies a verb from the maxim, “endure”, understood with the first clause.
- Merry and Riddell take 6.188–190 as a parenthesis, and 6.191 as a second reason added to the first. The main clause for both is then οὔτʼ οὖν ἐσθῆτος δευήσεαι (6.192), and οὖν, “then”, marks the return (note on 6.187, p. 269).

The translation follows neither supplement. It ends 6.187 with a semicolon and lets 6.188 begin afresh (see TRANSLATION.md, “Book 6: disputed constructions”). The effect in the scene is the same on either view. The girl begins with a judgment of the man, breaks off to state a general truth about fortune, and only then makes her promise.

**“But when we set foot in the city” (6.262).** In her second speech Nausicaa plans the walk to town. As long as they pass through the fields, he is to follow the wagon with the handmaids (6.259–261). “But when we set foot in the city” (αὐτὰρ ἐπὴν πόλιος ἐπιβήομεν, 6.262) should lead to a second instruction. Instead the city itself takes over: its high rampart, the harbor on either side and the narrow entrance, the ships drawn up along the road, each man's ship-shed, the place of assembly around the shrine of Poseidon, the tackle, sails and oars, and the remark that the Phaeacians care nothing for bow and quiver (6.262–272; see [[scheria]], [[posideion]] and [[epistion]]). At 6.273 she turns to what she fears, the talk of the people, and the instruction that the “when” clause promised never comes.

{{quote:8ecc0e52-4592-5b17-b1ea-7ce71b2963cd}}

- The scholia propose two places for the missing main clause: “stranger, listen quickly to my word” (6.289), with everything between as a parenthesis (διὰ μέσου, schol. H.Q.T.), or “you will find a splendid grove of Athena” (6.291, schol. H.Q.; Dindorf p. 315).
- Merry and Riddell find no proper apodosis unless it is sought as far off as 6.295, where her directions resume, and conclude that “the form of sentence is forgotten” in the length of the description (note on 6.262, p. 275).

The translation keeps the break and runs the description on with semicolons (TRANSLATION.md on 6.262–267). The plan has a reason that she states at 6.273–288: she must not be seen entering the town with a handsome stranger. The broken sentence carries her from the route to the gossip.

A third, smaller question of construction lies between these two. A scholion on 6.228 says it is unclear where the main clause of “But when he had bathed all over … and had put on the clothes” begins, at 6.229 (“Athena … made him”) or already within 6.228 with a redundant δέ (schol. Q, Dindorf p. 313). Merry and Riddell take 6.229 as the start of the main clause. That sentence is completed, so it is not an anacoluthon.
