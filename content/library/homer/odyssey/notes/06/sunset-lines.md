summary: The day of Book 6 ends with the half-verse δύσετό τʼ ἠέλιος, “and the sun set”, as the party reaches Athena's grove (6.321). An ancient scholion argues that here the verb means “was going down”, on the ground that Odysseus enters the city by daylight in the next book.
grc: ["δύσετό τʼ ἠέλιος"]
en: [And the sun set]
---

**The verse.** δύσετό τʼ ἠέλιος καὶ τοὶ κλυτὸν ἄλσος ἵκοντο, “and the sun set, and they came to the famous grove” (6.321; see [[grove-of-athena]]). δύσετο is an aorist of δύω, “sink, go down”, formed with -σ- and the endings of the thematic aorist; it has no augment. The Odyssey's usual sunset verse has the same first half and a second half about the dark: δύσετό τʼ ἠέλιος σκιόωντό τε πᾶσαι ἀγυιαί, “and the sun set, and all the streets grew dark” (2.388, 3.487, 11.12, 15.185). Book 6 puts the arrival in the second half instead, as Book 8 puts the arrival of gifts (8.417). The two events, sunset and arrival at the grove, happen together. The τε … καί joins them.

**The day.** The sunset closes the single day of the book, which began when “Dawn of the fair throne” woke Nausicaa (6.48; see [[dawn-line]]). Nausicaa had planned the timing: Odysseus is to wait in the grove “until we come to the town and reach the house of my father” (6.295–296), and to set out only when he expects that they have arrived (6.297–299). The night does not begin in this book. Book 6 ends with Odysseus' prayer in the grove (6.322–331; see [[prayer-type-scene]]).

**“Was setting”.** A scholion takes δύσετο here as “was declining toward the west”, because Odysseus goes into the city while it is still day, and that is why Athena pours a mist over him (schol. P.V. on 6.321, Dindorf p. 320). Another asks: “and how would Athena pour a mist on Odysseus if it were evening? So δύσετο is ‘was declining toward the west’” (schol. P.Q.T.). In Book 7 Athena does pour a thick mist over him so that no Phaeacian will taunt him or ask who he is (7.14–17). When he enters the hall he finds the Phaeacian leaders pouring libations to Hermes, “to whom they poured the last libation when they were mindful of bed” (7.136–138), which places his arrival late in the evening.
