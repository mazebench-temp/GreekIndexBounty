summary: Odysseus ends his plea to Nausicaa with a blessing. May the gods give her a husband, a home and good like-mindedness, “for there is nothing stronger and better than this, / than when two who are like-minded in their thoughts keep a house”.

**The blessing.** Odysseus has asked Nausicaa for very little, to be shown the town and given a rag to wear (6.178–179). He closes with what he can give in return, a prayer (6.180–185):

{{quote:97219079-02f3-5369-abbf-74a2ce172ac9}}

A scholion reads the prayer as tact: Odysseus “shrewdly joins in praying for the things which alone, he thinks, are her care” (schol. on 6.181, Dindorf p. 309). Another scholion explains why a suppliant blesses at all: those who cannot repay a favor at once take refuge in this return (schol. E.P. on 6.180, Dindorf p. 309; see [[charis]]). Odysseus does not know that Athena has already told Nausicaa in a dream that her marriage is near (6.25–35; see [[marriage]]).

**What it is.** The prayer moves from things to a state of mind: a husband (ἀνήρ) and a house (οἶκος), and then ὁμοφροσύνη. The scholia gloss it “genuine friendship” (γνησίαν φιλίαν, schol. Q.V. on 6.181, Dindorf p. 309). The next lines define it by its work. Husband and wife are “like-minded in their thoughts” (ὁμοφρονέοντε νοήμασιν), and the verb is in the dual: they are a pair. They “keep a house” (οἶκον ἔχητον, 6.183). A scholion says that here the poet “set out in the highest degree the things through which a house is happy” (schol. P.Q. on 6.183, Dindorf p. 309).

**Its effects.** The result is put as a contrast: “many sorrows to their enemies, / and joys to their well-wishers, and they themselves know it best” (6.184–185). Merry and Riddell take ἄλγεα and χάρματα as accusatives in apposition to the whole clause: the couple's agreement is itself a pain to enemies and a joy to friends (note on 6.184, p. 268). The last phrase, μάλιστα δέ τʼ ἔκλυον αὐτοί, is difficult, literally “and they themselves hear it most”. Merry and Riddell accept, with hesitation, the scholion's sense, that the couple themselves feel the benefit of their bond most of all. They compare *Iliad* 13.734, where the wise man saves many but knows it best himself. They also report Nauck's view that the words are corrupt (note on 6.185, pp. 268–269).

**In Book 6.** The blessing frames the rest of the meeting. Nausicaa's own wish for “such a man” as husband (6.244–245; see [[posis]]) and her fear of gossip about a husband from elsewhere (6.276–284) are about the first two goods of the prayer, the husband and the house. Odysseus has just refused Calypso's offer of immortality in order to go home to his wife (5.203–224). The blessing he gives the girl describes the marriage he is trying to return to (see [[husbands-and-wives]]).
