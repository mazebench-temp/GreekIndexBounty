---
summary: μεγαλήτωρ, the synonym of μεγάθυμος, occurs five times in Book 6, once of Odysseus in the verse that sends Athena to Scheria and four times of Alcinous, always in the genitive and always as the father of Nausicaa or the master of the house.
---

Book 6 has no form of μεγάθυμος. The great heart belongs to μεγαλήτωρ = μέγας (“great”) + ἦτορ (“heart”). LSJ notes that in Homer μεγαλήτωρ goes with proper names, except in the phrase μεγαλήτορα θυμόν, “great-hearted spirit”.

**Odysseus.** Athena goes to the house of Alcinous “planning a homecoming for great-hearted Odysseus” (νόστον Ὀδυσσῆι μεγαλήτορι μητιόωσα, 6.14). The verse returns unchanged at 8.9, when Athena gathers the Phaeacians to the assembly. A close variant stands at 5.233, where Calypso “planned the sending” of Odysseus (Ὀδυσσῆι μεγαλήτορι μήδετο πομπήν). The epithet goes with the hero as the object of a divine plan for his return. See [[odysseus]] and [[nostos]].

**Alcinous.** The other four uses are the genitive μεγαλήτορος Ἀλκινόοιο at the end of the verse. Three times the phrase defines Nausicaa: the narrator introduces her as “Nausicaa, the daughter of great-hearted Alcinous” (6.17), she names herself to the stranger with “I am the daughter of great-hearted Alcinous” (6.196), and the narrator repeats the whole-verse formula when the handmaids obey her (6.213). The fourth use defines the house: she tells Odysseus to ask in the city for “the house of my father, great-hearted Alcinous” (6.299). In Book 7 the same genitive goes with the house twice (7.85, 7.93), and Odysseus uses the vocative form of Nausicaa's formula when he says goodbye to her (8.464). See [[alcinous]], [[nausicaa]] and [[house-of-alcinous]].

In Book 6 Alcinous does not have the epithet when he is present. At 6.54–70 he speaks with his daughter as “father”, and at 6.12 he is introduced as the ruler “who knew counsels from the gods”. The epithet comes with his name only in the genitive, through his daughter and his house. Nausicaa adds to her own use the statement that “on whom the power and the strength of the Phaeacians depend” (6.197).
