**Grace that shows.** In Book 6 χάρις is mostly the visible kind, charm and beauty that others see. The handmaids who sleep by Nausicaa's door “had beauty from the Graces” (Χαρίτων ἄπο κάλλος ἔχουσαι, 6.18; see [[graces]] and [[handmaids]]). The book's central use comes after the bath. Odysseus has washed the brine from his skin and put on the clothes Nausicaa gave him, and Athena changes his appearance (6.229–237):

{{quote:45dbc0d6-7cdb-5501-a61f-4c115beb80bf}}

The simile comes from the goldsmith's craft. A craftsman taught by Hephaestus and Athena pours gold around silver and “completes graceful works” (χαρίεντα … ἔργα); so Athena “poured grace upon him, on his head and shoulders” (κατέχευε χάριν, 6.232–235; see [[gold-poured-on-silver]]). Odysseus then sits apart on the shore, “gleaming with beauty and graces” (κάλλεϊ καὶ χάρισι στίλβων), and the girl gazes at him (6.237). The plural χάριτες here are the charms themselves, not the goddesses.

**The scholion on 6.235.** The scholia explain the simile as a comparison for the change: as silver, already fine, is finer with gold poured round it, so Odysseus was handsome before but did not seem so after the damage of the sea, and now he has his own χάρις back. The scholion ends: “nature is the cause of being handsome, χάρις of seeming so” (τοῦ μὲν γὰρ εἶναι καλὸν ἡ φύσις αἰτία, τοῦ δὲ δοκεῖν ἡ χάρις; schol. H.Q. on 6.235, Dindorf p. 313). It also notes the fitness of the comparison: Athena does the work, so the poet takes his image from her own craftsmen.

**The same gift elsewhere.** Athena pours grace on Telemachus when he goes to the assembly (θεσπεσίην … χάριν κατέχευεν, 2.12) and again in Book 17 (17.63), and on Odysseus before he meets the Phaeacians in assembly (8.18–23). The lines 6.230–235 come back almost word for word when Odysseus is bathed in his own house before Penelope recognizes him (23.157–162). In each case the man is about to be seen by others: an assembly, the Phaeacians, his wife. Here it is Nausicaa who sees. Before, she says, he seemed to her “unsightly, but now he is like the gods” (6.242–243), and she wishes for such a husband (6.244; see [[posis]]).

**Grace as favor.** The other sense of the word, a pleasure given and the goodwill that answers it, is present too. Dymas' daughter, whose shape Athena takes, “was dear to her heart” (κεχάριστο δὲ θυμῷ, 6.23); the scholion glosses “she was kindly, she was loved” (schol. H on 6.23, Dindorf p. 295). Odysseus, who has nothing to give, answers Nausicaa's help in advance with a prayer for a husband, a house and like-mindedness (6.180–185; see [[homophrosyne]]). A scholion explains why: “those who cannot repay a favor (χάριν) at once take refuge in this return” of prayer (schol. E.P. on 6.180, Dindorf p. 309). Another scholion, on his plea “have pity” (6.175), says that a helper who sees that the χάρις of help will be owed to himself alone is readier to pity (schol. H.P.Q. on 6.175, Dindorf p. 309).
