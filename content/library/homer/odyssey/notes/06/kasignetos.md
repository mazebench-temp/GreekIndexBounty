**Nausicaa's brothers.** In his speech of supplication Odysseus praises the family of the girl before him. If she is a mortal, “thrice blessed are your father and lady mother, / and thrice blessed your brothers” (κασίγνητοι), whose hearts are warmed as they watch her go into the dance (6.154–157; see [[thrice-blessed]]). He does not know that she has brothers. Nausicaa has already named them to her father: five sons live in the house, two married and three unmarried, and they always want fresh-washed clothes for the dance (6.62–65; see [[sons-of-alcinous]]). In the next book the brothers meet her at the door, “like the immortals”, unyoke the mules and carry in the clothes (7.4–6).

**Her father's brother.** The book ends with Athena's restraint:

{{quote:d7f75c1e-b945-5120-94bf-1b259d2a6757}}

πατροκασίγνητος is “father's brother, uncle”: Poseidon is the brother of Zeus, Athena's father (see [[poseidon]] and [[zeus]]). The word occurs three times in Homer. Athena uses it herself when she explains on Ithaca why she did not help Odysseus at sea: “I did not wish to fight with Poseidon, my father's brother” (13.341–342). In the Iliad, Apollo will not fight Poseidon in the battle of the gods: “for he stood in awe of mixing in combat with his father's brother” (αἴδετο γάρ ῥα / πατροκασιγνήτοιο, *Iliad* 21.468–469). The half-verse αἴδετο γάρ ῥα and the word at the start of the next verse are the same in both passages. In each a younger god defers to an uncle (see [[aidos]]).
