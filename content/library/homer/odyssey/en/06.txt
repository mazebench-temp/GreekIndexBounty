# Homer, Odyssey, Book 6: English translation by Claude (Anthropic Opus 5.5), 2026.
# Translated line-for-line from the Murray Greek (Perseus); each row carries the Greek verse number.
# English syntax occasionally runs across a line break. Conventions: see ../TRANSLATION.md.
1	So he lay sleeping there, much-enduring brilliant Odysseus,
2	worn down by sleep and weariness; but Athena
3	went to the land and the city of the Phaeacian men,
4	who once in former days lived in Hypereia with its wide dancing floors,
5	near the Cyclopes, overweening men,
6	who kept doing them harm, and were greater in strength.
7	From there godlike Nausithous made them rise up and led them away,
8	and settled them in Scheria, far from grain-eating men,
9	and drove a wall around the city, and built houses,
10	and made temples of the gods, and divided the plowlands.
11	But he had already been overcome by fate and had gone to Hades,
12	and Alcinous was ruling then, who knew counsels from the gods.
13	To his house went the gray-eyed goddess Athena,
14	planning a homecoming for great-hearted Odysseus.
15	She went her way into the intricately worked chamber, in which a girl
16	was sleeping, like the immortal goddesses in build and in looks,
17	Nausicaa, the daughter of great-hearted Alcinous,
18	and beside her two handmaids, who had beauty from the Graces,
19	one by each of the doorposts; and the shining doors were closed.
20	And she, like a breath of wind, rushed to the girl's bed,
21	and stood above her head and spoke to her,
22	in the likeness of the daughter of Dymas, famous for ships,
23	who was of the same age as she, and was dear to her heart.
24	In her likeness gray-eyed Athena spoke to her:
25	“Nausicaa, why did your mother bear you so slack?
26	Your glossy clothes lie uncared for,
27	and your marriage is near, when you must wear fine clothes yourself
28	and provide others to those who will lead you home.
29	For it is from these things that a report goes up among men,
30	a good one, and your father and lady mother rejoice.
31	But let us go to wash them when dawn appears;
32	and I will go along with you as a fellow worker, so that very quickly
33	you may get them ready, since you will not be a maiden much longer;
34	for already the best men throughout the land are courting you,
35	of all the Phaeacians, where you yourself have your lineage too.
36	But come, urge your famous father in the early dawn
37	to make ready the mules and the wagon, to carry
38	the sashes and the robes and the glossy coverlets.
39	And for you yourself it is much better so than going on foot;
40	for the washing pools are a long way from the city.”
41	So saying, gray-eyed Athena went away
42	to Olympus, where, they say, the seat of the gods is, firm forever.
43	It is not shaken by winds, nor ever by rain
44	is it wet, nor does snow come near it; but a clear sky
45	is spread out, quite cloudless, and a white gleam runs over it;
46	in it the blessed gods take their delight all their days.
47	There the gray-eyed one went, when she had made all clear to the girl.
48	At once came Dawn of the fair throne, who woke her,
49	well-robed Nausicaa; and immediately she wondered at the dream,
50	and went through the house to tell it to her parents,
51	her dear father and her mother; and she found them indoors.
52	Her mother sat at the hearth with her handmaid women,
53	turning the sea-purple wool on her distaff; and her father, as he was going out of doors,
54	she met as he went to join the famous kings
55	in council, to which the noble Phaeacians were calling him.
56	And she stood very close and spoke to her dear father:
57	“Papa dear, would you not make ready for me a carriage,
58	high, with good wheels, so that I may take the glorious clothes
59	to the river to wash them, which lie here soiled?
60	And for you yourself it is fitting, when you are among the foremost,
61	to take counsel in the council with clean clothes on your skin.
62	And you have five dear sons born in the halls,
63	two married, and three unmarried young men in their bloom;
64	and they always want to wear freshly washed clothes
65	when they go to the dance; and all these things are a care to my mind.”
66	So she spoke; for she felt shame to name the marriage of her prime
67	to her dear father. But he understood everything, and answered her:
68	“I do not grudge you the mules, child, nor anything else.
69	Go; and the slaves will make ready for you a carriage,
70	high, with good wheels, fitted with an upper frame.”
71	So saying, he called to the slaves, and they obeyed.
72	Outside, they made ready the well-wheeled mule wagon,
73	and brought up the mules and yoked them to the carriage;
74	and the girl brought the shining clothing out of her chamber,
75	and laid it on the polished carriage,
76	and her mother put in a chest satisfying food
77	of every kind, and put in relishes, and poured in wine
78	in a goatskin; and the girl mounted the carriage.
79	And she gave her liquid olive oil in a golden flask,
80	so that she, with her handmaid women, might anoint herself after bathing.
81	And she took the whip and the glossy reins,
82	and whipped them to a run; and there was a clatter of the two mules.
83	And they stretched out without end, and carried the clothing and her as well,
84	not alone; other handmaids also went along with her.
85	And when they came to the very beautiful stream of the river,
86	where indeed there were never-failing washing pools, and much fair water
87	flowed up from beneath and out, to clean even things that were very dirty,
88	there they loosed the mules out from under the carriage,
89	and drove them along the eddying river
90	to graze on the honey-sweet field grass; and from the carriage they
91	took the clothes in their hands, and carried them into the dark water,
92	and trod them in the trenches, quickly, bringing on a rivalry.
93	But when they had washed them and cleaned away all the dirt,
94	they spread them out in a row along the shore of the sea, where most of all
95	the sea washed the pebbles clean against the dry land.
96	And they bathed and anointed themselves richly with olive oil,
97	and then took their meal beside the banks of the river,
98	and waited for the clothes to dry in the rays of the sun.
99	But when they had had their fill of food, the slave women and she herself,
100	they played at ball, having thrown off their veils;
101	and among them white-armed Nausicaa began the song and dance.
102	And as Artemis of the showering arrows goes through the mountains,
103	either along towering Taygetus or Erymanthus,
104	delighting in wild boars and swift deer;
105	and with her the nymphs, daughters of aegis-bearing Zeus,
106	who range the wild country, play, and Leto is glad in her heart;
107	and above them all she holds her head and brow,
108	and she is easily known, though all are beautiful;
109	so she stood out among her handmaids, the unwed maiden.
110	But when she was about to go home again,
111	after yoking the mules and folding the fine clothes,
112	then in turn the gray-eyed goddess Athena thought of something else:
113	that Odysseus should wake and see the fair-faced girl,
114	who would lead him to the city of the Phaeacian men.
115	Then the princess threw the ball toward a handmaid;
116	she missed the handmaid, and threw it into a deep eddy;
117	and they shouted aloud; and brilliant Odysseus woke,
118	and sitting up he pondered in his mind and in his heart:
119	“Ah me, to the land of what mortals have I come now?
120	Are they arrogant and savage and not just,
121	or are they kind to strangers, and is their mind god-fearing?
122	A female cry has come around me, as of girls,
123	of nymphs, who hold the steep peaks of the mountains
124	and the springs of rivers and the grassy meadows.
125	Or am I perhaps somewhere near human beings who have speech?
126	But come, I will try for myself and see.”
127	So saying, brilliant Odysseus came out from under the thickets,
128	and from the dense wood he broke off with his strong hand a branch
129	of leaves, to shield the man's genitals about his body.
130	And he went on like a mountain-bred lion, trusting in his courage,
131	which goes on, rained upon and blown by the wind, and his eyes
132	blaze within him; and he goes among the cattle or the sheep
133	or after the wild deer; and his belly bids him
134	make an attempt on the sheep, and go even into the close-built fold;
135	so Odysseus was about to go among the girls with lovely tresses
136	and mingle with them, naked though he was; for need had come upon him.
137	Terrible he appeared to them, marred by the brine,
138	and they fled in fear, this way and that, over the jutting beaches;
139	only the daughter of Alcinous stayed; for Athena
140	put daring in her mind and took the fear from her limbs.
141	She stood facing him and held her ground; and Odysseus was torn two ways in thought,
142	whether to clasp the knees of the fair-faced girl and entreat her,
143	or simply to stand apart and with soothing words
144	entreat her, in case she might show him the city and give him clothes.
145	So, as he pondered, this seemed to him the better way:
146	to stand apart and entreat her with soothing words,
147	lest the girl be angry in her mind if he clasped her knees.
148	At once he spoke a soothing and shrewd word:
149	“I beseech you, queen: are you some goddess, or a mortal?
150	If you are a goddess, one of those who hold the wide sky,
151	it is to Artemis, the daughter of great Zeus, that I for my part
152	liken you most closely, in looks and stature and build;
153	but if you are one of the mortals who dwell on the earth,
154	thrice blessed are your father and lady mother,
155	and thrice blessed your brothers; surely their hearts
156	are always warmed with gladness because of you,
157	as they watch such a young shoot going into the dance.
158	But that man in turn is most blessed in his heart, far above all others,
159	who, prevailing by the weight of his bride-gifts, leads you home.
160	For never yet have I seen with my eyes such a mortal,
161	neither man nor woman; awe holds me as I look on you.
162	Once on Delos, beside the altar of Apollo, I saw such a thing,
163	a young sapling of a palm tree springing up;
164	for I went there too, and a great army followed me,
165	on that journey on which evil sorrows were to come upon me.
166	And in just the same way, when I saw that, I stood amazed in my heart
167	for a long time, since never yet had such a stem come up out of the earth,
168	as I admire you, lady, and stand amazed, and I am terribly afraid
169	to touch your knees; and a harsh grief has come upon me.
170	Yesterday, on the twentieth day, I escaped the wine-dark sea;
171	and all that time the wave and the rushing storms were always carrying me
172	from the island of Ogygia. And now a god has cast me down here,
173	so that here too, I suppose, I may still suffer some evil; for I do not think
174	it will cease, but the gods will still bring many things to pass before that.
175	But, queen, have pity; for it is to you, after I have toiled through many evils,
176	that I have come first, and I know no one of the other
177	people who hold this city and land.
178	Show me the town, and give me a rag to throw around me,
179	if perhaps you had some wrapper for the cloths when you came here.
180	And may the gods give you as much as you long for in your mind,
181	a husband and a home, and may they grant like-mindedness
182	that is good; for there is nothing stronger and better than this,
183	than when two who are like-minded in their thoughts keep a house,
184	a man and a woman: many sorrows to their enemies,
185	and joys to their well-wishers, and they themselves know it best.”
186	Then white-armed Nausicaa answered him face to face:
187	“Stranger, since you seem like neither a base man nor a senseless one;
188	but Zeus the Olympian himself deals out prosperity to men,
189	to the good and the bad, to each one as he wishes;
190	and to you, I suppose, he has given these things, and you must endure them all the same.
191	But now, since you have come to our city and land,
192	you will not lack clothing, then, or anything else
193	of what befits a much-tried suppliant who has met with people.
194	I will show you the town, and I will tell you the name of the people.
195	The Phaeacians hold this city and land,
196	and I am the daughter of great-hearted Alcinous,
197	on whom the power and the strength of the Phaeacians depend.”
198	She spoke, and ordered her handmaids with lovely tresses:
199	“Stand still for me, handmaids. Where are you fleeing at the sight of a man?
200	Can it be that you think he is one of the hostile men?
201	There is no such man, no living mortal, nor will there be one,
202	who would come to the land of the Phaeacian men
203	bringing combat; for we are very dear to the immortals.
204	And we live far apart in the much-surging sea,
205	the farthest away, and no other mortal mingles with us.
206	But this is some unhappy man, a wanderer who has come here,
207	and we must care for him now; for from Zeus are all
208	strangers and beggars, and a small gift is a dear one.
209	But, handmaids, give the stranger food and drink,
210	and bathe him in the river, where there is shelter from the wind.”
211	So she spoke, and they stood still and urged one another on,
212	and they sat Odysseus down in the shelter, as she had ordered,
213	Nausicaa, the daughter of great-hearted Alcinous;
214	and beside him they laid clothes, a cloak and a tunic,
215	and gave him liquid olive oil in a golden flask,
216	and told him to bathe in the currents of the river.
217	Then brilliant Odysseus spoke among the handmaids:
218	“Handmaids, stand like this, some way off, so that I myself
219	may wash the brine from my shoulders, and with olive oil
220	anoint myself all over; for truly ointment has long been away from my skin.
221	But I for my part will not bathe in front of you; for I feel shame
222	to strip naked when I have come among girls with lovely tresses.”
223	So he spoke, and they went away apart, and told the girl.
224	But brilliant Odysseus, with water from the river, washed from his skin
225	the brine that covered his back and his broad shoulders,
226	and from his head he scrubbed the scurf of the barren sea.
227	But when he had bathed all over and anointed himself richly,
228	and had put on the clothes that the unwed maiden had given him,
229	Athena, child of Zeus, made him
230	taller to look upon and sturdier, and down from his head
231	she let fall curling locks, like the hyacinth flower.
232	And as when a man pours gold around silver,
233	a skilled man, whom Hephaestus and Pallas Athena have taught
234	craft of every kind, and he completes graceful works,
235	so she poured grace upon him, on his head and shoulders.
236	Then he went apart and sat down on the shore of the sea,
237	gleaming with beauty and graces; and the girl gazed at him.
238	Then she spoke among her handmaids with lovely tresses:
239	“Hear me, white-armed handmaids, so that I may tell you something.
240	Not against the will of all the gods who hold Olympus
241	does this man mingle with the godlike Phaeacians;
242	for before, he truly seemed to me to be unsightly,
243	but now he is like the gods who hold the wide sky.
244	If only such a man might be called my husband,
245	living here, and if only it pleased him to stay here.
246	But, handmaids, give the stranger food and drink.”
247	So she spoke, and they listened closely to her and obeyed,
248	and set food and drink beside Odysseus.
249	He, much-enduring brilliant Odysseus, drank and ate
250	greedily; for he had long been without a taste of food.
251	But white-armed Nausicaa thought of other things:
252	she folded the clothes and put them on the fine carriage,
253	and yoked the strong-hoofed mules, and she herself climbed up,
254	and she urged Odysseus on, and spoke to him, and called him by name:
255	“Rise now, stranger, to go to the city, so that I may send you
256	to the house of my wise-hearted father, where, I tell you,
257	you will come to know all the Phaeacians who are the best.
258	But act just like this; and you seem to me not to lack sense:
259	as long as we are going through the fields and the tilled lands of men,
260	for that long go with the handmaids behind the mules and the wagon
261	quickly; and I will lead the way.
262	But when we set foot in the city, around which there is a rampart,
263	high, and a fine harbor on either side of the city,
264	and a narrow entrance; and along the road the curving ships
265	are drawn up; for all have a ship-shed, each man his own.
266	And there they have their place of assembly, around the fine shrine of Poseidon,
267	fitted with hauled stones bedded deep in the earth.
268	There they tend the tackle of the black ships,
269	cables and sails, and they taper the oars.
270	For the Phaeacians do not care for the bow or the quiver,
271	but for masts and the oars of ships and balanced ships,
272	glorying in which they cross the gray sea.
273	It is their unkind talk that I shun, so that no one afterward
274	may blame me; and there are very overbearing men among the people;
275	and now some baser man, meeting us, might speak like this:
276	‘And who is this following Nausicaa, a handsome and tall
277	stranger? And where did she find him? Surely he will be a husband for her.
278	Perhaps she has taken in someone who was driven off course, from his own ship,
279	one of the men from a far land, since there are none nearby;
280	or some much-prayed-for god has come to her at her prayer,
281	down from the sky, and she will have him all her days.
282	It is better so, if she herself has gone about and found a husband
283	from elsewhere; for truly she dishonors these men here among the people,
284	the Phaeacians, who are courting her, many and good.’
285	So they will say, and these things would become reproaches against me.
286	And I too am indignant with another woman who does such things,
287	who, against the will of her dear father and mother, while they are living,
288	mingles with men before she has come to a public marriage.
289	As for you, stranger, listen quickly to my word, so that very quickly
290	you may gain an escort and a homecoming from my father.
291	You will find a splendid grove of Athena near the road,
292	of poplars; and in it a spring flows, and around it is a meadow;
293	and there is my father's domain and his flourishing orchard,
294	as far from the city as a man can make himself heard when he shouts.
295	Sit down there and wait for a time, until we
296	come to the town and reach the house of my father.
297	But when you expect that we have reached the house,
298	then go into the city of the Phaeacians and ask for
299	the house of my father, great-hearted Alcinous.
300	It is easily known, and even a child could lead you,
301	an infant; for in no way are they built like it,
302	the houses of the Phaeacians, such as is the house of Alcinous
303	the hero. But when the house and the courtyard enclose you,
304	go very quickly through the hall, until you come to
305	my mother; she sits at the hearth in the light of the fire,
306	turning the sea-purple wool on her distaff, a wonder to see,
307	leaning against a pillar; and her slave women sit behind her.
308	And there my father's throne leans against it,
309	on which he sits and drinks his wine like an immortal.
310	Pass him by, and throw your hands around the knees
311	of our mother, so that you may see the day of homecoming
312	rejoicing, and quickly, even if you are from very far away.
313	If she is minded in friendship toward you in her heart,
314	then there is hope for you to see your dear ones and to reach
315	your well-built house and your native land.”
316	So she spoke, and lashed with the shining whip
317	the mules; and they quickly left the streams of the river.
318	And they ran well, and stepped out well with their feet;
319	and she drove with care, so that they could follow on foot,
320	the handmaids and Odysseus, and she laid on the lash with judgment.
321	And the sun set, and they came to the famous grove,
322	holy to Athena, where brilliant Odysseus sat down.
323	At once he prayed to the daughter of great Zeus:
324	“Hear me, child of aegis-bearing Zeus, Atrytone:
325	now at least listen to me, since before you never listened
326	when I was being shattered, when the famous shaker of the earth shattered me.
327	Grant that I may come to the Phaeacians as one dear to them and pitied.”
328	So he spoke in prayer, and Pallas Athena heard him.
329	But she did not yet appear to him face to face; for she stood in awe of
330	her father's brother; and he raged furiously
331	against godlike Odysseus until he reached his own land.
